What a prompt template is

A prompt template is a ready-made instruction with blanks in it. Instead of staring at an empty box and wondering how to ask, you copy a template, fill in your own words where the square brackets are, and send it. The structure is already there — the role, the job, the shape of the answer — and your words are what make it yours.

Templates beat typing from scratch for a simple reason: a blank page is hard, and a half-finished sentence is easy. Write a polite email to [name] about [topic] tells you exactly what to decide, and once you have filled in the name and the topic, the hard part is done. People use ChatGPT for writing in 12.7% of conversations, and most of that writing follows the same few patterns — emails, plans, notes, stories — which is why templates work so well.

A template is not a finished answer. It is a starting point that gets you a useful first draft in seconds, and the picker above gives you one for each everyday job.

The fill-in-the-blank pattern

Most good templates follow the same pattern, and once you see it you can spot it everywhere. First comes the role: act as a friendly teacher, a travel planner, a study coach. Then the job: write an email, plan my meals, start a story. Then the details — the blanks — where your words go. And finally the shape: keep it under five sentences, use bullet points, end with a question.

The blanks are the important part. A template with no blanks is a finished instruction, and it will give you a generic answer. A template with the right blanks — the name, the place, the number of days — gives you an answer that fits your life. When you fill a template in, be specific: a real name, a real place, a real number. Specific in, specific out.

You can build your own templates with the same pattern. Take a request that worked well, replace your details with square brackets, and save it. Next time the same job comes up, the template is waiting.

Templates for writing

Writing is where templates shine, because most writing follows a shape. An email has a greeting, a point, and a request. A cover note has an opening, a reason, and a close. The template holds the shape while you supply the facts.

The email template is the one people reach for most: write a polite email to [name] about [topic], keep it under [number] short sentences, start with [greeting] and end by asking for [what you need]. Fill in the blanks and you get a draft that needs a quick read, not a rewrite. The job application template works the same way: a short cover note for a [job title] job, with your real experience in the blank. Keep it honest — a template can shape your words, but it cannot invent experience you do not have.

For any writing template, the last step is always yours. Read what comes back, fix the tone, cut the extra words, and make sure it sounds like you before you send it.

Templates for planning

Planning templates turn a vague intention into a schedule. A study plan template asks for the subject, the number of days, the hours per day, and the topics — and hands back a day-by-day plan with a rest day built in. A meal plan template asks for the number of days, the number of people, what you like and what you avoid — and hands back dinners you can actually cook.

The travel template is the same idea on a bigger scale: a [number]-day trip to [place] for [number] people, with your interests in the blank and a relaxed pace. The template does the organising; you do the choosing. If the plan it gives you is too busy, say so — slower, one rest day, no early starts — and it will reshape itself around you.

Planning templates work because a plan is mostly structure. Give the template your real limits — your real hours, your real budget, your real likes — and the plan it gives back will be one you can actually follow.

Templates for ideas and stories

Templates are not only for practical jobs. A story starter template — start a story about [character] who finds [object] in [place] — is a game as much as a tool: change one blank and the whole story changes. A brave baker who finds a silver key in a lighthouse is a different story from a shy robot who finds a broken compass in a market, and the template lets you try both in a minute.

The same trick works for ideas. A brainstorm template — give me ten ideas for [thing], each one different, and make the last one the strangest — turns a stuck afternoon into a list worth reading. The number keeps it generous, the last line keeps it playful, and the blank is where your project goes.

For creative templates, the best move is to run the same template twice with different blanks and pick the better result. The template stays the same; your imagination does the rest.

How to adapt a template

A template is a starting point, and the best ones are the ones you change. Three small changes make any template yours: change the role, change the length, change the tone.

Changing the role changes the voice. A study plan from a strict coach reads differently from one by a friendly tutor, and the same template can give you either. Changing the length changes the depth: under five sentences for a quick note, as much detail as you like for a plan you will follow for weeks. Changing the tone changes how it lands: formal for a work email, warm for a message to a friend.

Keep the structure when you adapt. The blanks stay where they are; only the dressing changes. And when a version works well, save it as your own template — the one that fits you is worth more than any template in a list.

Why specific templates beat vague ones

Write me an email is a template, but it is a vague one, and vague templates give vague answers. Write a polite email to my teacher about missing Friday's class, under five sentences, ending by asking for the homework is a specific template, and it gives a specific answer. The difference is the blanks, filled in.

Specificity works because ChatGPT has nothing to guess. A vague template leaves every choice open — the tone, the length, the details — and every open choice is a guess. A specific template closes the choices that matter and leaves open only the ones you do not mind. Fill in the name, the place, the number, the deadline, and the answer comes back shaped around your life instead of around nobody's.

If a template gives you a flat, generic answer, the fix is almost always the same: add one more detail. One real fact in the blank is worth more than any clever wording around it.

Questions people ask

What is a prompt template?

A prompt template is a ready-made instruction with blanks for your own words. You copy it, fill in the blanks — a name, a place, a number — and send it, instead of working out how to ask from scratch.

How do I use a ChatGPT template?

Copy the template, replace each square bracket with your own words, and paste the whole thing into ChatGPT. Read what comes back, fix anything that is off, and ask for changes in plain words if you want it shorter, simpler or friendlier.

Can I save my own templates?

Yes. Keep them in a note on your phone or in a document, with the blanks still in square brackets so you know what to fill in next time. The templates that work best are the ones you have already used and adjusted once.

What is the best template for writing emails?

Write a polite email to [name] about [topic]. Keep it under [number] short sentences. Start with [greeting] and end by asking for [what you need]. Fill in the blanks with real details and the draft comes back ready for a quick read, not a rewrite.

Why do templates work better than free typing?

Because a blank box is hard and a half-finished sentence is easy. A template holds the structure — the role, the job, the shape of the answer — so you only have to decide the details. It also keeps your requests consistent, which gives you consistent answers.

Are prompt templates free to use?

Yes. A template is just words, so using one costs nothing. ChatGPT itself has a free plan you can use them on, though the free plan may have limits on how much you can use in a day.
