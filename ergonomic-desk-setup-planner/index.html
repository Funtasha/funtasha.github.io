Ergonomic Desk Setup Planner

Plan a comfortable desk setup with your chair, screen and keyboard at the right heights for your body, using the measurements and checklist on this page. This is general guidance only, not medical advice, so adjust everything to what feels right for you.

General guidance only — not medical advice. If you have pain, numbness, tingling or an existing condition, see a professional before changing your setup. The measurements here are typical ranges; your own body is the final judge.

Why your setup matters more than your chair's price

Most desk aches do not come from one bad piece of furniture. They come from small mismatches that add up over hours: a screen a little too low, so the neck bends forward all day; a chair a little too high, so the feet dangle and the thighs press against the seat; a keyboard a little too far away, so the shoulders hunch forward. Each mismatch is small on its own, but eight hours a day, five days a week, turns small into sore.

The good news is that most of these mismatches are cheap to fix. A stack of books under a monitor, a footrest made from a sturdy box, a separate keyboard for a laptop — these cost little or nothing and often do more than an expensive chair bought to solve a problem that was really the desk height. The aim of this page is to help you measure your own setup, find the mismatches, and fix them in order of what matters most.

Work through the sections in order: chair first, then screen, then keyboard and mouse. Each section gives you the measurements to check and the range to aim for. The ranges are typical published ergonomic guidance, not exact prescriptions. Bodies differ, so use the numbers as starting points and trust comfort over centimetres. If a position in the recommended range still hurts, that is information, not failure — note it and, if it persists, talk to a professional.

Start with the chair: feet, knees and back

The chair sets everything else, so start there. Sit right back in the seat with your back against the backrest. Your feet should rest flat on the floor, and your knees should sit at about a right angle, with your thighs roughly level or sloping very slightly down toward the knees. If your feet dangle, the chair is too high: lower it, or add a footrest. If your knees sit well above your hips, the chair is too low: raise it until your thighs level out.

Next, check the lower back. The backrest should support the natural curve of your lower back. If your chair has lumbar support, adjust it so it fills that curve without pushing you forward. If it does not, a small cushion or a rolled towel placed at the small of your back does the same job. Sit back against it rather than perching on the front edge of the seat — perching feels alert for a few minutes and tired for the rest of the day.

Armrests are optional, and bad armrests are worse than none. If yours are adjustable, set them so your elbows rest at about a right angle with your shoulders relaxed and level. If the armrests push your shoulders up, or force your elbows out to the sides, lower them or remove them. Your shoulders should hang naturally, not shrug all day. Finally, sit close enough to the desk that you can reach the keyboard without leaning forward; leaning forward to type is one of the commonest causes of neck and shoulder ache.

Screen height and distance

Your screen should sit directly in front of you, not off to one side. Turning your head to one side for hours is a reliable way to earn a stiff neck. If you use two screens, put the one you look at most directly ahead and the second beside it, angled slightly toward you. The centre of the main screen should be about an arm's length away — roughly 50 to 70 cm for most people — and the top of the screen should be at or slightly below your eye level when you sit upright.

The top-of-screen rule matters because of how the eyes work. When you look at the centre of a screen whose top is at eye level, your gaze falls slightly downward, which is the eyes' natural resting direction. A screen that is too low forces the whole head down and loads the neck; a screen that is too high forces the chin up and strains the same muscles from the other side. If your screen sits on a fixed stand that is too low, raise it with a monitor stand or a stable stack of books until the top edge reaches eye level.

Distance matters too. Too close, and the eyes work hard to focus; too far, and you lean in to read, which undoes the good posture you just set up. Arm's length is the usual starting point: sit back in your chair, stretch one arm out, and your fingertips should just about touch the screen. If you wear glasses, your prescription may change the ideal distance — people with reading glasses often prefer the screen a little closer and lower. Adjust until text is easy to read without leaning in, and increase the text size in your settings before you move the screen closer.

Keyboard and mouse: keep your wrists straight

With the chair and screen set, turn to the keyboard. Your elbows should sit at about a right angle, close to your sides, with your forearms roughly level and your wrists straight — not bent up, down or to either side. The keyboard should sit at the height where that happens naturally. For most people that is around elbow height when seated, which is why the desk-height table below is built around elbow height.

If the desk is too high, your shoulders rise and your wrists bend back to reach the keys. A keyboard tray that sits below the desk surface can fix this, or you can raise your chair and add a footrest so that your elbows reach the desk at the right angle. If the desk is too low, your knees bump the underside and your wrists bend down; raising the desk on blocks or choosing a taller desk is the fix. A slight downward tilt of the keyboard, away from you, can help keep the wrists straight, but a steep tilt in either direction loads the wrists.

The mouse belongs at the same height as the keyboard, right beside it, so your hand moves between the two without reaching. Reaching for a mouse placed far to the side is a classic source of shoulder pain. Keep the mouse close, keep your wrist straight while using it, and rest the whole forearm on the desk or armrest rather than pivoting from the wrist alone. If you use the mouse for hours, a larger mouse that fills your palm is usually kinder than a tiny travel mouse.

Desk height by body height

The table below gives typical starting points for desk and chair heights by body height. They are drawn from standard published ergonomic guidance and are meant as a starting point, not a prescription. The real test is always the same: sit down, put your feet flat, let your elbows hang at a right angle, and see where your hands fall. That height is your desk height, whatever the table says.

Most fixed desks are built between 72 and 75 cm high, which suits the middle of the range well and leaves shorter and taller people to adapt. If you are shorter than the desk, raise the chair and add a footrest. If you are taller, raise the desk on blocks or choose a height-adjustable desk. A height-adjustable desk is the most flexible option if you share the desk with someone of a different height, or if you want to alternate sitting and standing.

Laptops: the compromise and how to fix it

A laptop is an ergonomic compromise by design: the screen and the keyboard are attached, so you cannot put both at the right height at once. With the laptop on a desk, the screen is too low; with the laptop raised so the screen is right, the keyboard is too high. For short sessions this hardly matters. For a full working day it matters a lot, and it is the commonest cause of laptop neck.

The fix is to separate the two. Raise the laptop on a stand until the top of its screen reaches eye level, then use a separate keyboard and mouse at desk height. That one change — a stand plus a keyboard — turns a laptop into a setup as good as a desktop. If you only have a keyboard and no stand, put the laptop on a stack of books. If you only have a stand and no keyboard, the built-in keyboard is still usable for short stretches, but your shoulders will tell you when the stretch has gone on too long.

If you work on a laptop away from your desk — on a sofa, in bed, on a train — accept that the setup will be imperfect and limit the time. Prop the laptop so the screen is as high as you can manage, take breaks often, and save long typing sessions for the desk. The worst laptop posture is the one held for hours without a break, whatever the position.

Standing, moving and the 20-8-2 idea

No single position is healthy for eight hours, however well set up. The body is built to move, and the best desk setup in the world still asks a lot of a body that never changes position. If you can, alternate sitting and standing through the day. A sit-stand desk makes this easy, but even a tall shelf or a counter can serve as a standing station for part of the day.

One widely shared guideline suggests a pattern of sitting for 20 minutes, standing for 8 minutes, and moving for 2 minutes, in each half hour. Treat that as a rhythm to aim for rather than a rule to obey exactly — the point is regular change, not precise timing. Standing still for hours brings its own aches, so standing time should still include shifting your weight and moving around.

Short movement breaks help more than one long break at lunch. Stand up, stretch your arms overhead, roll your shoulders, walk to get water, look out of a window at something distant to rest your eyes. Two minutes of movement every half hour does more for comfort than any single piece of equipment. Set a quiet timer if you forget; the habit matters more than the timer.

A quick daily checklist

Use this checklist each morning, or whenever your setup changes. It takes a minute, and it catches the drift that creeps in when someone else uses your desk or you come back from a holiday.

CheckWhat good looks like
FeetFlat on the floor or on a footrest
KneesAbout a right angle, thighs roughly level
Lower backSupported by the backrest or a cushion
ShouldersRelaxed and level, not shrugged or hunched
ScreenTop at or just below eye level, about an arm away
KeyboardElbows at a right angle, wrists straight
MouseBeside the keyboard, no reaching
BreaksMove for a minute or two every half hour

If one item on the list is wrong, fix that one thing before you start work. A setup that is right in the morning tends to stay right, and a setup that starts wrong tends to get worse as the day goes on. Your body will tell you when something is off — listen to it early, while the fix is still small.

Questions people ask

How high should my screen be?

The top of the screen should sit at or slightly below your eye level when you sit upright, with the screen about an arm's length away — roughly 50 to 70 cm. If you wear glasses for reading, you may prefer the screen a little lower.

What is the right chair height?

Set the chair so your feet rest flat on the floor and your knees sit at about a right angle, with your thighs roughly level. If your feet dangle, use a footrest rather than lowering the chair until your knees rise above your hips.

My desk is not adjustable. What can I do?

Work around it: raise your chair to match the desk, then add a footrest; raise a low screen with a sturdy stand or a stack of books; and use a keyboard tray or a separate keyboard if the desk is too high for comfortable typing.

How far away should my keyboard and mouse be?

Close enough that your elbows stay near your sides at about a right angle and your wrists stay straight. Your mouse should sit at the same height as the keyboard, right beside it, so you never have to reach.

Is a standing desk better than sitting?

Neither is better on its own — the research points to changing position through the day. Alternate sitting and standing if you can, and take short movement breaks often. Standing still all day brings its own aches.

When should I get professional advice about my setup?

If you have ongoing pain, numbness or tingling in your neck, shoulders, arms or hands, or an existing condition, see a professional. This page gives general guidance only and cannot diagnose or treat anything.
