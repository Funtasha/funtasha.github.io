Podcast Sponsorship Marketplace Directory

Podcast sponsorship is when a business pays a show to carry an advert for its product, usually priced by how many people hear it. This guide explains how sponsorship marketplaces work, how rates are worked out, how to pitch, and what to check before you sign.

Rates on this page are ranges that explain how pricing works, not promises of what you will be paid. Real rates vary by audience, topic and advertiser, and this page lists no real sponsors, so check any marketplace or offer carefully before you sign.

How to use this guide

This guide explains podcast sponsorship in plain English: how it works, how the money is worked out, how marketplaces connect shows with advertisers, and what to check before you sign anything. It is written for podcasters who are thinking about their first sponsor, and for small shows that want to understand what their audience is worth. You do not need any business background to follow it.

Read the sections in order the first time through. The first sections explain the basic deal and the marketplace, the middle sections explain rates and pitching, and the last sections cover contracts and the mistakes that cost podcasters money. Each section stands on its own too, so you can come back to one part later when you need it. This page lists no real sponsors and names no real marketplace, because sponsors and marketplaces change, and a list printed here would go out of date. Instead it teaches you how to judge any of them.

How podcast sponsorship works

Podcast sponsorship is a simple deal. A business pays a podcast to carry an advert for its product or service, and the podcast plays or reads that advert inside an episode. The business reaches listeners who trust the show, and the show earns money from the audience it has built. The advert is usually read by the host in their own voice, which is why podcast advertising works differently from a banner on a website: the listener hears it from someone they already listen to every week.

Adverts come in three places. A pre-roll plays at the start of the episode, before the content begins. A mid-roll plays in the middle, often at a natural break, and it usually costs the most because listeners are settled in by then. A post-roll plays at the end, when some listeners have already switched off. A sponsor may buy one place or all three, for one episode or for a run of episodes. Some sponsors send a script for the host to read, and some send a finished recording. Either way, the listener should always be able to tell that it is an advert, and the host should say so in plain words.

  • Host-read adverts: the host reads the message in their own words, from talking points the sponsor provides.
  • Produced adverts: the sponsor supplies a finished recording, and the show plays it as it is.
  • Live reads: the host talks about the product naturally, as part of the show, and marks it clearly as sponsored.
  • Show notes and links: some deals include a mention in the written notes for the episode as well.

How sponsorship marketplaces work

A sponsorship marketplace is a website where podcasters and advertisers find each other. The podcaster creates a listing for the show, giving the topic, the audience size, how often episodes come out, and what kind of advert the show will carry. The advertiser searches those listings for shows whose listeners match the people they want to reach, and then contacts the shows that fit. The marketplace itself does not choose the sponsor for you; it is a meeting place, and the deal is still yours to judge.

A good listing is honest and complete. It says how many listens an episode usually gets, and over what time, because a number with no time attached means nothing. It describes the audience in plain words: who they are, what they care about, and why they listen. It says what the show will and will not advertise, because a show that takes every sponsor loses the trust that makes its adverts work. Most marketplaces take a fee or a share of the deal for running the service, and the size of that fee varies, so read the terms of any marketplace before you list your show there.

  • Listings show audience size, topic, episode frequency and advert types.
  • Advertisers search by audience and topic, then contact the shows that fit.
  • The marketplace handles the introduction; the podcaster judges the deal.
  • Fees and payment terms differ between marketplaces, so read them first.

How rates are worked out

Most podcast sponsorship is priced by the number of people who hear the advert, using a measure called cost per thousand listens. The idea is simple: the sponsor pays a set amount for every thousand listens an episode gets, usually counted over the first month after the episode comes out. If the rate is 20 per thousand listens and an episode gets 5,000 listens in its first month, the price of one advert in that episode is 100. Those numbers are an example to show the arithmetic, not a promise of what any show will earn.

Real rates vary widely, and the number of listens is only part of the story. A small audience that trusts the host and buys what is recommended can be worth more per listener than a large audience that skips the adverts. The topic matters too: an audience of people about to spend money on a specific thing is worth more to the businesses that sell that thing. Host-read mid-roll adverts usually cost more than produced pre-roll adverts, a run of episodes usually costs less per episode than a single episode, and an exclusive deal, where no rival advertiser appears on the show, costs more than a non-exclusive one.

  • Cost per thousand listens is the usual starting point for pricing.
  • Listens are usually counted over the first 30 days after release.
  • A loyal niche audience can be worth more per listener than a large casual one.
  • Mid-roll and host-read adverts usually cost more than pre-roll and produced ones.
  • Longer runs and exclusivity change the price, so ask how each deal is priced.

Pitching a sponsor

A pitch is a short message that tells a business why its advert belongs on your show. The best pitches are short, specific and honest. They say who listens, how many listen, and why those listeners are the right people for that business. They say what the show offers: the advert places, the number of episodes, and the price range. They do not promise sales, because a podcast cannot promise sales, and a business that has sponsored podcasts before will not believe a promise like that anyway.

Before you pitch, listen to your own show as an advertiser would. Write down the audience in one sentence, the average listens per episode over the first month, and three businesses your listeners already use or talk about. Those three are your first pitches, because a business your listeners already know is easier to convince than a stranger. Keep the pitch to one page, put the numbers near the top, and end with one clear next step, such as a short call. If there is no reply in a week, one polite follow-up is reasonable, and then move on.

  • Open with the audience: who listens, and how many.
  • Say why this business fits this show, in one or two sentences.
  • Offer something concrete: advert places, episode count, price range.
  • Never promise sales or a fixed number of customers.
  • End with one clear next step, and follow up once if there is no reply.

The contract points to check

Never take a sponsorship on a handshake. A short written agreement protects both sides, and it does not need to be long or full of legal language. It should say how many episodes carry the advert, which advert places are included, what the sponsor pays and when, and what happens if an episode is late or a recording fails. It should say who approves the advert copy, and how many changes the host can ask for, because a script the host cannot read naturally will sound wrong to the listeners.

Two points matter most. The first is exclusivity: if the deal stops you from taking a rival advertiser, that should cost more, and it should have a clear end date. The second is the exit: how either side can end the deal early, and what happens to the money already paid. A deal with no exit clause can trap a show with a sponsor it no longer trusts, or trap a sponsor with a show that has changed direction. Read every contract before you sign it, and if a clause is unclear, ask what it means in plain words before you agree to it.

  • Episode count, advert places, payment amount and payment dates.
  • Who approves the advert copy, and how changes are handled.
  • Exclusivity: what it covers, and when it ends.
  • What happens if an episode is late or a recording fails.
  • How either side can end the deal, and what happens to money already paid.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is pricing by downloads alone. A download is not a listen: a download happens when a file is fetched, and a listen happens when a person actually plays it. Some downloads are never played, some are played twice, and some are fetched by apps in the background. Price on the listens you can measure over the first month, say how you measure them, and never present a download count as a listen count. An advertiser who knows podcasts will ask how you count, and an honest answer builds trust.

The second mistake is signing without an exit clause, which leaves a show stuck with a sponsor that no longer fits. The third is taking any sponsor that offers money: an advert for something your listeners dislike, or something you would not use yourself, costs more in trust than it pays in money. The fourth is promising results you cannot control, such as a fixed number of sales. And the fifth is forgetting to mark the advert as an advert. Listeners accept sponsorship when it is honest, and they resent it when it is hidden, so say plainly, in every sponsored episode, that the segment is paid for.

  • Downloads are not listens: price on measured listens, and say how you count.
  • Never sign a deal with no way out for either side.
  • Turn down sponsors that do not fit your show or your listeners.
  • Promise only what you control: the advert, the episodes, the dates.
  • Mark every sponsored segment clearly, in every episode.

Questions people ask

How does podcast sponsorship work?

A business pays a podcast to carry an advert inside an episode, usually read by the host or played as a recording. The advert can sit at the start, in the middle or at the end of the episode. The sponsor reaches listeners who trust the show, and the show earns money from its audience.

How much do sponsors pay?

Rates vary widely, and most are worked out per thousand listens, usually counted over the first month after release. As an example of the arithmetic, a rate of 20 per thousand listens and 5,000 listens gives 100 for one advert. Real rates depend on the audience, the topic and the advert type, so treat any figure as a range, not a promise.

How do I find a sponsor?

Start with the businesses your listeners already use, and with sponsorship marketplaces where advertisers search for shows by audience and topic. List your show honestly, with real listen numbers and a plain description of the audience. A direct pitch to a business that fits your show often works better than a long list of cold messages.

What should my pitch say?

Say who listens, how many listen, and why those listeners fit that business. Offer something concrete: the advert places, the number of episodes and the price range. Keep it to one page, put the numbers near the top, end with one clear next step, and never promise sales.

Do I need a big audience?

No. A small, loyal audience in a clear niche can be worth more per listener than a large casual audience, because the listeners trust the host and act on what they hear. What matters is fit: the right listeners for the right business, and honest numbers about how many of them there are.

What should the contract say?

It should say how many episodes carry the advert, which places are included, what the sponsor pays and when, and who approves the copy. It should cover exclusivity, what happens if an episode is late, and how either side can end the deal early. Read every clause before you sign, and ask what anything unclear means in plain words.
