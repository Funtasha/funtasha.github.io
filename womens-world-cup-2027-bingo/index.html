How to play World Cup bingo

World Cup bingo is simple enough for anyone to join in, even if they have never watched a full football match before. Each person gets one card, and every square on the card names a small moment that might happen during a match: a corner kick, a save by the goalkeeper, a shot that hits the woodwork, or a player celebrating with a dance. You watch the match together, and whenever one of the moments on your card happens, you mark that square. The middle square is free, so it is marked from the start.

The first person to mark a full row, a full column, or a full diagonal line calls out bingo and wins the round. If two people finish at the same moment, share the win and start a fresh card for the next match. There is no need to keep score across the whole tournament unless your group wants to: a simple tally of wins on a sheet of paper is enough to crown a family bingo champion by the time the final is played on 25 July 2027.

Because every card is different, nobody has an advantage. The generator shuffles a pool of match-day moments and picks twenty-four of them for each card, so even people sitting side by side will be watching for different things. That is part of the fun: one person is waiting for a penalty while another is hoping for a backheel pass, and everyone has a reason to watch the quiet minutes as closely as the goals.

What is on the cards

Every card holds twenty-four match-day moments plus the free centre square. The moments are the everyday happenings of a football match: goals and saves, corners and free kicks, tackles and throw-ins, celebrations and crowd chants. Nothing on the card depends on which teams are playing, so the same cards work for every match of the tournament, from the opening game on 24 June 2027 to the final on 25 July 2027.

The moments are deliberately small and frequent. A card full of rare events would leave everyone staring at empty squares, so most of the moments happen several times in a normal match. A corner kick, a throw-in deep in attack, a long pass down the wing: these are the bread and butter of football, and they keep the game moving. A few rarer squares, such as a penalty or a clearance off the line, add the excitement of a long wait that might pay off at any second.

Because the cards never name a team, a player, or a score, they stay fair and friendly for every group. Nobody needs to know the squads, and nobody is put on the spot for supporting the wrong side. The game is about watching the match closely and enjoying the little moments that make football fun, whatever the result turns out to be.

Bingo for a family watch party

A watch party is the perfect place for bingo, because it gives everyone in the room a job to do. Younger children who might drift away from a ninety-minute match suddenly have a card to watch over, and grandparents who rarely follow football can join in without needing to know the rules of the offside trap. Give each person a card before kick-off, hand out pens or small counters for marking, and agree the prize before the match starts.

Keep the prizes light and free: the winner picks the snack for the next match, chooses the music at half-time, or gets to pick which match the family watches next. Avoid anything that costs money or turns a friendly game into a competition with stakes. The point of bingo night is to laugh together, not to keep a ledger.

If you have a big group, print one card per person and let the generator make a different card each time. If you are short on paper, play on phones and tablets instead: tap a square on the screen to mark it, just as you would cross it off on paper. Either way, read the squares aloud before kick-off so younger players know what to watch for, and help them spot the first few moments so they feel part of the game from the start.

Bingo for a classroom or youth group

Teachers and youth leaders can use bingo to turn a match into a gentle learning activity. Before the game, talk through the squares and explain the football words on them: what a corner is, what a free kick means, and why a goalkeeper sometimes starts an attack with a long throw. Children learn the vocabulary of the game by watching for it, which is far more memorable than reading a list.

The tournament itself is a good starting point for wider learning. Brazil is hosting the World Cup for the first time, and the eight host cities stretch from Porto Alegre in the south to Fortaleza in the north-east. A quick look at a map, a few facts about the host country, and a discussion about teamwork and fair play can turn a fun game into a morning of learning that children actually enjoy.

For a group, print a stack of cards in advance and hand them out at random. Agree the rules clearly before kick-off: one card per person, no swapping cards, and the first completed line wins. If the group is large, play for a full house instead of a line, or run two rounds with a fresh card each time so more children get a turn at winning.

Make match day special

Bingo works best as part of a match-day routine. Set up the room before kick-off, lay out the cards and pens, and put out snacks that everyone can share. If the match is an evening game, a simple supper before kick-off keeps everyone comfortable; if it is an afternoon game, fruit and drinks are plenty. The routine matters more than the menu: when match day has a shape, children look forward to it all week.

You can also use the cards to learn about the tournament as it unfolds. The final draw takes place on 11 December 2026, when the eight groups of four teams will finally be known, and the eighteen teams already qualified include the hosts Brazil, the defending champions Spain, and past winners Japan and Germany. As the groups are announced, families can pick a team to follow together and watch their matches with extra interest.

Keep the atmosphere kind. Football is emotional, and a missed penalty or a late goal can upset younger fans. Bingo helps here too: even when a favourite team is losing, a player can still be one square away from a win, which keeps the mood light and gives everyone something to cheer about until the final whistle.

The tournament in brief

The Women's World Cup 2027 runs from Thursday 24 June to Sunday 25 July 2027 in Brazil, with thirty-two teams playing sixty-four matches across eight host cities: Belo Horizonte, Brasília, Fortaleza, Porto Alegre, Recife, Rio de Janeiro, Salvador, and São Paulo. The opening match sees hosts Brazil play at the Maracanã stadium in Rio de Janeiro on 24 June, and the final returns to the Maracanã on 25 July.

The format is easy to follow. Thirty-two teams are drawn into eight groups of four, and the top two teams in each group reach the round of sixteen. From there it is a straight knockout: quarter-finals, semi-finals, a third-place match, and the final. The draw that decides the groups takes place on Friday 11 December 2026 in Rio de Janeiro, with the teams sorted into four pots of eight based on the world rankings published on 7 December 2026.

Spain arrive as defending champions after beating England one goal to nil in the 2023 final in Sydney. The United States have won the tournament four times, in 1991, 1999, 2015, and 2019, and Japan won in 2011. Whoever lifts the trophy in Rio, the tournament promises a month of football, and bingo is a cheerful way to follow it from the first whistle to the last.

Tips for a fair game

A little preparation keeps bingo fair and fun for everyone. First, agree the winning line before kick-off: a row, a column, or a diagonal, or a full house if your group prefers a longer game. Second, agree what counts: a square is marked only when the moment clearly happens, and if the group disagrees, the majority decides. Third, one card per person, and no peeking at other people's cards to block them.

Fourth, decide the tie-breaker in advance. If two people complete a line on the same moment, the fairest answer is a shared win, but some groups prefer a quick decider, such as who marked the free centre square first, or a simple coin toss. Whatever you choose, decide it before the match starts, not in the heat of the moment.

Finally, remember that bingo is a side game, not the main event. If a square is missed because everyone was cheering a goal, that is fine: mark it late, or let it go. The match is the star of the evening, and the card is there to make watching it more fun, not to turn the living room into a courtroom.

Questions people ask

Do I need to print the bingo card?

No. You can play on a phone or tablet by tapping a square to mark it, or you can print the card and mark it with a pen. Both ways work, so use whichever suits your group and your printer.

Can children play World Cup bingo?

Yes. Bingo is suitable for all ages, and younger children enjoy it most when an adult reads the squares aloud before kick-off and helps them spot the first few moments. The game needs no football knowledge to start.

How many squares are on each card?

Each card has twenty-five squares in a five by five grid: twenty-four match-day moments drawn at random from a pool, plus a free centre square that is already marked when the game starts.

What counts as a win?

The usual win is a completed row, column, or diagonal line of five marked squares. Your group can also agree to play for a full house, where every square must be marked before someone wins.

When is the Women's World Cup 2027?

The tournament runs from Thursday 24 June to Sunday 25 July 2027 in Brazil. The opening match and the final are both played at the Maracanã stadium in Rio de Janeiro.

Do the bingo cards name real teams or players?

No. The cards use general match-day moments, such as a corner kick or a save, so they work for every match and never depend on which teams are playing or who is in a squad.
