Screen Reader Compatibility Guide

A screen reader turns a web page into speech or braille, so blind and low-vision people can read and use the web. This guide explains how screen readers read a page, the mistakes that break them, and how to test your own site with one. You need no special equipment to start, only a willingness to listen to your page the way a screen reader user does.

What this is and who needs it

A screen reader is software that turns the words on a screen into speech or braille. Blind people rely on it for almost everything online, and many people with low vision, dyslexia or a temporary eye injury use one too. This guide is for anyone who makes a website, writes for one, or buys one: designers, developers, content writers, small business owners and teachers. You do not need to be blind to care about screen readers, and you do not need a technical background to understand this guide.

If your site works well with a screen reader, it usually works better for everyone. Clearer headings help all readers, good labels help all form users, and a page that works by keyboard helps anyone with a broken mouse. The law in many countries also expects public and commercial sites to be usable by disabled people, so this is not only a kindness but often a duty. This guide explains what a screen reader does, how it reads a page, and the habits that make a site work well with one. It will not turn you into an accessibility expert in one sitting, but it will give you a solid start and a checklist you can follow on your next page.

Screen reader users are not a small edge case. Millions of people use a screen reader every day to shop, study, work, bank and keep in touch. When a site blocks them, they do not struggle through it; they leave and find a site that works. Making your site work for them is therefore good for your visitors and good for your business. The rest of this guide shows you how, step by step, starting with how a screen reader actually reads a page.

How screen readers read a page, step by step

A screen reader does not look at your page. It reads the code underneath it and turns that code into a spoken list of headings, links, buttons, form fields and text. It moves through the page in the order things appear in the code, which is usually the order you wrote them, not the order they appear on screen. A page that looks tidy can still read as a jumble if the code order is confused, so the structure you cannot see matters as much as the layout you can.

The user controls the reading with the keyboard. Arrow keys move line by line, special keys jump from heading to heading, and a list of all links can be called up at any time. Most users do not listen to a whole page from top to bottom; they skim by headings and links, just as sighted readers skim by eye. That is why headings must be real headings, in a sensible order, and why link text must make sense on its own. A link that says click here tells a screen reader user nothing when it is read out of a list of twenty identical links.

Forms work the same way: the screen reader reads each field together with its label, so a field with no label is announced as a blank box with no purpose. Images are announced by their alt text, or skipped entirely if the alt text is empty. Tables are read cell by cell, with the header row announced so the user knows what each column means. All of this happens through the page's structure, which is why good structure is the heart of screen reader compatibility.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

The good news is that making a site work with a screen reader costs very little if you start early. The best-known screen reader for Windows, NVDA, is free. VoiceOver is built into every iPhone, iPad and Mac at no extra cost, and TalkBack is built into Android phones. You already own the main testing tools, or can download them for nothing. What it costs is time and attention: time to learn the habits, time to test, and time to fix what you find. For a small site, a first pass over the main pages takes a few hours. For a large site, the work is best done page by page, starting with the pages visitors use most.

Before you start, you need a way to test with a keyboard alone: put your mouse aside and use only the Tab key, the arrow keys and Enter. You need a screen reader installed and a little patience while you learn its keys. You also need a short list of the pages that matter most, so you test the important journeys first. The most expensive way to do this work is to leave it until the end, when fixing a broken structure means rebuilding pages. The cheapest way is to build it in from the start, when a good heading or a clear label costs nothing at all.

Training helps too. A short course or a good book on web accessibility pays for itself quickly, and many free guides exist online. If you work in a team, agree on a few simple rules everyone follows: real headings in order, alt text on every meaningful image, labels on every form field, and no action that only works with a mouse. Those four rules alone prevent most of the problems screen reader users meet.

This guide teaches the ideas and the checks, but it cannot test your site for you. Automated checkers catch only a fraction of real problems, so always test with a real screen reader before you call a page accessible. If your site must meet a legal standard, get a proper audit from a qualified accessibility specialist.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is an image with no alt text, or alt text that says nothing useful. A photo of a product with the alt text image123 tells the user nothing, while a short sentence about the product tells them everything they need. The fix is simple: write alt text that describes what the image is for, and leave it empty for images that are purely decorative.

The second common mistake is a form field with no label. A box that a sighted user understands from the words next to it is a mystery to a screen reader unless the label is properly attached. Always pair each field with a real label element, not just nearby text. The third mistake is link text that makes no sense alone. Links that say click here, read more or this force the user to guess where they go; instead, make the link text describe its destination, such as our guide to alt text.

The fourth mistake is a page that only works with a mouse: menus that open on hover, buttons that respond only to clicks, and sliders with no keyboard controls. Everything a mouse can do, a keyboard must be able to do too. The fifth mistake is structure built for looks: headings chosen for their size instead of their level, bold text used instead of a real heading, and lists made from line breaks instead of list markup. Use the right element for the job and let the stylesheet handle the looks.

The sixth mistake is content that appears without warning, such as a pop-up that opens while the user is reading, or a page that reloads and sends the user back to the top. Warn users before anything changes, and keep the user's place. Each of these mistakes is easy to fix once you know it is there, and most of them are free to fix.

A practical checklist you can follow

Work through this list on every important page, and fix what fails before you move on. Keep it with your publishing notes and use it every time a page changes.

  • Read the page with the keyboard alone: can you reach every link, button and field, and can you always see where you are?
  • Check the headings: is there one main heading, do the subheadings follow in order, and does each one describe its section?
  • Check every image: does each meaningful image have short, useful alt text, and is decorative art marked to be skipped?
  • Check every form: does each field have a label, are required fields clearly marked, and are error messages explained in words?
  • Check every link: does the link text make sense on its own, out of context?
  • Check for mouse-only features: does every menu, slider and pop-up work by keyboard?
  • Listen to the page with a real screen reader from top to bottom and note every place where the spoken words confuse you.
  • Check that nothing on the page flashes, moves or plays sound without a way to stop it.
  • Check that colour is never the only way meaning is shown: errors, required fields and status messages need words as well as colour.
  • Ask a screen reader user to try the page if you can: real feedback beats every checklist.

If a page passes all ten checks, it is in good shape. If it fails two or three, fix the headings and the labels first, because those two fixes help the most users in the least time.

Tips from real practice

Test early and test often. The cheapest time to find a problem is while you are building the page, not after it is published. A two-minute keyboard check after each change catches most issues before they reach a visitor. Listen to your page the way a user would: put on headphones, start the screen reader, close your eyes and try to complete a real task, such as finding a product or filling in a form. The places where you get stuck are the places your users get stuck.

Write link text and headings for the ear, not only the eye. Read them aloud: if a heading sounds vague spoken on its own, it is vague. Keep alt text short and purposeful, and remember that a screen reader user does not need to know what a decorative border looks like. Learn the keyboard shortcuts of one screen reader well instead of learning a little about many. NVDA on Windows is a good place to start because it is free and widely used.

Finally, remember that accessibility is a habit, not a project. Pages change, new content arrives, and old fixes rot. A short check each time you publish keeps a site accessible far better than a grand audit once a year. The goal is not a perfect score but a site that real people can really use. One more habit helps more than any tool: talk to screen reader users. If you know someone who uses one, ask them to show you how they browse. Watching a skilled user move through a page in seconds, jumping by headings and links, changes how you think about structure forever.

Questions people ask

What is a screen reader?

A screen reader is software that reads the text on a screen aloud, or sends it to a braille display, so a blind or low-vision person can use a computer or phone. It does not look at the screen the way a sighted person does; it reads the page's underlying structure, including headings, links, buttons and form labels. Common screen readers include NVDA and JAWS on Windows, VoiceOver on Apple devices, and TalkBack on Android.

How does a screen reader read a web page?

A screen reader moves through a page in the order the content appears in the code, not in the order it appears visually. It announces headings, landmarks and links so the user can jump around, and it reads form fields together with their labels. A well-structured page reads like a well-organised document; a page built only for looks reads like a jumble of disconnected words.

What mistakes break a site for screen reader users?

The most damaging mistakes are images with no alt text, form fields with no labels, links that all say click here, and buttons that only work with a mouse. Pages that rely on colour alone to show meaning, or that trap the keyboard inside a pop-up, also shut screen reader users out. Most of these mistakes are easy to fix once you know they exist.

How do I write good alt text?

Describe what the image is for, not every detail in it: a photo of a smiling team at a picnic needs a short sentence about the team, not a list of every face. Keep it to one or two short sentences, skip phrases like image of, and leave the alt text empty for purely decorative images so the screen reader skips them. If an image is also a link, the alt text should say where the link goes.

How can I test my site with a screen reader?

Start with a keyboard-only test: unplug your mouse and try to reach every link, button and form field with the Tab key. Then try a real screen reader: NVDA is free on Windows, VoiceOver is built into every Apple device, and TalkBack is built into Android. Listen to your page from start to finish and note every place where the spoken words do not make sense.

What is the most common accessibility mistake?

The most common mistake is treating accessibility as a finishing touch instead of part of the build. Images without alt text, forms without labels and links that say click here all come from the same habit: building for the eye and forgetting the ear. Fixing them late costs far more time than building them in from the start.
