How to Make a Comic Strip with AI

By the Funtasha team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

A comic strip is a short story told in pictures and speech bubbles, and AI can help you plan the story, draw the panels, and write the dialogue. This guide shows you how, from story beats to the finished strip, with three ready mini-story outlines to get you started.

Comic strips are one of the most satisfying things to make. A few panels, a simple story, and a good punchline can make someone laugh in under a minute, and the format is forgiving: you do not need to be an artist to tell a story in pictures.

AI helps at every step. It can suggest stories, draw the panels in a consistent style, and help you write dialogue that fits in a speech bubble. This guide walks through the process, then gives you three complete mini-story outlines you can turn into strips today.

Comic strips are for fun, so keep them kind. Never make a strip that mocks a real person, and use only your own photos or images you have permission to use as a starting point for your characters.

How a comic strip works: story beats

Most comic strips follow three beats: a setup, a twist, and a punchline. The setup introduces the character and the situation, the twist changes something, and the punchline delivers the surprise or the joke. In a three-panel strip, each beat gets one panel, which makes planning simple.

A four-panel strip adds a beat, often a pause or a reaction, which gives the joke more room. The first panel sets the scene, the second builds it up, the third adds the twist, and the fourth delivers the punchline. Four panels suit stories with a little more dialogue or a slower build.

Keep the story small. A comic strip works best with one character, one place, and one idea. A cat who wants the last biscuit, a robot learning to dance, or a gardener whose flowers keep disappearing are all good comic strip ideas, because they are simple, visual, and easy to follow.

Planning your panels

Before you draw anything, sketch the strip on paper or in a note. Draw three or four empty boxes, and write one line in each box describing what happens there. This rough plan is called a thumbnail, and it saves time because you can fix the story before you spend effort on the pictures.

Think about what each panel shows. A wide shot suits the setup, because it shows the place and the character. A close-up suits a reaction, because it shows the face. A panel with no words at all can be powerful, because the reader fills in the silence.

Leave space for speech bubbles. A panel crammed with pictures leaves no room for words, so plan for the bubbles from the start. Ask the image tool for a simple composition with empty space at the top of each panel, where the bubbles will go.

Keeping your character consistent

The hardest part of AI comics is keeping the same character in every panel. Image tools can change a character's hair, clothes, or face from one picture to the next, so you need a trick: describe your character the same way in every prompt, word for word.

Write a short character description and reuse it exactly. For example: a small round robot with a blue body, big friendly eyes, and a red scarf. Paste that description into every panel prompt, and add only the action and the setting for that panel. Consistency in the words leads to consistency in the pictures.

Simple characters are easier to keep consistent than detailed ones. A character with one clear colour, one clear feature, and a simple shape will survive from panel to panel far better than a character with many small details. If your character keeps changing, simplify the description and try again.

Writing dialogue that fits

Speech bubbles are small, so dialogue must be short. Aim for one or two lines per bubble, and no more than two bubbles per panel. If a line is too long, shorten it, or move part of it to the next panel. Comic dialogue is closer to how people actually talk: short, direct, and full of feeling.

Read your dialogue aloud. If it sounds stiff or formal, rewrite it the way you would say it. A character who says I am very hungry indeed sounds less funny than one who says I am starving. Short words and strong feelings make dialogue come alive.

AI can help you write dialogue. Describe the scene, the characters, and the joke, and ask for three versions of the line. Pick the one that makes you smile, and change any words that do not sound like your character.

Three ready mini-story outlines

These three outlines are written fresh for this guide. Each one has three beats, and each beat becomes one panel. Pick one, write your character description, and draw the panels.

  1. The Biscuit Thief. Panel one: a cat sits by an empty biscuit tin, looking guilty. Panel two: the owner holds up a biscuit and asks who took the last one. Panel three: the cat points at the dog, who is fast asleep with crumbs on his nose. Punchline: the dog wakes up, confused, as the cat quietly eats the biscuit.
  2. The Robot Who Learned to Dance. Panel one: a small robot watches children dancing and tries to copy them, stiffly. Panel two: the robot practises all night, spinning and wobbling. Panel three: the robot dances at the school fair, and the children cheer. Punchline: the robot bows, and a small spring pops out of its head.
  3. The Gardener and the Missing Flowers. Panel one: a gardener plants flowers and waters them carefully. Panel two: the flowers vanish overnight, and the gardener finds tiny footprints. Panel three: the gardener discovers a family of rabbits wearing the flowers as hats. Punchline: the gardener plants a special flower patch just for the rabbits.

Assembling your panels

Once your panels are drawn, arrange them in order, left to right and top to bottom, so the reader follows the story naturally. Add the speech bubbles in the same order, with the first speaker's bubble higher or further left than the second speaker's, so the reading order is clear.

Keep the lettering large and clear. Hand-lettering adds charm, but a simple, bold font is easier to read, especially on a phone screen. Leave a little space around each panel so the strip breathes, and add a title at the top if you like.

Share your finished strip with family and friends, print it for the fridge, or collect your strips into a small booklet. A series of strips about the same character is even better, because readers start to know the character and look forward to the next adventure.

Questions people ask

How many panels should a comic strip have?

Three or four panels suit most short stories. Three panels fit a setup, a twist, and a punchline, while four panels give the joke a little more room to build.

How do I keep my character the same in every panel?

Write a short character description and reuse it word for word in every panel prompt. Simple characters with one clear colour and one clear feature stay consistent best.

How long should comic dialogue be?

One or two lines per bubble, and no more than two bubbles per panel. Read the dialogue aloud, and shorten anything that sounds stiff or formal.

Can AI write the story for my comic?

Yes. Ask for a short story outline with a setup, a twist, and a punchline, then adapt it to your characters. The three outlines in this guide are a good place to start.

What is the best way to share a finished strip?

Print it for the fridge, share it with family and friends, or collect your strips into a small booklet. A series about the same character keeps readers coming back.
