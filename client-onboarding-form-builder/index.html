Why onboarding matters


The first form a client fills in sets the tone for the whole project. A clear, friendly onboarding form tells the client you are organised, that you know what you need, and that you respect their time. A messy one — or no form at all — means the same questions asked three times by email, missing files discovered halfway through, and a project that starts late because nobody asked about the deadline.


Onboarding is not paperwork for its own sake. It is the moment you learn what the client actually wants, who you will be working with, what already exists, and what success looks like. Every answer you collect up front is an email you do not have to send later and a misunderstanding you do not have to fix. The hour you spend building a good form pays for itself on the first project that uses it.

What a good form asks


A good onboarding form asks four kinds of things. First, the basics: who the client is, who your contact will be, and how to reach them. Second, the work itself: what they need, what success looks like, and when they need it. Third, the materials: files, brand details, and anything you need before you can start. Fourth, the practicalities: budget range, timeline, and access to the tools or accounts the work touches.


The order matters. Put the easy questions first — name, contact, what they need — and save the harder ones, like budget and access, for later in the form. A client who has already answered five questions is much more likely to answer the sixth than a client who meets a budget question first. Keep the whole form short enough to finish in about ten minutes; ten to fifteen questions is a good target for most projects.


Every question should earn its place. If you would not act on the answer, cut the question. Clients can tell when a form is fishing rather than asking, and a long form full of questions nobody reads teaches them to answer carelessly. A short form full of questions that clearly matter gets careful answers.


The sections, explained


Services needed covers what the client wants you to do and what a good result looks like. Goals and success goes a step further: who the work is for, and how the client will judge whether it worked. These two sections together are the heart of the form — if you only include two sections, include these.


Contacts is the section people skip and then regret skipping. Knowing the main contact, a phone number, and the best email address to reach them sounds trivial until the one person who can approve something is on holiday and nobody knows who stands in for them. Ask for a second contact if the project is large.


Files needed asks what the client can share: documents, brand files, photographs, previous work. Budget range asks what they have planned, in broad terms. Timeline asks when the work should start and whether there is a fixed date. Brand details asks how the client describes their own brand. Access and logins asks which accounts or tools you will need — and reminds you never to ask for passwords in a form. Passwords should be shared through a proper password manager, never in plain text.


Writing questions clients actually answer


The best onboarding questions are specific, short, and easy to answer without thinking too hard. "What does success look like for this project?" gets a better answer than "Please describe your objectives in detail." "When would you like the work to start?" beats "What is your ideal timeline?" One asks for a date; the other asks for an essay.


Avoid jargon the client may not share. If you ask about "deliverables" or "stakeholders", some clients will guess rather than ask what you mean, and a guessed answer is worse than a blank one. Plain words — "What do you want us to make?" and "Who needs to approve it?" — get honest answers from everyone.


Give examples where a question could be read two ways. After "Who is the audience for this work?", add a hint like "for example, new customers, existing clients, or your own team". A short example shows the kind of answer you want without putting words in the client's mouth. And keep the tone friendly: a form that reads like a tax return gets tax-return answers.


Files and access: the part everyone forgets


More projects stall on missing files than on anything else. The client meant to send the logo, the photographs are on someone else's computer, the brand colours live in a file nobody can find. Asking for files in the onboarding form — with a list of what you need — moves that problem to the start of the project, where it belongs, instead of the middle, where it costs days.


Be specific about what you need. "Your logo" is vague; "your logo as a high-resolution file, and the colours and fonts you use" is a request the client can act on. If you need access to accounts — a website, a social account, a shared drive — ask which accounts and who can approve access. Never ask for a password in a form, in an email, or in a chat message. Use a password manager or an invite link, and say so in the form so the client knows what to expect.


When the files arrive, check them straight away. A low-resolution logo or a missing font found on day one is a quick message; found on the day of delivery, it is a crisis. The onboarding form is only useful if someone reads the answers and acts on them.


Sending the form and following up


Copy the finished form from this page and paste it into an email or a document, or download it as a text file. For a neater result, paste the questions into your usual form tool and send the link — the wording is yours to reuse however you like. Send it with a short message saying what it is for and when you need it back. A deadline, even a soft one, roughly doubles the speed of replies.


When the form comes back with blanks, follow up on the blanks only. A short message — "Thanks, this is really helpful; two things are still missing, and I need them before I can start" — gets a faster answer than sending the whole form again. Say why you need each missing answer; clients answer faster when they know a blank is holding up their own project.


Keep the completed form where the project lives. When a question comes up mid-project — what did the client actually ask for, who approves this, what was the deadline — the onboarding form is the record that settles it. A form that is filled in and then lost is barely better than no form at all.


Keeping client information safe


An onboarding form collects personal information: names, phone numbers, email addresses, sometimes business details. That information deserves care. Store completed forms where only the people working on the project can reach them, and do not forward them around more widely than the work needs.


Ask only for what the project needs, and say what you will do with it. A line in your message — "I will only use these details for your project" — costs nothing and builds trust. Delete details you no longer need when a project ends, and keep passwords out of forms entirely, always.

Questions people ask


What should an onboarding form ask first?
Start with the essentials: what the client needs, who the contact is, and when they need it. Save detailed questions about files, access and brand for later in the form, after the client is already answering.


How long should an onboarding form be?
Short enough to finish in ten minutes. Ten to fifteen questions is a good target for most projects. If your form is longer, split it: the essentials first, and the detail once the project is confirmed.


Should I ask about budget in the form?
Yes, if budget shapes what you can offer. A range question is friendlier than an exact figure, and it saves both sides time when expectations are far apart. Keep it optional if your clients are shy about money.


What if a client leaves questions blank?
That is normal. Follow up with a short message asking only about the missing answers, and say why you need them. Most clients answer quickly when they know a blank answer is holding up their project.
