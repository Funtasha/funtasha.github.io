Art Supply Comparison Portal

Buying art supplies is confusing because the same shelf holds student paint and artist paint, three kinds of paper, and brushes from pocket money to serious money. This guide compares the types side by side, so you can choose with your eyes open.

The staff will help if you ask, but it helps to arrive knowing what the types are and what to compare. That is what this guide does.

The supplies explained

Paint comes in types, and the type decides how it behaves. Watercolour is paint thinned with water, transparent, and best on paper — it rewards a light touch and punishes overworking. Acrylic is paint in a plastic base: it dries fast, sticks to almost anything, and can be used thin like watercolour or thick like oil. Oil paint dries slowly, blends beautifully, and needs solvents for cleaning. Gouache is watercolour with body: opaque, matte, and forgiving, good for bold shapes and illustration.

Brushes come in shapes, and the shape decides the mark. A round brush makes lines and details; a flat brush makes washes and sharp edges; a filbert, with its rounded flat head, blends and softens; a fan brush makes texture and foliage. Bristle matters too: soft hair holds water and makes smooth marks, stiff bristle pushes thick paint around. One good brush in each of two shapes will carry a beginner further than a cheap set of twenty.

Surfaces matter as much as paint. Watercolour needs paper made for it — thick, absorbent, and sized to hold the paint on the surface. Acrylic and oil need canvas or board, primed so the paint sits on top rather than soaking in. Mixed-media paper is the all-rounder: heavy enough for wet paint, smooth enough for drawing. The wrong surface will fight you, whatever the paint.

What to compare

When you compare two supplies, compare the things that change how they work, not the things on the packaging. For paint, the first is pigment load: how much colour the paint actually holds. More pigment means stronger colour, cleaner mixing, and less paint needed per stroke. The second is lightfastness: whether the colour fades in sunlight. A fugitive colour looks bright in the tube and dull on the wall a year later. The third is handling: how the paint flows, how it layers, and how it feels under the brush. Handling is personal — test it before you commit to a set.

For brushes, compare the hair, the shape, and the spring. A good brush snaps back to a point after a stroke; a poor one splays and stays splayed. Check the ferrule, the metal band that holds the hair: it should be tight, with no loose hairs and no wobble. Hold the brush as you would to paint — balance matters more than brand.

For paper and canvas, compare weight, surface, and sizing. Heavier paper buckles less; a rougher surface holds more water and gives more texture; sizing controls how the paint sits. Ask to touch the paper before you buy it, and buy the best you can afford — the surface is the one supply you cannot upgrade later, and a bad surface ruins good paint.

Student grade vs artist grade

Almost every paint and paper comes in two grades, and the difference is real. Student-grade supplies use less pigment and more filler, so colours are weaker, mixing is muddier, and the paint behaves less honestly. Artist-grade supplies hold more pigment, mix more cleanly, and last longer on the wall. The gap is biggest in paint and smallest in brushes, where a good student brush can serve a beginner well for a long time.

Start with student grade if you are trying a medium for the first time. There is no sense in buying artist-grade oils before you know whether you like oils. But upgrade as soon as you know you will keep going, and upgrade one thing at a time, starting with the supply you use most. The usual order is paper first, then paint, then brushes — paper makes the biggest difference to the result, and brushes make the smallest.

Be honest about where you are. A beginner with artist-grade paint and cheap paper will struggle; a beginner with student-grade paint and good paper will learn faster. Spend where it shows, save where it does not, and remember that the best supply is the one you actually use.

The starter kit

A starter kit should be small, good, and complete enough to make real work. For watercolour: good paper, six or seven tubes of paint — a warm and a cool yellow, red, and blue, plus one earth colour — one medium round brush, a palette, and two water jars. For acrylic: a small set of paints, two or three brushes, and canvas boards or heavy mixed-media paper. For drawing: a range of pencils, an eraser, and a sketchbook you like enough to carry.

Buy less than you think you need. A small set used often beats a big set gathering dust, and mixing your own colours teaches you more than owning forty tubes. Add one new supply at a time, when you feel the lack of it, and you will build a kit that fits the way you actually work.

Check current prices before you buy, and compare shops — the same supply can cost very different amounts in different places. Sales and sets can save money, but only if the set holds things you will use. A set of forty colours, half of which you never touch, is not a bargain.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is buying forty colours before one painting. A small set, mixed well, gives you every colour you need, and mixing teaches you colour faster than owning tubes ever will.

The second is cheap paper that buckles. Paper is the one supply you cannot upgrade later, and bad paper punishes good paint. Buy the best paper you can afford, even if it means fewer paints.

The third is a big brush set of poor quality. One good round brush beats twenty that splay, shed, and never return to a point. Buy one good brush, then add shapes as you need them.

The fourth is comparing prices without comparing what is in the tube. A cheap paint with little pigment costs more per painting than a dearer paint that goes further. Compare pigment load, not just the price tag — and check current prices, because they change often.

The fifth is buying for the artist you wish you were, instead of the one you are. Start with what your next painting needs, not with a studio full of supplies. The kit grows with the work.

Questions people ask

What art supplies do I need first?

Start with the medium you want to learn: paint, brushes, and a surface to paint on, plus a palette and water jars. Buy less than you think you need — a small set used often beats a big set gathering dust.

What is student grade vs artist grade?

Student-grade supplies use less pigment and more filler, so colours are weaker and mix less cleanly. Artist-grade supplies hold more pigment and behave more honestly, and they are worth the extra cost once you know you will keep going.

How do I compare paints?

Compare pigment load, lightfastness, and how the paint handles — not the number of colours in the set. Two paints with the same colour name can behave very differently, so test before you commit to a brand.

Which paper should I buy?

Buy paper made for your medium: watercolour paper for watercolour, heavier mixed-media paper for acrylic and drawing. Cheap thin paper buckles and pills, and it will fight you at every step.

How much should I spend to start?

Spend the most on paper and the least on extras, and check current prices before you buy, because they change often. A small set of good basics costs less than a big set of poor ones, and it will last longer.

Where do I buy art supplies?

Art shops, online art retailers, and craft stores all sell them, and each has its strengths. A local art shop is worth a visit, because you can see and hold the supplies before you buy.
