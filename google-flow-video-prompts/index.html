Google Flow Video Prompts

By the Funtasha team · Published 20 November 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026

Google Flow turns written prompts into short films. This guide shows how a Flow prompt is built, how to keep a character the same across scenes, and how to start.

Never use Flow to make a film that pretends a real person said or did something they did not. Keep it fun, keep it honest, and say it is AI-made when you share it.

What Google Flow is

Google Flow is Google's AI filmmaking tool. Where most video tools make a single clip from a sentence, Flow is built around the idea of a film: scenes, characters, camera moves, and clips that belong together. You describe a scene, Flow films it, and you can keep the same character going through scene after scene.

People are noticing. Google Flow gets about 823,000 monthly searches, up 809% in a year and 124% in three months. That is not a slow climb — it is a surge, and it tells you that filmmakers, teachers, small sellers and curious beginners are all arriving at the same tool at the same time.

Flow needs an account to use, and some features need a paid plan, so check the current details on Google's own pages before you start. The guide below is about the prompts, which cost nothing to learn: once you know how a Flow prompt is built, you can write one for any scene you can imagine.

How a Flow prompt is built

A Flow prompt has five parts, and they always come in the same order: scene, subject, camera, light, mood.

  • Scene. Where are we? A misty mountain lake, a cosy bookshop, a desert road at sunset. Name the place and the time of day together, because they set everything else.
  • Subject. Who or what is in the scene? An explorer with a backpack, a robot learning to paint, a cat in a tiny scarf. One subject, described so clearly you could pick it out of a crowd.
  • Camera. How is it filmed? A slow zoom draws the eye in. A gentle pan sweeps the scene. A slow orbit circles the subject. A tracking shot follows the character as they move. One camera move per clip.
  • Light. Soft morning light, warm lamplight, storm grey. Light sets the time, the weather and the feeling in a single phrase.
  • Mood. Calm, dreamy, dramatic, playful. The mood is the last word, and it colours everything that came before it.

The scene builder at the top of this page follows this shape: pick a setting, a character and a camera move, and it assembles a Flow-ready prompt you can copy.

Keeping a character the same across scenes

The hardest part of any AI film is keeping the same character looking the same from scene to scene. A character who is a young explorer in one clip can become a different person in the next if the description drifts.

The fix is to describe your character the same way, word for word, in every prompt. Not roughly the same — exactly the same. 'A young explorer with a backpack, brown hair, a green jacket' in every scene, not 'a young explorer' in one and 'a hiker with a pack' in the next. The tool matches what you repeat, so repetition is your friend.

Pick two or three details that define the character and never change them: the backpack, the jacket, the hair. Keep the face small in the frame where you can, because faces drift most, and let the outfit and the silhouette do the recognising. If a scene comes back with the wrong character, change nothing else — run the same prompt again, and the next attempt often lands closer.

Short clips first, then stitching

A film made with Flow is not one long clip. It is a row of short clips, each one a single scene, stitched together into something longer. That is how every Flow film is built, and it is good news, because short clips are easier to get right.

Start with one clip: one scene, one character, one camera move. Watch it. If it works, make the next scene — same character, same description, new setting. If it does not work, change one thing and try again. Each clip is cheap to remake, so you can afford to be picky.

When you have a few clips you like, stitch them in order. Many people edit in CapCut, trimming the ends, adding music and titles, and joining the clips into a short film. The stitching is where a row of clips becomes a story: a beginning, a middle, and an end, even if the whole film is under a minute long.

What Flow does well

Flow shines brightest on moody scenes and product shots — the two kinds of film where atmosphere matters more than action.

Moody scenes are Flow's home ground: a misty lake at dawn, a bookshop at night, a lighthouse in a storm. Slow camera moves, soft light, a single character, and a mood that fills the frame. These scenes play to the tool's strengths, because nothing moves fast and everything has time to settle.

Product shots work beautifully too. A bottle on a table with light moving across it, a cake turning slowly on a stand, a pair of shoes in golden light. The product stays still, the camera moves gently, and the result looks like an advert. Small sellers use these clips for shop pages and social posts, and they cost nothing but a sentence.

Both kinds of film share the same recipe: one subject, one camera move, and a mood. If your first Flow film is a moody scene or a product shot, you are playing to the tool's strengths.

What Flow struggles with

Flow has limits, and knowing them saves you from fighting the tool for a film it cannot make yet.

Hands are the classic struggle. Fingers bend the wrong way, hands merge with objects, a wave becomes a blur. Keep hands small in the frame, or out of it: a character seen from behind, or at a distance, avoids the problem entirely.

Fast action is the second struggle. A chase, a dance with quick turns, a ball flying across the frame — fast motion smears and warps, because the tool has to invent too much between frames. Slow down the action: a walk instead of a run, a turn instead of a spin, a wave instead of a throw.

Crowds are the third. Many people in one frame means many faces to keep consistent, and the tool will usually get some of them wrong. One character, or two at most, is the safe choice. The limits will shrink as the tool improves, but for now, slow and simple wins.

Your first Flow film: a simple plan

Here is a plan for a first film that takes an evening, not a week.

Pick one character and one setting from the builder above, and make your first clip. Then make two more with the same character in different settings: morning, afternoon, and night, or three places on one journey. Keep the character description identical in all three prompts.

Watch all three clips. Pick the best two, or keep all three if they all work. Stitch them in order — a beginning, a middle, and an end — and add a title and a piece of music if you like. Then share it, and say it is AI-made.

That is a film: three clips, one character, one evening. Your second film will be easier, because you will already know which words Flow listens to — and which ones it ignores.

Questions people ask

What is Google Flow?

Google Flow is Google's AI filmmaking tool. You describe a scene in words — the setting, the character, the camera move and the mood — and Flow turns it into a short film clip, with the same character able to carry across scenes.

How is a Flow prompt different from a photo prompt?

A photo prompt describes a still moment, while a Flow prompt describes a moving scene, so it needs a camera move and a sense of time as well as the subject and the style. The shape is scene, subject, camera, light, mood — the builder at the top of this page follows it exactly.

How do I keep the same character in every scene?

Describe your character the same way, word for word, in every prompt — the same outfit, the same hair, the same two or three defining details. Repetition is what the tool matches on, so never reword the character between scenes, and keep the face small in the frame where you can.

How long are Flow clips?

Flow makes short clips, and the exact length depends on the tool and the plan you are on, so check Google's own pages for the current limit. Short clips are the right shape anyway — a Flow film is built from several short scenes stitched together, not one long take.

Is Google Flow free?

Google Flow needs an account to use, and some features need a paid plan, so check the current details on Google's own pages before you start. Plans and limits change, and the tool's own pages are the only reliable place to see what is free today.

What can I make with Flow?

Flow is best at moody scenes, product shots and short character films — a misty lake at dawn, a bottle turning in golden light, an explorer crossing three landscapes. It struggles with hands, fast action and crowds, so slow and simple subjects work best.
