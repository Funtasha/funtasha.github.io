Photography Lighting Planner

Good light makes a good photo, and the best light is free. This planner teaches you when natural light looks best, how to use window light, golden hour, open shade and bounce, and gives you a planner sheet so you arrive at your shoot with a plan, not a hope.

This page is a planning guide, not a light meter. It cannot measure the light in your room or at your location, so use your camera's own exposure reading on the day, and use the planner sheet below to prepare before you go.

Why light matters more than the camera

Every photo is a record of light. The camera matters far less than most people think: a phone in beautiful light will beat an expensive camera in harsh, flat light almost every time. Light decides the mood of a photo, the shape of a face, the colour of a scene, and whether a picture feels warm and inviting or cold and hard. When a photo disappoints you, the problem is usually the light, not the camera.

There are three things to notice about light: where it comes from, how soft or hard it is, and what colour it is. Light from the side gives shape and texture, because it leaves one side of the subject in gentle shadow. Light from directly in front flattens things, which can be kind to faces but dull for landscapes. Light from behind makes silhouettes and glowing edges. Soft light, like light on a cloudy day or light through a big window, wraps around a subject and hides small flaws. Hard light, like direct midday sun, makes sharp shadows and strong contrast.

The colour of light changes through the day too. Morning and evening light is warm and golden. Midday light is neutral and bright. Shade is slightly cool and blue. Your eyes adjust to these changes without you noticing, but the camera records them, which is why the same street can look completely different at eight in the morning and at six in the evening. Planning a shoot means planning around these three things: direction, softness, and colour.

Natural light through the day

The sun gives you a different kind of light every few hours, and each kind suits different subjects. The table below is a rough guide for a clear day; the exact times shift with the season and where you live, so treat the hours as starting points and watch the sky on the day.

Before sunriseSoft, blue, dim, evenQuiet streets, city lights, calm water
First hour after sunriseWarm, low, long shadowsPortraits, landscapes, long-shadow scenes

Cloud changes everything, usually for the better. A cloudy sky is nature's biggest soft light: shadows soften, colours look richer, and faces photograph beautifully without squinting. Do not cancel a shoot because of cloud — change the plan instead, and use the soft light for portraits and close-up work. The only light that is truly difficult is a mix of very bright sun and deep shade in the same frame, because the camera cannot hold both at once.

Window light: the easiest studio you own

A large window is the best free studio light there is. The bigger the window, and the closer your subject stands to it, the softer the light will be. A north-facing window (in the northern half of the world) gives steady, soft light all day, which is why painters have loved it for centuries. A window that gets direct sun gives stronger, warmer light for part of the day, and you can soften it by hanging a thin white curtain or a sheet of baking paper across it.

For a portrait, place your subject near the window and turn them slowly until you like what you see. Facing the window gives even, flattering light with a bright catch of light in the eyes. Turned sideways to the window, one side of the face lights up and the other falls into soft shadow, which gives the face shape and depth. With the window behind them, you get a glowing outline and a darker face — beautiful for a mood shot, but you will need to brighten the face or accept a silhouette.

Distance matters too. Light falls off quickly as you move away from a window, so a subject two steps back from the glass gets much less light than one standing right beside it, and the background behind them goes darker. Use that: a subject close to the window against a darker room makes the person stand out with almost no effort. And watch the background — a tidy corner, a plain wall, or a curtain behind your subject keeps the attention where it belongs.

Golden hour: warm light, short window

Golden hour is the hour or so after sunrise and before sunset, when the sun sits low and the light turns warm and golden. Shadows stretch long, skin looks warm, and ordinary places glow. It is the most loved light in photography, and it is also the most rushed: the best of it can last twenty minutes, and then it is gone. That is why it needs planning.

Arrive early. If sunset is at six, be in place by five, with your subject, your ideas, and your camera ready. The light changes every few minutes, so take photos throughout rather than waiting for one perfect moment — the shot from ten minutes before sunset often beats the shot from sunset itself. A low sun also means long shadows, so think about where your own shadow falls, and where your subject's shadow falls, before you start.

Golden hour is kind to faces, because the warm, low light softens skin and makes eyes glow. It is also the time when backlight works best: place the sun behind your subject for a glowing rim of light around hair and shoulders, and let the face fall into a softer, warmer shadow. If the face goes too dark, a white board, a light wall, or even a pale piece of card held low in front of the subject will bounce enough light back to lift it.

Open shade: soft light on a bright day

Open shade is the shade of a building, a tree, or a roof on a bright day, with open sky above and around it. The light there is soft, even, and slightly cool, with no harsh shadows and no squinting. It is the easiest light for portraits on a sunny day, and it is free and everywhere: the shaded side of a street, under a tree, in a doorway, under a bridge.

The trick is to keep your subject fully in the shade, with no patches of sun on their face or clothes. A single bright patch on a forehead or a shoulder will pull the eye and spoil the shot. Stand your subject just inside the shade line, facing toward the open sky, so their face catches the soft sky light. The background behind them can be sunny — that contrast is fine, and often beautiful — but keep the sun out of your own lens, or it will wash the photo out.

Open shade is also the answer to the midday problem. When the sun is high and hard, and every face has deep shadows under the eyes, move into shade and the problem disappears. The light is softer, the colours are cleaner, and people relax because they are not squinting into the sun. Some of the best outdoor portraits ever taken were shot in open shade on a bright day, with nothing more than a wall and a sky.

Bounce: shaping light with what you have

Bounce means turning light back toward your subject with a pale surface. A white wall, a light ceiling, a sheet of white card, a car roof, or a shop window can all act as a reflector, and the effect is real: shadows lift, eyes brighten, and the light feels fuller. You do not need to buy a reflector to try it — a large piece of white card from a stationery shop works well, and so does a white bedsheet held by a friend.

The position of the bounce surface matters. Held low in front of a subject, it lifts shadows under the chin and eyes. Held to the side, it fills the shadow side of the face. Held behind the camera, it adds a gentle overall lift. The closer the surface is to the subject, the stronger the bounce, so start close and move back until the effect looks natural. If the bounce is too strong, the light goes flat; if it is too weak, nothing changes. Small moves make a big difference.

Colour matters too. A white surface bounces neutral light. A silver surface bounces brighter, slightly cooler light. A gold surface bounces warm light, which can be lovely at golden hour and too yellow at midday. A coloured wall will tint your light with its colour — a green wall gives a green cast, a red wall a warm one. Use that on purpose or avoid it, but notice it: the colour of the bounce is the colour of the light it adds.

Your shoot planner sheet

A planner sheet turns good intentions into a shoot that actually happens. Fill it in the evening before, or on the morning of the shoot, and take it with you — on paper or on your phone. The sheet below has blank rows for your own plan; copy it into a notebook, or use it as a checklist in your head. The point is to decide the light, the place, and the subject before you leave home.

When you fill in the sheet, start with the light, not the place. Decide which kind of light you want — golden hour warmth, window softness, open shade — and then pick the time and the place that give it to you. Write down one idea per row, not ten: one subject, one setup, one clear plan. A sheet with five calm rows will get you better photos than a sheet with twenty rushed ones.

Leave room for the weather. If the forecast changes, change the plan rather than cancelling: cloud becomes a portrait day, rain becomes a window-light day, and a bright midday becomes an open-shade day. The planner sheet is a guide, not a contract. The best shoots often happen when the plan bends a little and the light surprises you.

Common lighting mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is shooting into bright sun at midday and wondering why every face is half shadow. The fix is simple: move into open shade, turn the subject so the sun is behind them, or wait for a softer hour. The second mistake is mixing light colours — a portrait lit by a warm lamp on one side and a cool window on the other will look strange, because the two sides of the face will be different colours. Pick one main light and let it win.

A third mistake is forgetting the background. A beautiful subject against a bright window, a messy shelf, or a sunlit wall will lose the viewer's eye to the bright or busy background. Before you shoot, look past your subject and ask what else is in the frame. One step to the left, a plainer wall, or a lower angle can turn a cluttered background into a clean one.

The last mistake is rushing golden hour. People arrive at sunset, take three photos, and miss the best light because they were still setting up. Arrive early, set up in the ordinary light, and be ready when the good light comes. Light rewards the patient: the photographer who waits ten minutes for a cloud to move, or who returns to the same place at a better hour, gets the photo the hurried photographer misses.

Questions people ask

What is the best time of day for outdoor photos?

The hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset give the warmest, softest light, with long shadows and glowing skin. Mid-morning and late afternoon are also good. Midday sun is hard and overhead, so on a bright midday, move into open shade instead of shooting in direct sun.

Do I need special equipment for window light?

No. A large window and a willing subject are enough. A thin white curtain softens direct sun, and a sheet of white card can bounce light back into the shadow side of a face. That is the whole kit for most window-light portraits.

How long does golden hour last?

It depends on where you live and the season, but the best warm light often lasts twenty to forty minutes around sunrise or sunset. Arrive at least an hour early so you are set up and ready when the light turns, rather than still unpacking.

What is open shade?

Open shade is the shade of a building, tree, or roof on a bright day, with open sky above. The light there is soft, even, and slightly cool, with no harsh shadows and no squinting. It is one of the easiest lights for portraits on a sunny day.

Can I take good photos on a cloudy day?

Yes, often better ones. A cloudy sky is a giant soft light: shadows soften, colours look richer, and faces photograph beautifully without squinting. Cloud is a reason to change your plan, not to cancel the shoot.

What should I write on the planner sheet?

One row per idea: the time, the place, the light you expect, the subject and the setup, and what to bring. Start with the light you want, then pick the time and place that give it. Five calm rows beat twenty rushed ones.
