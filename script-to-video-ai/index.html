Writing a script that films well

A script that films well is a script of short sentences. Each sentence should hold one visual: one thing happening, in one place, that a viewer can picture. A sentence about a baker opening an oven films well. A sentence that packs a life story, a location, and a thought into one breath does not — it holds three ideas and no single picture.

Write the way people talk. Plain words, short lines, one idea per line. Read the script aloud: if you run out of breath, the sentence is too long. If you stumble, the line needs simpler words. A script that reads well out loud will narrate well, whether you speak it yourself or use a spoken track.

Put one visual per line, and the splitter below will do the rest. Each line becomes a scene, each scene gets a visual prompt and a narration line, and the finished list is your shot plan.

Scene splitting rules

A scene is one idea, one visual, and one line of narration. The splitter follows three rules. First, one sentence becomes one scene, because a sentence usually holds one idea. Second, a very short sentence joins the next one, so a scene is never a fragment. Third, a scene lasts 5 to 15 seconds on screen, because that is what free generators make.

When a sentence holds two ideas, split it yourself before you paste. A line about a shop opening and a baker waving is two scenes: the shop opening, and the baker waving. Splitting by hand takes a minute and saves credits, because a two-idea prompt often produces a confused clip.

The splitter handles up to 12 scenes at a time. For a longer script, paste it in parts and run each part through separately. Twelve scenes at about 10 seconds each is roughly two minutes of video, which covers most short videos in one pass.

Narration: your voice or a spoken track

Your own voice is the simplest narration, and it costs nothing. Record in a quiet room, speak slowly, and read one scene at a time. Small pauses between scenes make the edit easier, and a mistake costs only a re-read of one line.

Free text-to-speech tools are the alternative when you would rather not record. Paste the narration lines, pick a clear voice, and listen to the whole track once before you use it. Short sentences help any voice, and a word the voice misreads is worth spelling out.

Typed captions are the third option, and they work even with no voice at all. Many viewers watch muted, so captions carry the message when sound cannot. CapCut adds captions for free, and a captioned video works for viewers in a noisy room, a quiet office, or a second language.

Assembling scenes in CapCut

Once you have the clips, assembly is the easy part. CapCut is free, and it handles the whole finish: put the clips in scene order, trim the slow frames at the start and end of each clip, and lay the narration or captions over the top.

Keep the cuts clean. Each scene should start on its visual and end before the next idea begins. A one-second overlap of sound between scenes smooths the joins, and a simple caption style keeps the text readable on a phone screen.

Export in 9:16 for TikTok, Reels, and Shorts, or 16:9 for a wide screen. Watch the finished video once on your phone before you post it: if a scene drags, trim it; if a join jars, swap the two clips around.

Common mistakes that waste credits

The most expensive mistake is a long sentence. A sentence with three ideas produces a confused clip, and the credits are gone. Split the sentence first, then generate. The splitter does this for you, but only if the sentences you paste hold one idea each.

The second mistake is re-running the same prompt. A failed clip rarely fixes itself on a second run with the same words. Change one thing — the camera move, the action, or the light — and the next try has a real chance. The same goes for a warped face: shorten the prompt before you spend more credits.

The third mistake is generating before you plan. Write the script, split it into scenes, and read the scene list once before you generate anything. Ten minutes of planning saves more credits than any trick, because every clip you generate is one you already know you need.

Questions people ask

Does this page turn my script into a video?

No. This page splits your script into scenes and writes a visual prompt and a narration line for each scene. You generate the clips in a video tool and stitch them together in CapCut.

How long should each scene be?

One idea, and about 5 to 15 seconds of video. If a scene needs two ideas, split it into two scenes.

How many scenes is a 60-second video?

About six, at roughly 10 seconds a scene. A 30-second video needs about three scenes, and the splitter handles up to 12 scenes at a time.

Can I use my own voice for narration?

Yes, and it is often the best choice — your own voice sounds natural and costs nothing. Free text-to-speech tools are an option too, and typed captions work for viewers who watch muted.

What if my script is very long?

Paste it in parts. The splitter handles up to 12 scenes at a time, so split a long script into sections and run each one through separately.

Which free tools make the clips?

Flow gives 50 free credits a day, Kling gives free daily credits with a watermark on free exports, and PixVerse gives 60 starter credits plus 30 a day with free clips up to 540p. Finish the stitched video in CapCut, which is free.
