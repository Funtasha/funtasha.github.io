Energy Tariff Comparison Guide

An energy tariff is the deal you agree with your supplier: the price for each unit of power, any standing charge, and the terms that go with them. What this is and who needs it

An energy tariff is the price plan you agree with your energy supplier. It sets the price of each unit of electricity or gas you use, any standing charge you pay every month, and the terms, such as how long the price is fixed and what you pay if you leave early. Your bill is the tariff applied to the energy you actually used. This guide is for anyone who pays an energy bill and wants to pay less for the same power: households, renters, small business owners and anyone who has looked at a bill and wondered what it all means.

Most people stay on the same tariff for years, often the default plan their supplier put them on when a fixed deal ended. Default plans are rarely the cheapest, and the gap between the cheapest deal and the default can be large over a year. Switching is usually free and takes little effort, but it is hard to compare plans fairly without knowing how tariffs work. Suppliers do not make comparison easy: unit prices, standing charges, discounts and fees are presented in different ways, and the cheapest headline price is not always the cheapest total.

This guide explains how tariffs work, how to read a bill, how to compare two plans fairly, and the tricks to watch for in the fine print. It shows no live tariffs and quotes no price as fact, because prices change often and differ by area. Instead it teaches you a method you can use with any offers you find, today or next year, wherever you live.

How it works, step by step

Start with your own usage, because a tariff is only cheap or dear for a particular amount of energy. Find a recent bill, or a full year of bills, and note how many units of electricity and gas you used. If you have a smart meter, your supplier or your meter display can show your yearly usage. Without a real usage figure, any comparison is a guess.

Next, understand the two parts of every tariff. The unit price is what you pay for each unit of energy you use: use more, pay more. The standing charge is a fixed amount you pay every day or every month whether you use energy or not, covering the cost of the wires, the meter and the service. A plan with a low unit price and a high standing charge suits a heavy user; a plan with a high unit price and a low standing charge suits a light user. That is why the same plan can be cheap for one household and dear for the next.

Now compare the total yearly cost, not just the unit price. For each plan, add twelve months of standing charge to the unit price multiplied by your yearly usage, and do the same for gas if the plan covers it. Use the same usage figure for every plan, so the comparison is fair. The table below shows the method with made-up numbers, so you can see how it works before you try it with real offers.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Comparing tariffs costs nothing but a little time. A careful comparison takes under an hour once you have your usage figures, and the saving can last a whole year. Switching supplier is usually free, and your power stays on throughout: the change happens behind the scenes, and no engineer needs to visit. The main cost to watch for is the exit fee on your current plan, which can wipe out the saving if you leave a fixed term early. Check it before you switch.

Before you start, gather three things. First, your yearly usage for electricity and gas, from a bill or a smart meter. Second, your current tariff details: the unit price, the standing charge, the end date of any fixed term, and the exit fee. Third, a note of how you pay, because some suppliers charge more for some payment methods and less for others. With those three in hand, you can compare any offer fairly.

Time your switch well. The best time is usually just before a fixed term ends, when no exit fee applies. If your fixed term has already ended, you are probably on a default plan and can switch at any time without a fee. Avoid switching in the middle of a fixed term unless the saving clearly beats the exit fee. If you have recently changed how much energy you use, for example by buying an electric car or adding solar panels, re-check your usage before you compare, because old figures will mislead you.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is comparing only the unit price. A plan with a low unit price and a high standing charge can cost more over a year than a plan with a higher unit price and no standing charge, especially for a light user. Always compare the total yearly cost, using your own usage figures. The second mistake is comparing plans with different usage figures, such as one supplier's estimate against another's. Use one usage figure, your own, for every plan.

The third mistake is ignoring the fine print. Some plans offer a discount that ends after a few months, so the price jumps. Some charge an exit fee that swallows the saving. Some charge extra for a paper bill, for late payment, or for paying by a method the supplier dislikes. Read the full terms before you sign, and ask the supplier to confirm any fee you do not understand. The fourth mistake is switching to a plan that does not fit how you use energy: a plan with cheap night-time power only saves money if you actually use power at night.

The fifth mistake is forgetting what happens when a fixed term ends. Many fixed plans move you onto a default plan at the end of the term, and the default is rarely cheap. Put the end date in your calendar and compare again a month before it arrives. The sixth mistake is assuming that switching is risky or complicated. It is not: your power stays on, the change is handled between suppliers, and you can switch back if you are unhappy. The real risk is staying on an expensive default plan year after year.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this list every time you compare energy plans. It keeps the comparison honest and complete.

  • Find your real yearly usage for electricity and gas, from a bill or a smart meter.
  • Note your current unit price, standing charge, fixed-term end date and exit fee.
  • Compare the total yearly cost of each plan, using the same usage figure for all of them.
  • Check the standing charge as well as the unit price: light users pay more attention to the standing charge.
  • Read the fine print for exit fees, discounts that end, and charges for how you pay.
  • Check what happens when a fixed term ends, and put the end date in your calendar.
  • Make sure the plan fits when you use energy, not just how much you use.
  • Check the supplier's reputation for billing and customer service, not just the price.
  • Confirm the new deal in writing before you switch, including the price and the terms.
  • Compare again a month before any fixed term ends, and at least once a year.

If a plan fails the checklist on price but passes on everything else, it may still be the right choice: a slightly dearer plan from a reliable supplier beats a cheap plan from one that bills wrongly. Price matters, but it is not the only thing that matters.

Tips from real practice

Keep your bills, or at least a note of your yearly usage, in one place. A comparison is only as good as the usage figure behind it, and a guessed figure produces a guessed answer. If you have a smart meter, check your usage every few months: it changes with the seasons, with new appliances, and with changes in who lives at home. A comparison based on last year's usage is better than one based on a guess, but this year's usage is better still.

Use less energy as well as cheaper energy. No tariff beats using less: turning down the heating by a degree, fixing draughts, switching off lights and choosing efficient appliances all cut the bill whatever the tariff. The cheapest unit of energy is the one you never use. Compare tariffs once a year, and look for waste in your home just as often.

Finally, help someone else compare. Many people stay on expensive default plans because the comparison looks complicated, especially older relatives and busy neighbours. Walk them through the method in this guide with their own bill, and you may save them more in an hour than any gift you could buy. Energy is a household cost everyone shares, and the knowledge of how to compare is worth passing on.

Questions people ask

What is an energy tariff?

An energy tariff is the price plan you agree with your energy supplier. It sets the price of each unit of electricity or gas you use, any standing charge you pay every month, and the terms, such as how long the price is fixed and what you pay if you leave early. Your bill is the tariff applied to the energy you actually used.

What is the difference between a fixed and a variable tariff?

On a fixed tariff, the unit price and standing charge stay the same for a set period, usually a year or two, so your bills are easier to predict. On a variable tariff, the price can go up or down when the supplier changes it, usually with notice. Fixed plans give certainty; variable plans can be cheaper when prices fall and dearer when they rise.

How do I compare two energy plans fairly?

Compare the total yearly cost, not just the unit price: add the standing charge for twelve months to the unit price multiplied by your yearly usage. Use the same usage figure for both plans, taken from a real bill or a full year of meter readings. Check the small print too: exit fees, discounts that end, and what happens when a fixed term runs out.

What hidden fees should I look for?

Look for exit fees for leaving a fixed plan early, late payment charges, paper bill fees, and charges for paying by a method the supplier dislikes. Some plans offer a discount that ends after a few months, so the price jumps. Read the full terms before you sign, and ask the supplier to confirm any fee you do not understand.

When should I switch energy plans?

Switch when your fixed term is ending, when your bill has risen and a better deal is available, or when your usage has changed, such as after buying an electric car or adding solar panels. Avoid switching in the middle of a fixed term if the exit fee wipes out the saving. Always check the new deal against your real usage before you move.

Will switching save me money?

Switching can save money, but it is not guaranteed: the saving depends on your usage, your current tariff and what is on offer in your area. A plan that is cheap for a heavy user can be dear for a light one, because of the standing charge. Work out the yearly total for each plan with your own usage figures before you decide.
