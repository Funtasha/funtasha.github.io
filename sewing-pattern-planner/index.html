Sewing Pattern Planner

A first garment goes well when the planning comes first. This page walks you through choosing a beginner pattern, taking your measurements, picking a fabric you can handle, cutting in the right order, and fitting as you sew.

No pattern guarantees a perfect fit on the first try. This planner helps you prepare and adjust, so always make a test version in cheap fabric before cutting your good fabric.

Choosing your first pattern

The best first pattern is a simple one: few pieces, no fiddly closures, and a forgiving fit. A straight skirt, a loose top, a simple tote-style dress, or elastic-waist trousers are all good first garments, because they teach the main skills — cutting, seaming, hemming — without asking for advanced techniques on day one. Look for the words beginner or easy on the pattern envelope, and check how many pieces the pattern has: fewer pieces means a faster, calmer first project.

Avoid patterns with fitted bodices, set-in sleeves, collars, buttonholes, or zips for your very first garment. Each of those is a skill of its own, and learning them all at once is how beginners end up frustrated. You will learn them soon enough, one at a time, on later projects. Your first garment should finish, not impress — the pride comes from wearing something you made, however simple it is.

Printed patterns and digital patterns both work. Printed patterns come with tissue pieces you cut out; digital patterns print at home on paper sheets you tape together. Digital patterns are often cheaper and come with clear photo instructions, while printed patterns are easier to handle if you dislike taping. Either way, read the whole instruction sheet once before you cut anything. Knowing the shape of the whole project makes every step easier.

Taking your measurements

Pattern sizes are not shop sizes. A person who wears a size 12 in a shop might cut a size 16 in a pattern, and that is normal — pattern sizing follows body measurements, not shop labels. So forget the number on your clothes and measure your body instead. You need a soft tape measure, a mirror, and someone to help if you can find one, though most measurements can be taken alone with care.

Measure over light clothing, standing straight and relaxed, with the tape snug but not tight. The key measurements for most garments are bust, waist, and hips: bust around the fullest part, waist at the narrowest point (usually just above the belly button), and hips around the fullest part. For tops, add the shoulder width and the length from shoulder to waist. For trousers, add the inside leg and the waist-to-hip length. Write every number down as you go, and measure twice — a wrong number here follows you through the whole project.

Then compare your numbers with the pattern's size chart, which is printed on the envelope. If your measurements fall across two sizes — a size 14 bust with a size 16 hip, for example — that is normal too. Most sewers cut one size at the top and blend to another size at the bottom, drawing a smooth line between the two on the pattern pieces. Patterns are made to be adjusted; they are a starting shape, not a fixed rule.

Reading the pattern envelope

The pattern envelope is your shopping list and your map. The front shows the finished garment in its different versions, usually labelled A, B, C. The back lists what you need: the fabric amounts for each size, the notions (thread, elastic, buttons, zips, interfacing), and the suggested fabrics. Read the back before you buy anything, and take the envelope with you to the fabric shop — it answers most of the shop assistant's questions for you.

Fabric amounts are given for two fabric widths, usually narrow and wide. Check the width of the fabric you plan to buy and use the matching column, or you may come home short. The amounts include a little extra for cutting, but not for mistakes, so if you are nervous, buy a little more than the chart says — an extra half metre is cheap insurance against a miscut piece.

The envelope also tells you the finished garment measurements, which are different from your body measurements. A top with a finished bust of 110 centimetres on a body of 100 centimetres has 10 centimetres of ease — the room that lets you move and breathe. Ease is part of the design: a loose top has more of it, a fitted dress has less. If you want a looser or closer fit, you change the ease, not your body size.

Choosing a fabric you can handle

For a first garment, choose a fabric that behaves: stable, not slippery, not stretchy, and easy to press. Medium-weight cotton, cotton poplin, linen, and light denim are all kind to beginners — they stay where you put them, they cut cleanly, and they press into crisp seams. Avoid silk, satin, chiffon, and very stretchy jersey for your first project: they slide under the needle, they show every wobble, and they punish small mistakes.

Check the pattern's suggested fabrics and stay inside that list for your first garment. The pattern was designed and tested with those fabrics in mind, and the fit and the drape depend on them. A pattern made for a flowing fabric will look stiff in denim; a pattern made for a firm fabric will collapse in a soft jersey. Matching the fabric to the pattern is half the job.

Buy your fabric, then wash and dry it exactly as you will wash the finished garment, before you cut. Most fabrics shrink a little on the first wash, and a garment that fit perfectly before washing can come out too small after it. Pre-washing also removes the factory finish that can make new fabric feel stiff. Press the fabric after washing, fold it neatly, and only then lay out your pattern.

Cutting order, step by step

Cutting is the step beginners rush and regret. Slow down, clear a large flat surface, and lay the fabric out flat with no wrinkles. Fold it as the pattern's layout diagram shows — usually right sides together, with the fold on one edge — and pin the pattern pieces to the fabric, following the grain line printed on each piece. The grain line must run parallel to the fabric's straight edge; a piece cut off-grain will twist and hang wrong.

Cut the large pieces first, then the small ones, and cut with long, smooth strokes rather than short choppy ones. Sharp scissors matter more than expensive ones: fabric scissors should cut fabric only, never paper, or they go blunt fast. Mark the notches, dots, and other symbols as you go, with tailor's chalk or small snips into the seam allowance — these marks are the map that lines the pieces up when you sew.

Work through the pieces in the order the instructions list them, and keep each cut piece with its pattern piece until you need it. A tidy cutting table saves a confused sewing session: pieces in piles, pairs together, right sides marked. If you miscut a small piece, check whether the leftover fabric holds a spare — this is why buying a little extra fabric is wise.

Fitting as you sew

Fitting is not one step at the end; it is a habit through the whole project. Try the garment on at the stages the instructions suggest, usually after the main seams are sewn but before the hems and finishing. Stand in front of a mirror, move your arms, sit down, and ask the honest questions: does it pull anywhere, does it gape, does it hang straight, can you breathe and reach?

Most first-fit problems have simple fixes. A garment that pulls across the chest or hips needs more room — let out the side seams a little. A garment that gapes at the neck or waist needs the seam taken in, or a small fold of fabric pinned out and sewn as a shaped seam. A hem that hangs unevenly usually means the garment is sitting unevenly on your body, not that the hem is wrong — check the shoulders and waist first.

Pin the fix, try it on again, and only then sew it. Fitting is a loop of pin, try, adjust, and it is the part of sewing that turns a pattern into a garment that fits your body rather than a standard body. Nobody's first fit is perfect, and the fitting stage is where the garment becomes yours. Take your time with it.

Pressing and finishing

Pressing is the step that makes handmade look professional. Press every seam as you sew it — open or to one side, as the instructions say — with the iron set for your fabric, and use a pressing cloth on delicate fabrics. A pressed seam lies flat and looks neat; an unpressed seam looks homemade in the bad sense. Sewers joke that pressing is half of sewing, and the joke is mostly true.

Finishing the raw edges stops fraying and makes the inside of the garment as tidy as the outside. The simplest finish is a zigzag stitch along each raw edge, or trimming with pinking shears. If your machine has an overlock stitch, that works too. Finish the edges of each piece before you sew the seams, or finish the seam allowances together after sewing — either way, do not skip it, or the garment will fray in the wash.

The hem is the last big step. Try the garment on with the shoes you will wear with it, mark the hem length with a friend's help or a mirror, and press the hem up before you sew it. A hand-sewn hem is invisible and worth learning; a topstitched hem is faster and looks neat on casual garments. Press the finished garment once more, hang it up, and step back — you made that.

Your first-garment checklist

Before you start, run through this list. Pattern chosen and read through once. Measurements taken and written down, twice. Size chosen from the pattern chart, not the shop label. Fabric bought, washed, dried, and pressed. Notions bought: thread to match, needles, pins, elastic or buttons if the pattern needs them. Scissors sharp, machine threaded, iron hot, table clear.

During the project, keep this rhythm: cut slowly, sew the seams, press the seams, try it on, adjust, then finish. Do not skip the trying-on stages, and do not sew a hem before the fit is settled. If a step confuses you, read the instructions again, look for a photo of that step, and sew a practice seam on a scrap of your fabric before you sew the real one.

After you finish, write three lines in a notebook: what you made, what size you cut, and what you would change next time. That note is worth more than any pattern, because it turns one finished garment into the start of a skill. Your second garment will be faster, your third will fit better, and by the fifth you will be choosing patterns with confidence.

Questions people ask

What is the easiest first garment to sew?

A simple top, a straight skirt, or elastic-waist trousers. Pick a pattern labelled beginner or easy, with few pieces and no zips, buttonholes, or collars. The goal of a first garment is to finish it and wear it, not to master every technique at once.

How do I know which size to cut?

Measure your body with a soft tape measure and use the pattern's size chart, not your shop size. Pattern sizes run differently from shop sizes, so a size 12 shopper may cut a size 16 pattern. If your measurements span two sizes, cut one at the top and blend to the other at the bottom.

What fabric should a beginner choose?

A stable, non-stretchy fabric such as medium-weight cotton, cotton poplin, linen, or light denim. Stay inside the pattern's suggested fabrics list. Avoid slippery fabrics such as silk and satin, and very stretchy jersey, until you have a few garments behind you.

Do I need to wash fabric before cutting?

Yes. Wash and dry new fabric exactly as you will wash the finished garment, then press it. Most fabrics shrink a little on the first wash, and a garment cut from unwashed fabric can come out too small after its first wash.

What is a test version, and is it worth the time?

A test version is a rough copy of the garment sewn in cheap fabric, to check the fit before cutting your good fabric. It takes extra time, but it saves the far worse outcome of cutting expensive fabric and finding the fit is wrong. For a first garment, it is always worth it.

My garment does not fit — what do I fix first?

Try it on and find where it pulls or gapes. Pulling usually means more room is needed, so let out the side seams a little. Gaping usually means fabric to take in, pinned out as a shaped seam. Fix one thing, try it on again, and only then sew the change.
