Planning a route step by step

A good route is planned backwards from the destination, in small steps. Start with where you want to end up, and the latest time you want to arrive. Then work out the waypoints in between: the harbour entrance, the headland, the channel marker, the turning point, the entrance to the destination harbour. Each waypoint is a place where the course changes or a decision is made, and the route is the line that joins them. Short legs are easier to follow than long ones, and a route with too few waypoints is a guess, not a plan.

For each leg, ask three questions. How far is it, and how long will it take at the speed you expect? What hazards lie on or near it: shallow water, rocks, strong currents, busy shipping? And where is the alternative: if the weather turns or the wind dies, where do you go instead? A route with an alternative for every leg is a route you can sail with confidence. A route with no alternative is a route that depends on everything going right, and at sea, that is not a safe way to plan.

  • Start from the destination and the latest arrival time.
  • Mark a waypoint wherever the course changes or a decision is made.
  • For each leg, ask: how far, what hazards, and what is the alternative?
  • Short legs are easier to follow than long ones.

Waypoints and distances

A waypoint is a named position, given by its latitude and longitude. Latitude measures how far north or south of the equator a place is, and longitude measures how far east or west of the prime meridian. Together they fix a point on the Earth to within a few metres, which is why every chart, every navigation app and every harbour guide uses them. Enter them in decimal degrees, with a minus sign for south latitudes and west longitudes: a latitude of 51.5 is north, and a longitude of minus 0.12 is west.

The distance between two waypoints is worked out from their coordinates with the haversine formula, which measures the shortest path over the curved surface of the Earth. The planner shows each leg in nautical miles, which is the unit sailors use, and in kilometres. One nautical mile is 1.852 kilometres, and one knot is one nautical mile per hour, so a boat sailing at 5 knots covers 5 nautical miles in an hour. The time estimate divides the total distance by your chosen speed, and it is an estimate, not a promise: wind, tide and sea state all change the speed you actually make.

  • A waypoint is a named position, fixed by latitude and longitude.
  • Enter coordinates in decimal degrees, with minus for south and west.
  • Distances use the haversine formula, over the curved Earth.
  • One nautical mile is 1.852 kilometres; one knot is one nautical mile per hour.
  • Time estimates are estimates: wind, tide and sea state change your speed.

Weather and tides

Weather and tides decide whether a route is safe to sail, and this page cannot tell you what they are, because they change by the hour and by the place. Check the weather from an official forecast for your sailing area before you go, and check it again on the day, because a forecast from yesterday is not a forecast for today. Look for the wind strength and direction, the sea state, visibility, and any warnings in force. If any of those is beyond what you and your boat can handle, do not go.

Tides matter just as much. The tide decides the depth of water over shallow ground, the strength of the current in a channel, and whether a harbour entrance is usable at a given time. Check the official tide tables for your area, and work out the depth at each shallow point on your route at the time you will be there. A route that is safe at high water can be dangerous at low water, and a harbour that is easy to enter at one time can be impossible an hour later. Plan your departure time around the tide, not around convenience.

  • Check the official forecast for your area, and check it again on the day.
  • Look at wind, sea state, visibility and any warnings in force.
  • Check the official tide tables for every shallow point on the route.
  • Plan departure around the tide, not around convenience.
  • If the weather or the tide is beyond you, do not go.

The passage plan

A passage plan is the written version of your route: the waypoints, the distances, the times, the hazards, the alternatives, and the checks to make along the way. Write it down before you leave, and keep it where you can read it while sailing. The copy button on the planner gives you the route part of the plan as text; add the weather, the tides and the safety notes by hand, because those are the parts only you can fill in for your boat and your crew.

Tell someone ashore your plan before you go: where you are going, the route, and when you expect to arrive. That person is your safety net: if you do not arrive when expected, they know where to look. This is called a float plan, and it costs nothing and can save a life. Leave a copy of the plan with them, and agree what they should do, and when, if they do not hear from you. A plan nobody knows about helps nobody if something goes wrong.

  • Write the plan down: waypoints, distances, times, hazards, alternatives.
  • Add the weather, tides and safety notes by hand.
  • Tell someone ashore where you are going and when you expect to arrive.
  • Agree what they should do, and when, if they do not hear from you.

Safety checks before you go

Before you leave, check the things that keep people safe. Lifejackets for everyone aboard, in sizes that fit, and worn when the weather or the water calls for it. A working radio, and someone aboard who knows how to use it. Flares, a first aid kit, and a means of calling for help that works where you are sailing. Fuel enough for the journey plus a margin, because a wind that dies leaves you motoring, and a motor with no fuel is ballast.

Check the boat too: the engine starts, the steering works, the bilge is dry, the lights work, and the anchor and its line are ready to use. Check the crew: everyone knows where the safety gear is, everyone knows what to do if someone falls overboard, and nobody is sailing beyond their ability. And check yourself: if you are tired, unsure or rushed, the safest decision is to stay in harbour. The sea will still be there tomorrow.

  • Lifejackets for everyone, in sizes that fit.
  • A working radio, flares, a first aid kit, and a way to call for help.
  • Fuel for the journey plus a margin.
  • Engine, steering, bilge, lights, anchor: all checked before leaving.
  • Everyone aboard knows where the safety gear is.
  • If you are tired, unsure or rushed, stay in harbour.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is no alternative harbour. A route with one destination and no backup leaves you nowhere to go when the weather turns, the wind dies, or the harbour entrance closes. Every route should have an alternative for every leg, chosen before you leave, not in a hurry when things go wrong. The second mistake is no fuel margin: planning fuel for the exact distance, and then motoring into a headwind with an empty tank.

The third mistake is trusting a forecast from days ago, or a tide table for the wrong place. The fourth is entering coordinates in the wrong format, degrees and minutes typed as decimal degrees, which puts a waypoint miles from where it should be: always check that each waypoint sits where you expect it on the chart. And the fifth is sailing to a timetable instead of to the weather: a plan is a guide, and the sea does not read it. If the weather says no, the answer is no.

  • Always have an alternative harbour for every leg.
  • Carry fuel for the journey plus a margin.
  • Check the forecast and the tide tables for the right place and day.
  • Enter coordinates in decimal degrees, and check each waypoint on the chart.
  • Sail to the weather, not to the timetable.
