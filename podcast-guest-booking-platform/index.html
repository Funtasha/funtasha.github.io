How to use this guide

This guide is for podcasters who want better guests — not more guests, better ones. It covers where to find them, how to invite them, how to prepare an interview that respects their time, and how to follow up so they say yes again. The templates in the third and sixth sections are words you can copy and adapt; everything else is method you can use for every guest, every episode.

Be clear about what this page is and is not. It is a guide with outreach templates. It is not a booking platform: it cannot find guests for you, send invitations, or hold a calendar. The work of booking is yours — the emails, the messages, the scheduling — and this guide makes that work easier, not unnecessary. A guest booked well is a guest who arrives prepared, relaxed, and ready to talk.

Finding guests

The best guests are usually one step away from where you already look. Start with your own field: the people whose work you admire, the authors of the books and articles you cite, the speakers at events you attend, and the practitioners your listeners already mention. A guest your audience already respects arrives with trust built in, and trust makes a better interview.

Then widen the net. Ask past guests who they would recommend — a guest who enjoyed the show will often name two or three people you would never have found alone. Look at who writes, speaks, or teaches in your field, and look for people with a story rather than a title: the person who did the work is usually a better guest than the person who manages the people who did it.

Keep a list. Every time you hear a name worth inviting, write it down with one line about why — the book, the project, the story. A list of fifty possible guests, kept over months, is worth more than a frantic search the week before recording. Book ahead, too: the best guests are busy, and a request sent six weeks before the episode has a far better chance than one sent in six days.

The invitation that gets a yes

A good invitation is short, specific, and about the guest — not about you. It says what your show is in one line, why this guest in particular, what you would like to talk about, and how much of their time it takes. It does not flatter, it does not beg, and it does not bury the ask in three paragraphs of background. Busy people say yes to invitations they can understand in thirty seconds.

Here is a template you can copy and adapt. Change the bracketed parts to fit your show and your guest, keep it short, and send it as your own message — this page cannot send it for you: 'Hello [name], I host [show], a podcast about [subject]. I would love to invite you on to talk about [specific topic — their book, project, or story]. The recording takes about [45 minutes], by [phone or video call], at a time that suits you. Would you be open to it? Thank you for considering it — [your name].'

The details matter. Name the specific topic, not just the guest's field — 'your work on night-shift nursing' beats 'healthcare'. Say how long it takes, and how it happens. Offer times, or ask when suits them, and mean it. And if the guest says no, thank them anyway and ask whether you may try again for a future episode — a polite no today is often a yes in six months.

Preparing the interview

Preparation is the difference between an interview and a chat. Before the recording, listen to the guest's work, read their book or their articles, and watch a talk if one exists. You do not need to be an expert in their field, but you need to know enough to ask questions nobody else has asked them — a guest who has answered the same five questions on every show will give you the same five answers.

Then write your questions. Aim for eight to ten questions for a forty-five-minute episode, in an order that tells a story: where the guest started, what changed, what they learned, and what comes next. Write the questions as prompts, not as a script — the best moments in an interview come from following up on an answer, and a script leaves no room for that.

Finally, prepare the practical side: test the recording setup, check the sound, and have a backup plan if the connection fails. Tell the guest how the recording works, when it starts, and how long it runs. A guest who knows the plan relaxes; a guest who does not spends the first ten minutes wondering what is happening.

The pre-interview checklist

A short pre-interview call — fifteen minutes, a few days before the recording — saves more episodes than any other habit. It is not a rehearsal; it is a conversation. You learn how the guest talks, what they care about, and which questions light them up, and the guest learns that you have done your homework and that the show is worth their time.

Use the call to agree the shape of the episode: the topics you will cover, anything the guest would rather not discuss, and how the guest would like to be introduced. Agree the practical details too — the date, the time, the link, and what happens if something goes wrong. A guest who has had a pre-interview call arrives at the recording like a collaborator, not a stranger.

  • Listen to or read the guest's work before the call
  • Agree the topics, and anything the guest would rather skip
  • Agree how the guest would like to be introduced
  • Confirm the date, time, recording link, and backup plan
  • Send the questions in advance — see the next section
  • Test the recording setup, and test it again on the day

After the recording: the thank-you that brings them back

The interview ends when the recording stops; the relationship does not. Thank the guest the same day, in a message that says what you enjoyed — name a moment from the interview, not just the fact of it. A thank-you that mentions something specific tells the guest you were listening, and a guest who feels heard says yes next time.

Here is a template you can copy and adapt: 'Thank you for joining me on [show] today — I really enjoyed our conversation, especially [one specific moment]. The episode will be out on [date], and I will send you the link as soon as it is live. Thank you again for your time — [your name].' Then do what you promised: send the link on the day, tag the guest if they like that, and share the episode where their audience will see it.

Keep in touch after the episode. Send the guest the listener response, mention them when a related episode comes out, and invite them back when the time is right — a second episode with a guest your audience already knows is often better than the first. Guests who feel valued become advocates for your show, and advocates bring you the next guest.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is inviting a guest without listening to their work. The invitation is generic, the questions are generic, and the guest knows within five minutes that the host has done no homework. A guest who feels like a number gives a numbered performance. Listen first, invite second.

The second mistake is no questions sent in advance. Some hosts fear that prepared guests sound rehearsed, but the opposite is true: a guest who has seen the questions arrives with stories ready, and a guest who has not spends the interview thinking instead of talking. Send the questions a few days before, with a note that they are a guide, not a script.

The third mistake is no follow-up after the recording. The guest gave you an hour of their time and never heard from you again — no thank-you, no link, no mention. That guest will not come back, and will not recommend your show. A thank-you takes five minutes and buys you a relationship.

Questions people ask

How do I find podcast guests?

Start with your own field: the authors, speakers and practitioners your listeners already respect. Ask past guests who they would recommend — a guest who enjoyed the show will often name two or three people. Keep a running list of names with one line about why each is worth inviting, and book six weeks ahead: the best guests are busy.

What should my invitation say?

Keep it short, specific, and about the guest. Say what your show is in one line, why this guest in particular, what you would like to talk about, and how long it takes. Name the specific topic — their book, project or story — not just their field. This page includes a template you can copy and adapt into your own message.

How do I prepare for an interview?

Listen to the guest's work, read their book or articles, and watch a talk if one exists. Write eight to ten questions in an order that tells a story, as prompts rather than a script, so you can follow up on answers. Test the recording setup, and tell the guest how it works, when it starts, and how long it runs.

Should I send questions in advance?

Yes — send the questions a few days before, with a note that they are a guide, not a script. A guest who has seen the questions arrives with stories ready; a guest who has not spends the interview thinking instead of talking. Prepared guests do not sound rehearsed — they sound confident.

What if a guest cancels?

Stay calm and gracious: thank the guest, ask whether another date works, and have a backup episode ready — a solo episode, a past guest, or a topic you can record alone. Never pressure a guest who cancels, and never complain about it publicly. A guest who cancels politely and is treated well often says yes next time.

How do I thank a guest?

Thank the guest the same day, in a message that names a specific moment from the interview — that tells them you were listening. Send the episode link on the day it goes live, share it where their audience will see it, and keep in touch afterwards. A guest who feels valued says yes to a second episode and recommends your show to others.
