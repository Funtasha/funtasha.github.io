What this page does — and how the audio works


This page trains your ears with short spoken passages from everyday life: a shoe shop, a train station, a meeting at work, a phone call, a cafe, and a neighbour's visit. Each passage is followed by three questions with instant answers, and a transcript you can check after you have answered.


The audio uses your device's built-in voice — the same voice your phone uses to read messages aloud. It speaks a little slower than normal conversation, which is deliberate: at full speed, new listeners hear a stream of sound and catch almost nothing. The slower pace lets you hear where one word ends and the next begins.


If your device has no speech voice, the listen button will tell you so plainly instead of failing silently. You can still read each passage as a transcript, answer the questions, and practise by reading the passage aloud to yourself. The listening skill itself, though, needs sound — the section below names free sources of real spoken English for exactly that.


How to listen: main idea first, details second


Most learners listen the wrong way round: they try to catch every word from the first second, miss one, panic, and lose the rest. Good listeners do the opposite. On the first listen, ignore the details and ask only one question: what is this about? A shop, a train, a meeting? That main idea is the frame everything else hangs on.


On the second listen, go after the details: names, numbers, times, places. These are the facts the questions ask about, and they are easier to catch once you know the frame. On the third listen, check what you still missed — and only then look at the transcript.


This order matters because real conversation works the same way. Nobody catches every word in a noisy cafe or a fast phone call. Skilled listeners catch the main idea, fill in the details from context, and ask about the rest. You are training a real-life skill, not preparing for a perfect test.


One more habit helps: listen with your eyes closed. Without the screen to distract you, your brain gives the sound its full attention, and the words stand out more clearly. It feels odd the first time, but many learners find that closing their eyes is worth more than listening twice with them open. Try it on the next passage and compare.


Worked example: one passage, done slowly


Take the shoe shop passage. First listen: what is happening? A customer is buying shoes. That is the main idea — enough for now. Do not worry about the size or the price yet.


Second listen: the details. The customer wants black shoes, size eight. The eight is too tight, so she tries a nine. The price is mentioned, and she pays by card. If you caught two of those three facts, you are doing well; if you caught one, listen once more.


Third, answer the three questions and read the explanations, right or wrong. Then, and only then, open the transcript. Read it while the passage plays once more, and notice the words you missed — usually small linking words like "to", "of", and "and", which native speakers swallow. Those small words are the last thing to come, and they matter least. The main idea and the facts matter most.


The 6 passages and what each one teaches


Each passage practises a different kind of listening. The shoe shop teaches numbers and sizes — the facts you must get exactly right. The train station teaches times and platforms, where one wrong number sends you to the wrong place. The meeting at work teaches dates and names, the details of a changing plan.


The phone call is the hardest of the six, on purpose: on the phone there is no face to read, no gesture to help, and the sound is thinner. If you can follow a phone call, you can follow almost anything. The cafe teaches fast, friendly service language — ordering, choosing, paying — and the neighbour's visit teaches the slower, warmer language of invitations and small talk.


Work through all six, but repeat the ones that trouble you. A passage you half-understood is worth more than a new passage you rush through. Listen to the same passage on three different days and you will feel it getting easier — that feeling is your ear learning.


Notice, too, how much you can understand from a few key words. In the station passage, "ten forty-five", "platform three", and "forty minutes" carry almost the whole meaning; the rest is polite padding. Real listening is mostly this: catching the load-bearing words and letting the padding pass. The questions on this page train exactly that — they ask about the words that matter, not the ones that do not.


Where to hear real voices


A device voice is a good teacher, but it is not a real person. Real people mumble, pause, laugh, interrupt each other, and speak with accents from every corner of the world. At some point you need to hear them — and the good news is that excellent free sources exist.


VOA Learning English publishes short news stories read slowly, with transcripts, made for learners. It is one of the best free listening sources in the world, and the card below takes you to it. BBC Learning English also has listening programmes for learners at every level, and is well worth a visit.


Use them the same way you use this page: listen for the main idea first, then the details, then check the transcript. The jump from a device voice to a real voice feels hard for a week and then suddenly normal. That week is the price of real listening — pay it, and the skill is yours.

Common mistakes (and how to fix them)


The most common mistake is trying to catch every word. It feels like the right goal, but it makes listening worse: the moment you miss a word, your brain stops listening to the next one while it worries about the last. Train yourself to let words go. If you understood the main idea and the key facts, you understood the passage.


The second mistake is never checking the transcript. Learners often answer the questions, get two out of three, and move on — without finding out what they missed. The transcript is where the learning happens: it shows you exactly which words your ear skipped, and reading it while listening again connects the sound to the word for next time.


The third mistake is listening at the wrong level. A passage where you understand nothing teaches nothing except frustration, and a passage where you understand everything teaches nothing either. The right level is about seventy percent understood on the first listen — hard enough to stretch you, easy enough to follow. All six passages here are written at that level for an intermediate learner.


What this page cannot do


This page cannot give you real voices, real accents, or real conversation. A device voice speaks clearly, evenly, and without emotion; real people do none of those things. Use this page to build the habit and the confidence, then move to real voices as soon as you can — the sources above are the bridge.


It also cannot make you fluent on its own. Listening is one skill of four: you also need speaking, reading, and writing, and each one feeds the others. Listening practice makes speaking easier, because the words you have heard are the words you can say — but only speaking practice teaches your mouth to say them.


Finally, this page cannot tell you which words you personally struggle with. Only you can find that out, by noticing which questions you miss and which words you skip in the transcript. Keep a small list of those words, and listen for them in the world. That list is your real curriculum.


One last piece of advice: be patient with your ears. Listening improves in jumps, not in a straight line — weeks of feeling stuck, then suddenly a passage that was hard is easy. The stuck weeks are not wasted; they are when your brain is quietly building the patterns. Keep listening through them, and the jump will come.


Questions people ask


How do I improve my English listening?
Listen a little every day to short passages at your level, with questions to answer afterwards. Listen for the main idea first, then for details, then check the transcript to see what you missed. Daily short practice beats occasional long sessions, because your ear learns from repetition.


Should I read the transcript first?
No — listen first, even if you understand only part of it. Reading first turns a listening exercise into a reading exercise, and your ear learns nothing. The transcript is for checking afterwards: it shows you which words you missed and connects the sound to the written word.


Why do I understand reading but not listening?
Reading gives you time: you can pause, re-read, and look words up. Listening is real time, and spoken words run together — sounds disappear, link up, and change at word boundaries. The gap is normal and it closes with listening practice, not with more reading.


How long should I practise?
Ten to fifteen minutes a day is enough to make steady progress. Listen to one passage, answer its questions, check the transcript, and stop. Longer sessions help only if you stay focused — twenty tired minutes teach less than ten alert ones.


What speed should I listen at?
Start at the page's slightly slowed pace, which lets you hear where words begin and end. Once a passage feels easy, move to normal-speed real voices — the free sources named on this page. Listening only at slow speed leaves you unprepared for real conversation, so raise the speed as you improve.


Can listening alone make me fluent?
No. Listening builds understanding and vocabulary, and it makes speaking easier, but fluency also needs speaking practice, reading, and writing. Think of listening as one leg of a table: necessary, but not enough on its own. Use this page alongside speaking practice with real people.
