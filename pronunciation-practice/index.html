What this page does — and how the audio works

This page helps you practise the English sounds that learners find hardest: the th sound, the difference between r and l, v and w, short and long vowels, and word stress. Each sound has a list of everyday words, a plain-words hint about how to make the sound, and a listen button that plays the word aloud.

The audio uses your device's built-in voice — the same voice your phone uses to read messages aloud. Nothing is recorded and nothing is uploaded; the sound is made on your own device. If your device has no speech voice, the listen buttons will tell you so plainly instead of failing silently. You can still use the whole page: the hints, the word lists, and the minimal-pair exercises all work without audio, and the guide below explains how to practise with a recording of your own voice.

The 5 sounds learners find hardest

The th sound does not exist in most languages. For "think", put your tongue gently between your teeth and blow soft air — no voice. For "mother", keep the tongue in the same place but switch your voice on. Many learners say "tink" or "sink" instead; the tongue position is the whole trick.

R and l confuse each other because both are made near the front of the mouth. For l, the tongue tip touches the ridge behind your top teeth. For r, the tongue pulls back and the lips round slightly — the tongue tip touches nothing. "Right" and "light" differ only in that one movement.

V and w are a similar pair. For v, your top teeth rest on your bottom lip and your voice hums. For w, the lips round like a small kiss and the teeth stay out of it. "Very" and "west" feel very different in the mouth once you slow them down. Short and long vowels change meaning too: "ship" and "sheep" differ only in how long the vowel lasts. And word stress — which part of the word is loudest — is what makes speech sound natural: TA-ble, to-DAY, im-POR-tant.

These five sounds cause most of the misunderstandings learners run into, which is why this page starts with them. You do not need to master all five at once. Pick the one that causes you the most trouble — the sound people ask you to repeat — and practise that one for a week before moving on. One sound, done well, improves your speech more than five sounds practised once each.

How to practise (listen, repeat slowly, record yourself)

Good practice has three steps. First, listen to the word two or three times without speaking. Notice the sound, not the spelling — English spelling is a poor guide to sound, and reading the letters in your head will pull you back to your old habits.

Second, repeat the word slowly, out loud, exaggerating the new sound. Say "th-th-think" slowly, then at normal speed. Slow is not a beginner's crutch; it is how the mouth learns a new movement. Speed comes later, on its own, once the movement is familiar.

Third, record yourself on your phone and listen back. This is the step most learners skip, and it is the one that helps most — because the voice you hear in your head is not the voice other people hear. Compare your recording with the model sound on this page, notice one difference, and try again. Ten minutes of this beats an hour of silent reading.

A useful habit is to practise the same small set of words for several days in a row. New sounds feel strange at first, and the strangeness fades only with repetition. Saying "think" correctly once proves nothing; saying it correctly on the fifth day, without thinking about your tongue, means the movement is starting to stick. That is the real goal — not a perfect word once, but a natural word every time.

Worked examples: three words, done slowly

Take "think". First the th: tongue between the teeth, soft air, no voice. Then the vowel, short and relaxed. Then the k at the end, a small stop. Th-ink. Say it three times slowly, then at normal speed: think, think, think. If it sounds like "sink", your tongue is behind your teeth instead of between them.

Take "really". The r starts with the tongue pulled back and lips slightly rounded — "rrr". Then the long ee, stretched. Then the l, tongue tip up to the ridge. Then a soft y ending. Rrr-ee-lly. The two sounds that cause trouble, r and l, sit in one word, which is why "really" is such good practice.

Take "photography". Four parts: pho-TOG-ra-phy, with the loud part on the second one. English stress is not decoration — it is part of the word. Say it with the stress on the wrong part and even a perfect set of sounds will be hard to understand. Tap the table on the loud part until the rhythm feels natural.

Minimal pairs: small differences, big meaning

A minimal pair is two words that differ in exactly one sound: ship and sheep, fan and van, think and sink. They are the best practice tool in pronunciation, because they force your ear and your mouth to notice the one sound that matters.

The exercise below the word lists shows you a pair and asks a question about it — which word has the long vowel, which starts with the v sound, and so on. Answer, and you get instant feedback with a one-line explanation. The point is not the score; it is the noticing. Each correct answer means your ear caught a difference it used to miss.

Use the pairs in conversation too. When you learn a new word, ask yourself: is there a word that differs from it by one sound? If there is, practise the pair together. "Live" and "leave" are a classic trap — "I live here" and "I leave here" mean opposite things, and only the vowel tells them apart.

Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

The most common mistake is reading the spelling instead of the sound. English spelling is full of history: "though", "through", and "thought" share letters and share almost no sounds. When you practise, listen first and look at the letters second — or cover the word and listen only.

The second mistake is rushing. Learners often race through a word to get it over with, which hides the difficult sound instead of practising it. Slow down on purpose. A slow, clear "think" teaches your mouth more than ten fast, mumbled ones.

The third mistake is practising silently. Pronunciation is a physical skill, like dancing — reading about it does not train the muscles. If you cannot speak aloud where you are, whisper the words; whispering still moves the mouth through the right shapes, and it is far better than nothing.

What this page cannot do

This page cannot hear you, so it cannot tell you whether your pronunciation is right. No page like this can — real feedback needs a human ear, a teacher, or a conversation partner. What this page gives you is the model sound, the physical hint, and exercises that train your ear.

It also cannot make you fluent on its own. Pronunciation improves fastest when you speak with real people: a language partner, a class, or even a patient friend. Use this page to prepare for those conversations — practise the sounds first, then use them where they matter.

Finally, this page teaches one clear model of English, close to the standard accents used in teaching. Real English has many accents, and all of them are correct. Your goal is not to sound like one particular country; it is to be understood easily, by anyone, anywhere.

If you want more listening practice after this page, the English Listening Practice page on this site has short spoken passages with questions, and free listening sources with real human voices are named there too. Hearing real people — not just a device voice — is the next step once the sounds on this page feel comfortable.

Questions people ask

Why is English pronunciation hard?

Because English spelling is a poor guide to sound — the same letters sound different in different words — and because English has sounds, like th, that many languages do not have. It is hard for everyone at first, and it gets easier with listening and slow, out-loud practice, not with reading rules alone.

What are minimal pairs?

Minimal pairs are two words that differ in exactly one sound, like ship and sheep, or fan and van. They are useful because they force your ear to notice the one sound that changes the meaning. Practising pairs together is one of the fastest ways to train both hearing and speaking.

How long should I practise each day?

Ten focused minutes beats an hour of half-listening. Listen to a few words, repeat them slowly out loud, record yourself once, and stop while you are still paying attention. Daily short practice builds the mouth movements far better than one long session a week.

Should I copy an American or British accent?

Either is fine — pick the one you hear most and be consistent, because mixing them makes words harder to remember. What matters most is clarity, not accent: a clear speaker with any accent is easy to understand, and a mumbling speaker with a perfect accent is not.

Why do I understand but not speak clearly?

Listening and speaking are different skills. Understanding uses your ear and your memory; speaking uses muscles that need their own training. Many learners understand far more than they can say. The gap closes when you practise speaking out loud — slowly, daily, and with recordings to check yourself.

Can I practise without a teacher?

Yes, up to a point: model audio, hints about mouth position, and minimal-pair exercises can take you a long way, and recording yourself gives you honest feedback. A teacher still helps for the things a page cannot do — hearing your speech and pointing at the one sound to fix first.
