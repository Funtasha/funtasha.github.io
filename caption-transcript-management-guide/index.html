Caption & Transcript Guide

Captions let deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers follow a video, and transcripts let everyone search, skim and read it at their own pace. This guide shows how to write good captions, how to build a transcript, and which free tools can help. No uploads and no paid software are needed to start.

What this is and who needs it

Captions are the words of a video shown as text on screen, timed to match the speech and the important sounds. A transcript is the same content as a plain text document that can be read, searched and printed. Together they make video open to everyone: deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers, people watching with the sound off, people learning the language, and anyone in a noisy room or a quiet library. This guide is for anyone who makes or shares videos: teachers, small business owners, creators, charities and anyone who posts a video online and wants the widest possible audience.

Captions are not a luxury extra. In many countries the law expects public videos to be captioned, and many platforms now rank captioned videos better because search engines can read the caption text. A captioned video is also simply a better video: viewers stay longer, understand more, and can find the part they need. The work is not hard, but it is real work, and this guide walks through it honestly: what good captions look like, how to write them, how to build a transcript, and which free tools can help.

You do not need to be a professional subtitler to caption a short video well. You need patience, a good pair of ears, and a willingness to read your own work twice. The rest of this guide shows you the method, the mistakes to avoid, and a checklist you can follow on every video you publish.

How it works, step by step

Start by watching the video once all the way through, without typing anything. Listen for who speaks, what matters, and which sounds carry meaning, such as applause, a doorbell or music that sets a mood. Then play it again in short sections of ten to twenty seconds and type exactly what is said, including the speaker's name when it matters. Do not tidy up the speech too much: captions should match what was actually said, though you may remove repeated filler words if they make the text hard to follow.

Next, break the text into captions of one or two short lines each. A good caption stays on screen long enough to read comfortably, breaks at a natural pause or at the end of a phrase, and never covers a face or an important part of the picture. Time each caption to the speech it matches: it should appear as the words are spoken, not seconds before or after. Most caption tools let you drag the timing with a mouse, which is quicker than typing numbers.

For the transcript, take the same text and lay it out as a document. Put each speaker on a new line with their name, add time markers every minute or so, and note important sounds in square brackets, such as applause or music. Publish the transcript next to the video, on the same page, so readers can find it without hunting. Finally, watch the video once more with the captions on, reading along, and fix every error you spot. That final pass is what separates usable captions from sloppy ones.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Captioning costs little money and a fair amount of time. The caption editor built into YouTube Studio is free and lets you type or correct captions for any video you upload there. Free subtitle editors such as Subtitle Edit and Aegisub run on your own computer and cost nothing. If you want automatic captions as a starting draft, several free tools can produce one, though every automatic draft needs a careful human read-through before it is published. Paid captioning services exist for long videos or tight deadlines, but a short video is very doable by hand.

The time it takes depends on the video. A clear, slow-spoken five-minute video might take half an hour to caption well; a fast, technical or multi-speaker video can take several times its own length. Budget more time than you think for your first few videos, and less as you learn the tools. You will work faster with good headphones, a quiet room and a keyboard you trust.

Before you start, gather the video file, a list of any names, places and specialist terms in it, and a note of who is speaking. Spelling a name wrong in a caption is a common and avoidable error, so check names against a reliable source before you publish. If the video has music, decide whether the music matters to the meaning: background music can be noted briefly, while a song with words usually needs its lyrics captioned too.

This guide explains the work honestly: good captions take time, and automatic captions always need a human to check them. Never publish auto-generated captions without reading them through, because a single wrong word can change the meaning of a sentence. If a video carries medical, legal or safety information, have an expert check the captions before you publish.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The worst mistake is publishing automatic captions without checking them. Automatic speech recognition is impressive, but it still mishears names, numbers and specialist words, and a single wrong word can reverse the meaning of a sentence. Always read automatic captions through while watching the video, and fix every error. The second mistake is captions that flash past too fast. If a viewer cannot finish reading a caption before it vanishes, the caption has failed; split long sentences into two captions rather than cramming them into one.

The third mistake is breaking lines in the middle of a phrase, which forces the reader to hold half a thought while waiting for the rest. Break at natural pauses, at the end of a clause, or before a new idea. The fourth mistake is text that blends into the picture behind it: light text on a light background, or captions placed over a busy part of the frame. Use a dark background behind the text, or a strong outline, and keep captions at the bottom of the frame unless they cover something important there.

The fifth mistake is leaving out the sounds that matter. A door slamming, a phone ringing or a crowd gasping can be part of the story, and a deaf viewer misses it entirely unless the caption notes it. The sixth mistake is forgetting speaker names in a conversation: when two people talk, the viewer needs to know who said what. A simple name at the start of the caption solves it. None of these mistakes needs special skill to avoid, only attention.

A practical checklist you can follow

Run through this list before you publish any captioned video. It takes a few minutes and catches most errors.

  • Every spoken word is captioned, in the order it was spoken.
  • Speaker names appear whenever more than one person talks.
  • Important sounds are noted, such as applause, music, a doorbell or a phone ringing.
  • Each caption stays on screen long enough to read comfortably.
  • Lines break at natural pauses, not in the middle of a phrase.
  • Names, places and specialist terms are spelled correctly.
  • The caption text is easy to read against the picture behind it.
  • Captions do not cover faces or important parts of the frame.
  • The transcript is published next to the video with speaker names and time markers.
  • You have watched the whole video once with the captions on and fixed every error you found.

If a video fails more than two checks, fix the timing and the accuracy first. A caption that is readable and right is worth more than a caption that is beautifully styled but wrong.

Tips from real practice

Caption in short sessions. An hour of careful captioning is tiring work, and tired ears miss words. Take a break, come back fresh, and your error rate will drop. Keep a personal list of the words you caption often, such as product names and place names, so you spell them the same way every time. If you caption for an organisation, agree on a short style guide: how speaker names look, how sounds are noted, and how long a line may be. Consistency helps viewers more than cleverness.

Learn the keyboard shortcuts of your caption tool. The tools that let you play, pause and step back with the keyboard are far faster than tools that need the mouse for everything. Save your work often, and keep the caption file with the video file so the two never get separated. When you revise a video, revise the captions at the same time, or the two will drift apart and confuse everyone.

Finally, ask a deaf or hard-of-hearing viewer to watch your captioned video if you can. They will spot problems that hearing captioners miss, such as captions that vanish too fast or sounds that should have been noted. Feedback from real viewers is the fastest way to get better, and it is also a mark of respect for the people your captions serve. Good captions are a craft, and like any craft they improve with practice and honest criticism.

Questions people ask

Why do videos need captions?

Captions let deaf and hard-of-hearing people follow a video, and they help everyone else too: people watching with the sound off, people learning the language, and anyone in a noisy room. Captions also make a video searchable, because search engines can read the caption text. A video without captions shuts out a large part of its possible audience.

What is the difference between a caption and a transcript?

A caption is text that appears on screen in time with the video, showing what is said and what important sounds happen. A transcript is a plain text document of everything said in the video, usually with speaker names and time markers, that a reader can search and skim. Captions serve people watching the video; transcripts serve people who want to read it instead.

What should a good caption include?

A good caption shows every spoken word, identifies who is speaking when it matters, and notes important sounds such as applause, music or a doorbell. Each caption should stay on screen long enough to read, break at natural pauses, and never cover important parts of the picture. Accuracy matters more than speed: a wrong word is worse than a late caption.

How do I write a transcript for a video?

Play the video in short sections and type exactly what is said, adding the speaker's name each time someone new talks. Add time markers every minute or so, and note important sounds in square brackets. When you finish, read the transcript while watching the video once more to catch mistakes, then publish it next to the video where readers can find it.

Can I make captions for free?

Yes. The caption editor built into YouTube Studio is free and lets you type or correct captions for any video you upload there. Free subtitle editors such as Subtitle Edit and Aegisub let you create caption files on your own computer. Automatic captions can give you a starting draft, but always read them through and fix the errors before you publish.

What mistakes make captions hard to read?

The worst mistakes are captions that flash past too fast, lines that break in the middle of a phrase, and text that blends into the picture behind it. Long unbroken blocks of text, missing speaker names and captions that lag behind the speech all make viewing hard work. Keep lines short, keep the timing tight, and give the reader time to breathe between captions.
