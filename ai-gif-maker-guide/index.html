What a GIF is — and why looping clips are back

A GIF is a short moving picture that loops forever. It has no sound, it starts again the moment it ends, and it is small enough to drop into a chat, a comment or a post without thinking twice. GIFs have been around for decades, but they are having a second life — because the loop is the perfect shape for a reaction, a joke, or a tiny moment worth repeating.

The loop is what makes a GIF different from a video. A video has a beginning and an end; a GIF has a moment, and the moment never stops. A cat blinking. A candle being blown out. A friend laughing. Watched once, it is a clip; watched on a loop, it becomes a feeling. That is why GIFs work so well in chats — they say in two seconds what a sentence cannot.

Until recently, making a GIF meant cutting a clip out of a longer video. Now AI can make one from nothing more than a sentence, or from a single still photo. The barrier has dropped to zero: if you can describe the moment, you can loop it.

The new thing: GIFs from ChatGPT Images 2.6

The news in November 2026 is that ChatGPT Images 2.6 can now make looping GIFs from a description or from a still image you upload. Type what you want to see move — a dog wagging its tail, a birthday cake with sparklers, a wave rolling onto a beach — and it comes back as a loop. Or upload a still photo and ask for the movement: the photo stays the same, but the moment inside it starts to move.

One honest note: it is still rolling out in November 2026, so it is not on every account yet. If you open ChatGPT and cannot find the GIF option, nothing is wrong — it simply has not reached your account. Check again in a few days. The feature is new, and rollouts take time.

When it is there, the trick is to ask for one movement, not five. A GIF is short, so the movement has to be simple: a tail wagging, a balloon floating up, a candle flame flickering. One clear movement, described in plain words, gives you a clean loop. Five movements at once give you a mess.

How to make a GIF, step by step

Making a GIF with AI follows the same shape whatever tool you use. Start with the moment: what moves, and how? A pet, a person, an object, a place — pick one subject and one movement. Then write it down in a single sentence, as if you were describing it to a friend.

  • From a sentence. Describe the scene and the movement together: a puppy wagging its tail on a sunny lawn, looping smoothly. Keep it short and name the movement first.
  • From a photo. Upload a clear still photo, then describe only the movement: the candles flicker, the balloons drift up, the waves roll in. The photo supplies the scene; your words supply the motion.
  • Check the loop. Watch it twice. A good loop has no jump at the join — the end flows back into the start. If it jerks, ask for a smoother loop or a simpler movement.
  • Keep it short. A few seconds is plenty. Short loops load fast, share easily, and never outstay their welcome.

The best GIF ideas

Some GIFs are made to be shared, and some are made to be kept. The best ideas are the ones people actually send.

Reaction GIFs. The everyday workhorse: a face, a gesture, a shrug, a cheer. Make a small set of your own — one for yes, one for no, one for wow — and you will use them more than any other kind. Your own reactions beat stock ones because they are yours.

Birthday GIFs. A cake with sparklers, balloons floating up, confetti falling — the celebration moment, looping forever. A birthday GIF made for one person beats a card bought in a shop, because it was made for them and nobody else.

Pet GIFs. A dog wagging its tail, a cat blinking slowly, a bird tilting its head. Pets are the easiest GIF subject there is: the movement is simple, the loop is natural, and nobody ever gets tired of watching.

Celebration GIFs. The big moments — a graduation cap in the air, a goal being scored, fireworks over a skyline. Keep the movement to one thing and let the loop do the work.

Keeping a GIF small so it loads fast

A GIF that takes forever to load is a GIF nobody watches. Size matters more than quality here, because GIFs travel through chats and comments where patience is short.

Three things control the size: length, dimensions, and movement. A short loop is smaller than a long one, a small frame is smaller than a big one, and a simple movement is smaller than a busy one. If your GIF feels heavy, shorten it first — cut the loop to the moment that matters and nothing else.

Colour matters too. A GIF with a few flat colours is much smaller than one full of photographic detail. That is why cartoon-style and simple-scene GIFs load so fast, and why a busy real-life scene can feel slow. If you are making a GIF to share in a chat, simple beats detailed every time.

And before you send it, watch it once on your phone, on a slow connection if you can. If it loads quickly and loops smoothly there, it will work anywhere. If it stutters, make it shorter and try again.

Where to share GIFs

GIFs belong wherever people talk. Chats are the natural home — a reaction GIF in a group chat lands better than any typed reply, because it carries a face and a feeling. Comments are next: a GIF under a friend's post says you saw it, you liked it, and you took a moment to answer in kind.

Party pages and event chats are the third home. A birthday GIF in the group chat on the morning of the party sets the tone for the day. A celebration GIF after good news — a new job, a graduation, a win — does the cheering for you.

A few habits keep GIF sharing friendly. Send one GIF, not five in a row. Match the GIF to the moment — a celebration GIF in a serious chat lands badly. And remember that a GIF of a real person should be of someone who would be happy to see it shared. Your own face, your own pet, your own moments: those are the GIFs that never cause a problem.

When a GIF does not work

Sometimes a GIF comes out wrong, and the fixes are usually simple. If the loop jumps at the join, the movement was too complicated — ask for one movement instead of several, and ask for a smooth loop. If the subject melts or warps, the prompt asked for too much at once — shorten it and name the movement first.

If the GIF is too big to send, shorten the loop and simplify the scene. If the colours look wrong, name them: warm light, soft colours, gentle tones. If the tool will not make a GIF at all, check whether the feature has reached your account yet — ChatGPT Images 2.6 GIFs are still rolling out in November 2026, and some accounts are still waiting.

And if a GIF simply will not come right after a few tries, change the subject, not the settings. A different photo, a simpler scene, or a smaller movement often fixes what no amount of rewording can. The best GIF makers are not the ones who never fail — they are the ones who try one more idea.
