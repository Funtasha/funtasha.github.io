Antique Valuation Guide

An antique is worth what a buyer will pay for it, and that price turns on age, rarity, condition and provenance — not on how old it merely looks. This guide explains each factor in plain language, shows how to spot fakes and reproductions, and tells you where to get a real valuation from a qualified valuer before you buy or sell.

What this is and who needs it

An antique is an old object that people collect, use or display because of its age, its making or its story. The most common rule of thumb is that an object should be at least one hundred years old to count as an antique, while younger collectable objects are usually called vintage. The line is a rule of thumb, not a law: different countries, dealers and auction houses draw it in slightly different places, and some objects are collected as antiques well before they reach a century. What matters more than the label is whether the object has the qualities collectors look for.

This guide is for anyone who has an old object and a question about it. You may have inherited a houseful of furniture and china and need to know what is worth keeping. You may be thinking of buying something at a market or a fair and want to avoid paying too much. You may be planning to sell, or to insure a collection, or you may simply be curious about the clock that has sat on your mantelpiece for as long as you can remember. Whatever brought you here, the method is the same: identify the object, understand the factors that set its value, and get a real valuation from a qualified valuer before money changes hands.

One thing this guide will not do is tell you what your object is worth. No guide can do that honestly, because value depends on the exact object in front of you, its condition, and what buyers want at the time you sell. Prices in books and on the internet are often asking prices, not selling prices, and they go out of date fast. Use this guide to understand the questions a valuer will ask, to avoid the common mistakes that destroy value, and to find the right person to give you a real answer.

How to judge an antique, step by step

Valuing an antique is a process, not a guess. A valuer works through the same steps for a chair as for a painting, and you can follow them yourself to build a sensible picture before you pay for a professional opinion. Take your time: an hour spent looking carefully will teach you more than a week spent guessing.

  1. Identify the object. Work out what it is, what it was made of, and where and when it was probably made. Look for marks: a maker's stamp, a hallmark on silver, a pattern number on china, a label inside a drawer. Photograph every mark, and measure the object. A maker's name or a date letter can change everything.
  2. Check how it was made. Hand-made objects show the signs of the hand: slight unevenness, tool marks, joints fitted by eye. Machine-made copies are regular and crisp. Old furniture is usually joined with pegs, dovetails or mortise and tenon joints; modern screws, staples and chipboard are warning signs on a piece claimed to be old.
  3. Judge the condition honestly. Look for cracks, chips, repairs, replaced parts and missing pieces. Note them all. Condition matters enormously, and a repair done badly can cost more value than the damage it was meant to fix. Original condition, even with honest wear, usually beats a heavy restoration.
  4. Ask about rarity. How many of these were made, and how many survive? A mass-produced ornament from a big factory is rarely valuable however old it is, while a short production run, a failed design or a piece from a small workshop can be scarce. Rarity only counts when collectors want the thing: a rare object nobody wants is still hard to sell.
  5. Trace the provenance. Provenance is the documented history of an object: who owned it, where it was bought, and what papers prove it. Receipts, old photographs, letters, auction records and family stories all help. Good provenance builds trust, and for important pieces it can be the difference between a sale and a shrug.
  6. Compare, then ask a professional. Look at what similar objects have actually sold for at auction, not what sellers are asking. Then take the object, or clear photographs of it, to a qualified valuer or a reputable auction house for a real opinion. A verbal estimate is often free or low cost; a written valuation for insurance usually carries a fee.

Behind those steps sit the six factors that set value: age, rarity, condition, provenance, quality of making, and demand. No single factor decides the price on its own. A very old object in poor condition may be worth less than a younger one in fine order, and a beautiful object nobody collects may sell for little. The most valuable antiques score well on several factors at once, and they have a buyer waiting.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Before you ask anyone for a valuation, gather what a valuer needs. Clear photographs from several angles, including close-ups of any marks, labels or damage, are the starting point. Add the measurements, a note of what the object is made of, and anything you know about where it came from. If you have old receipts, letters or photographs showing the object in a family home, keep them with it: they are the beginning of its provenance, and they cost nothing to save.

There are two kinds of valuation, and it helps to know which one you are asking for. A verbal estimate tells you roughly what an object might sell for, and it is often given free at an auction house valuation day or for a small fee. A written valuation is a formal document, usually for insurance or for settling an estate, and it carries a fee that varies with the number of objects and the work involved. Ask what the fee covers before you agree, and ask whether the valuation is for insurance, for sale or for probate, because the figure is different in each case.

Time depends on the size of the job. A single object can often be assessed in one visit. A houseful takes longer: a valuer may need a full day to look through a collection, and a written report can take a week or more to prepare. Do not rush the process, and never accept a first offer on the spot. If someone offers to buy on the day they value, get a second opinion first: a valuer who also buys has a reason to value low.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

Most value is lost through good intentions. People clean, polish, repair and throw away, and each of those actions can take value off an object for ever. The mistakes below are the ones valuers see most often, and all of them are avoidable.

  • Over-cleaning. The darkened surface on old wood, silver or brass is called patina, and collectors value it: it is the sign of age. Harsh cleaning, polishing or stripping removes it and can cut the value sharply. Dust gently and leave the rest alone until a specialist advises you.
  • Bad restoration. A repair by someone who does not understand old objects often does more harm than the damage itself. Modern glue, filler, paint and varnish stand out to a trained eye. If a repair is needed, ask a conservator who works on objects of that kind.
  • Trusting asking prices. The price on a shop tag or an online listing is what the seller hopes for, not what buyers pay. Completed auction results are a far better guide, and even those vary with the day and the room.
  • Ignoring reproductions. Good copies exist for almost every popular antique, from furniture to porcelain to clocks. Uniform wear, modern materials, and marks that look stamped rather than cut are warning signs. When in doubt, ask a specialist before you buy.
  • Throwing away the broken bits. A missing handle, a cracked lid or a broken leg still matters: original parts, even damaged ones, help a restorer and prove the object is complete. Keep every piece, and keep the original packaging if you have it.
  • Selling to the first buyer. The first offer is rarely the best. A dealer, an auction house and a private collector will each pay differently for the same object. Get more than one opinion, and give yourself time to choose.

A practical checklist you can follow

Work through this checklist for each object, one at a time. It takes only a few minutes per piece, and it gives a valuer everything needed for a first opinion.

  • Photograph the object from the front, back, sides and top.
  • Photograph every mark, stamp, label, signature and date letter.
  • Measure height, width and depth, and note the weight if you can.
  • Write down what it is made of, as far as you can tell.
  • Note every crack, chip, repair, replaced part and missing piece.
  • Write down where the object came from and who owned it before you.
  • Gather any receipts, letters, photographs or auction records.
  • Keep all loose parts, lids, handles and original boxes together.
  • Do not clean, polish, paint or repair anything yet.
  • Store the object safely, away from damp, heat and direct sunlight.
  • Take the photographs and notes to a qualified valuer for an opinion.

Keep the checklist with the object, or keep a copy in a folder with the photographs. If you have many objects, work through them in small batches and number each one so your notes and photographs stay matched. A little order at the start saves hours of confusion later.

Tips from real practice

Learn the marks of the things you collect. A few hours studying hallmarks, maker's stamps and pattern numbers will save you from most bad purchases, because marks are the first thing a forger gets wrong. Reference books and museum collections are good places to learn, and many auction houses publish free guides to the marks of the makers they sell.

Buy the best you can afford, and buy slowly. One fine object in good condition is a better collection than ten poor ones, and it is easier to sell. Take your time at a fair or a market: walk around the whole room before you buy anything, compare the same kind of object at several stalls, and walk away if you feel rushed. The object will still be there after a cup of tea, and if it is not, another will come along.

Keep records from the day you buy. Note the date, the seller, the price you paid and anything the seller told you, and keep the receipt. Those notes become the provenance for the next owner, and they protect you if you ever need to prove what you bought. Finally, enjoy the objects for what they are. The best collections are built by people who love the things they own, not by people chasing a price.

What this page cannot do

This page teaches you how to think about antique value. It cannot value your object, it cannot tell you whether a particular piece is genuine, and it cannot predict what anything will sell for. Value depends on the exact object, its condition, and the buyers in the market at the time you sell — and all three change.

For a real answer, take the object, or clear photographs of it, to a qualified valuer, a reputable auction house or a specialist dealer, and get the opinion in writing if it matters for insurance or for an estate. Never accept a first offer on the spot, and never let anyone take an object away for valuation without a written receipt.

Questions people ask

What makes an antique valuable?

Age, rarity, condition, provenance, quality of making and demand, taken together. Age alone is not enough: a common object can be very old and still worth little, while a younger object that is rare, well made and wanted by collectors can be worth far more. The most valuable antiques score well on several of these factors at once.

How can I tell if something is a real antique?

Look at the marks, the materials and the way it was made: hand-made joints, old screws and nails, natural wear in the places hands touch. Reproductions often show uniform wear, modern materials, machine-cut parts and marks that look stamped rather than cut. Compare with known examples in museums and reference books, and ask a specialist before you pay a high price.

What is provenance?

Provenance is the documented history of an object: who owned it, where it was bought, and what papers prove it. Receipts, old photographs, letters, auction records and family notes all count. Good provenance builds trust, proves an object is genuine, and can raise its value, especially for important pieces.

Where can I get a real antique valuation?

From a qualified valuer, a reputable auction house or a specialist dealer. Check credentials before you book, ask what the valuation is for — insurance, sale or probate — and ask whether the fee covers a written report. Many auction houses hold valuation days where a verbal estimate is free or low cost.

Does condition matter more than age?

Often, yes. A rare object in poor condition can be worth less than a common one in fine order, because collectors pay for what they can see and enjoy. For very rare pieces condition matters less, since there may be no better example to buy. Original condition, even with honest wear, usually beats a heavy or careless restoration.

Should I restore an old object before selling it?

Usually not without specialist advice. Poor restoration can destroy value, and collectors often prefer an object in honest original condition. Leave cleaning to professionals, keep all original parts, and ask a valuer or conservator whether a repair would help or harm the price before you agree to any work.
