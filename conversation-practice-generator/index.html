What this generator does — and how to use it

Every learner knows the moment: you want to speak English, but you cannot think of anything to say. This generator fixes that one problem. Pick your level and a situation, and it gives you a conversation topic with an opening line, five starter questions, follow-up questions, and useful phrases — a complete practice plan you can copy and use.

Use it with a partner or alone. With a partner, take turns asking and answering; the follow-up questions keep the conversation going past the first minute, which is where most practice conversations die. Alone, speak both sides out loud: ask the question, then answer it as yourself. It feels strange at first and works surprisingly well.

Tap "New topic" whenever you want a fresh one. The generator cycles through two topics for every level and situation, so you will not get the same topic twice in a row. When a topic feels too easy, move up a level; when it feels impossible, move down one. The right level is the one where you can say most of what you want, with a little effort.

How to practise a conversation (alone or with a partner)

With a partner, set a timer for five minutes and talk about one topic only. One person asks, the other answers, then swap roles. Do not stop to correct every mistake — note one or two and discuss them afterwards. Stopping mid-sentence to fix grammar kills the flow you are trying to build.

Alone, the method is almost the same. Read the opening line aloud, then answer it as yourself, out loud, for at least thirty seconds. Then ask yourself the next starter question and answer that. Speaking aloud matters: thinking the answers in your head trains reading, not speaking. Your mouth needs the practice as much as your brain.

Record yourself once a week. Play it back and listen for one thing only — not every mistake, just one pattern. Maybe you always answer in one word. Maybe you never ask a follow-up. Fix one pattern for a week, then find the next. Small, steady fixes add up faster than trying to fix everything at once.

If you practise alone, give yourself a role to play. Pretend you are meeting a new colleague, or catching up with an old friend, and answer as that person would. Role-play sounds childish and works brilliantly, because it gives your answers a reason to exist. A question answered to an imaginary person is still a question answered — and your mouth cannot tell the difference.

How to keep a conversation going (ask, answer, ask back)

Most conversations die for one reason: someone answers and stops. "Where are you from?" — "Bangladesh." Silence. The fix is a simple rhythm: ask, answer, ask back. Answer the question, add one detail, and return a question of your own. "I am from Bangladesh — from Dhaka. Have you ever been there?" Three moves, and the conversation is alive again.

The follow-up questions in each practice plan are built for this rhythm. They are the questions a good listener asks: "Tell me more about that", "What surprised you?", "Would you do it again?" Learn five of these by heart and you will never be stuck. They work in almost any conversation, about almost any topic.

Also learn the phrases that buy you time: "That is a good question", "Let me think", "How do I say this in English?" These are not signs of weakness — native speakers use them constantly. They keep the conversation warm while your brain catches up, and they sound far more natural than silence.

Worked example: a full short conversation

Here is a beginner conversation about home towns, using the plan this generator gives. A: "Hello! I am glad to meet you. Where are you from?" B: "I am from Chittagong, in Bangladesh. It is a port city by the sea. What about you?" A: "I am from Manchester. What is your home town like?" B: "It is busy and green, with hills all around. I miss the sea most. How long have you lived here?"

Notice the pattern: every answer adds one detail and asks something back. Nobody gives a one-word answer, and nobody gives a speech. The conversation moves because both people are working at it — asking is half the job, and answering with a detail plus a question is the other half.

Try the same conversation yourself, out loud, with your own home town. Then try it with a different topic from the generator. The words change; the rhythm stays the same. Once the rhythm feels natural, you can talk about anything — because conversation is a skill, not a list of topics.

Levels: what beginner, intermediate and advanced mean here

Beginner topics use simple questions about facts: where, when, who, what. The sentences are short, the vocabulary is everyday, and the answers can be short too. If you can handle these comfortably, you are ready for intermediate.

Intermediate topics ask for opinions and stories: why, how, what happened, what would you do. The questions are longer, the answers need more than one sentence, and the phrases help you organise your thoughts — "on the one hand", "the best thing was", "looking back".

Advanced topics ask for judgement and reflection: what changed you, what you believe, what the future holds. There is no single right answer, which is the point — advanced conversation is about thinking out loud in English, not reciting prepared lines. If these feel hard, that is normal; they are meant to stretch you.

Levels are a guide, not a fence. A beginner topic can still challenge an advanced speaker if they push themselves to answer at length, and an advanced topic can help a beginner who is willing to struggle through it with simple words. Move between levels freely, and choose by how the topic makes you feel: interested and slightly stretched is exactly right.

Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

The most common mistake is answering yes or no and stopping. "Do you like your job?" — "Yes." The conversation has nowhere to go. The fix is mechanical: every answer gets one extra sentence. "Yes, I like it — the people are friendly, and I learn something new every week." One sentence changes everything.

The second mistake is memorising scripts. Learners sometimes prepare perfect answers to expected questions, then freeze when the real question is slightly different. Scripts feel safe and teach nothing. Use the questions here as practice, not as lines to memorise — the goal is to handle any question, not to perform one answer.

The third mistake is worrying about mistakes in the middle of speaking. Stopping to fix a verb tense breaks the flow and teaches your brain that speaking is dangerous. Let the mistake go, finish the sentence, and note it for later. Fluency grows from finishing sentences, not from perfecting them.

What this generator cannot do

This generator cannot be your conversation partner. It gives you the material — topics, questions, phrases — but it cannot listen, react, or surprise you the way a real person does. Real conversation is unpredictable, and that unpredictability is exactly what you need to practise. Find a partner: a classmate, a colleague, a language exchange, or a friend who is also learning.

It also cannot judge your speaking. It cannot hear your pronunciation, your rhythm, or your confidence. For that, record yourself, or ask a partner for one honest observation after each practice session: "What one thing should I work on?" One observation, acted on, beats a page of corrections.

Finally, it cannot make you speak. The topics will sit on the screen until you say them aloud. Five minutes of speaking a day — one topic, out loud, every day — will change your English more in a month than any amount of reading about conversation. Start with one topic now.

A good routine is one topic a day, five minutes, out loud, at the same time each day — after breakfast, on the bus, before bed. The routine matters more than the topic: a small daily habit beats a heroic weekly session, because speaking is a muscle, and muscles grow from regular use, not from occasional effort.

Questions people ask

How do I start a conversation in English?

With a greeting and one simple question: 'Hello! How are you?' or 'Have you been here before?' You do not need a clever opening — a friendly greeting and a question about the other person is enough. The generator's opening lines give you a ready-made start for each topic.

What if I do not understand the other person?

Say so, plainly: 'Sorry, could you say that again, slowly?' or 'I did not catch that — could you repeat it?' Native speakers ask this all the time, and nobody minds. Pretending to understand is far worse than asking — it leads to wrong answers and confusion for both sides.

How do I keep a conversation going?

Use the rhythm: ask, answer, ask back. Answer the question, add one detail, then return a question of your own. Learn five follow-up questions by heart — 'Tell me more', 'What happened next?', 'Would you do it again?' — and you will rarely be stuck.

Is it okay to make mistakes?

Yes — mistakes are how speaking improves. A conversation partner cares about understanding you, not about perfect grammar. Correct the big mistakes that cause confusion, let the small ones go, and finish your sentences. Fluency grows from finishing sentences, not from perfecting them.

What topics are safe for small talk?

Weather, food, travel, weekend plans, films, and sports are safe almost everywhere. Avoid money, politics, religion, and personal health with people you have just met. When in doubt, ask about the other person's interests — people enjoy talking about what they like.

How can I practise alone?

Speak both sides of a conversation out loud: ask the question, then answer it as yourself, for at least thirty seconds each. Record yourself once a week and listen for one pattern to fix. It feels strange at first, but it trains the same muscles as real conversation — and it is far better than silent study.
