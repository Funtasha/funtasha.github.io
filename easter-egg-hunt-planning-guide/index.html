Easter Egg Hunt Planning Guide

This guide walks you through planning an Easter egg hunt, from age splits and hiding spots to eggs per child, clues, prizes, safety notes and an indoor rain plan. Use the checklist to keep track of every task, and try the egg calculator to work out how many eggs you need.

Split the hunt by age

The secret to a happy egg hunt is splitting the children by age. Younger children hunt in one area with easy, visible eggs, while older children hunt in a bigger area with trickier hiding spots. A simple split — under-fives, five to eight, and nine and over — works well, and each group gets its own start time so nobody is rushed or crowded out.

If the group is small, split by area instead of time: a front garden for the little ones and a back garden for the older ones. Tell each group where their area is before you start, and give the youngest children a head start so they find their eggs before the older children arrive.

Choose hiding spots

Hide eggs at different heights for different ages: on the grass and on low branches for little children, and tucked behind pots, under leaves and on windowsills for older ones. Keep every hiding spot safe — no climbing, no roads, no water, and nothing near a fence or a gate that leads out of the garden.

Walk the hunting area yourself before you hide anything, and remove anything sharp, slippery or fragile. Count the eggs as you hide them, and write down the tricky spots — you will need the list when the hunt is over and one egg is still missing.

Plan how many eggs per child

Eight to twelve eggs per child is a good target: enough to make the hunt exciting, without so many that the older children finish in two minutes. Younger children are happy with fewer, easy eggs, while older children enjoy a bigger challenge with more eggs spread further apart.

Buy a few spare eggs beyond your count, for cracked shells and eggs that go missing. Fill the eggs with small treats, stickers or tiny toys, and keep a few empty eggs in the mix for older children — an empty egg is a fun surprise, not a disappointment.

Add clues for older children

Older children love a hunt with clues. Write a short rhyming clue for each hiding spot, and let the children solve their way from one egg to the next. Start with an easy clue and make each one a little harder, so the hunt builds to a finish. A treasure map, drawn simply, works just as well as written clues.

Keep the clues fair: every clue should point to a real place in the hunting area, and every child should be able to solve them with a little thought. If a clue is too hard, a gentle hint from an adult keeps the hunt moving.

Prizes everyone enjoys

Every child should leave with something, so plan a small prize for each hunter — a chocolate egg, a sticker sheet or a tiny toy. A special prize for the child who finds the golden egg, or the most eggs, adds excitement, but keep the main prize small so nobody feels like a loser.

Hand out the prizes at the end, when everyone has finished and the eggs are counted. A short prize moment — a round of applause for every hunter — ends the hunt on a happy note.

Allergy and supervision safety

Ask parents about allergies before you choose the treats, and label anything that contains common allergens. Keep treats with nuts, milk or gluten in a separate, clearly marked batch, and offer a non-food prize — stickers or a small toy — for any child who cannot eat the treats. Check every treat's label before you put it in an egg.

Make sure an adult is watching each hunting area, and keep small items and small eggs away from very young children, who put everything in their mouths. Count the children in and out, and agree a simple rule before you start: stay inside the hunting area, and come back when you hear the whistle or the call.

Have an indoor rain plan

Rain should not cancel the hunt — it just moves it indoors. Hide the eggs around the living room, kitchen and hallway instead, at heights the children can reach, and keep the fragile things out of the way. A shorter indoor hunt with fewer eggs works well, and the children will enjoy it just as much.

Decide on the rain plan the day before, so you are not scrambling in the morning. Tell parents the hunt moves indoors if it rains, and have the indoor hiding spots picked out in advance. A hunt that goes ahead, rain or shine, is one the children will remember.

Questions people ask

How many eggs per child?

Eight to twelve eggs per child is a good target. Younger children are happy with fewer, easy eggs, and older children enjoy more eggs spread further apart.

What age groups work best?

A simple split — under-fives, five to eight, and nine and over — works well, with each group hunting in its own area or at its own start time.

What if it rains on the day?

Move the hunt indoors. Hide the eggs around the living room, kitchen and hallway, and tell parents the rain plan in advance.

What about children with allergies?

Ask parents before you choose the treats, label anything with common allergens, and offer a non-food prize like stickers or a small toy.

How long should an egg hunt last?

Half an hour of hunting is plenty for most children, followed by counting the eggs and handing out prizes. Keep the whole event to about an hour.
