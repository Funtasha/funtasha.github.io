Animal Rescue Case Management Tool

A rescue can take in dozens of animals a month, and every one of them needs the same thing: a file that says where it came from, what it needs, and where it is going. This guide explains how rescues track an animal from intake to adoption, what a case file holds, and the templates a small rescue can use from today.

This page explains how animal rescues track their cases. It is not a case system, and it does not hold any animal's records — a static page cannot do that, and this page does not pretend to. The templates here are paper and spreadsheet templates your rescue fills in and keeps itself.

How to use this guide

This guide is for people who run, volunteer for, or want to start a small animal rescue. It explains how a rescue keeps track of every animal in its care — from the day the animal arrives to the day it goes to a new home — using a simple case file and a handful of templates. You do not need special software, a budget, or a big team: you need a system everyone uses the same way.

Be clear about what this page is and is not. It is a guide with printable templates. It is not a case management system: it cannot store records, send reminders, or track an animal for you. The templates are yours to copy, print, and fill in — on paper, or in a spreadsheet — and the system works because the people using it keep it up to date, not because the page does anything clever.

The case file explained

A case file is the rescue's memory of one animal. It starts the day the animal arrives and it is not finished until the animal is adopted — and even then, the file is kept, because an adopted animal may come back, and a new owner may one day call with a question the file can answer. One animal, one file, from intake to adoption and beyond.

A good case file holds the facts a stranger would need to look after the animal safely: what it is, where it came from, what the vet has done, what it eats, what medicine it takes, how it behaves, and where it is now. It does not hold opinions dressed up as facts — 'friendly' means nothing unless the file also says friendly with whom, and in what situation. The best case files are short, dated, and honest about what is not yet known.

The file also holds the decisions: who decided the animal was ready for adoption, who approved the adopter, and who signed the adoption off. Decisions without names are decisions nobody owns, and a rescue that cannot say who decided what cannot learn from its mistakes. Write the name and the date next to every decision.

Intake to adoption: the journey of one animal

Every animal in a rescue follows the same road, and the case file follows it too. The road starts at intake: the animal arrives, is checked by a vet, is given a name or an ID number, and gets a case file. The intake record notes where the animal came from, its condition on arrival, and anything urgent — an injury, an illness, a pregnancy — that needs attention first.

Next comes care: feeding, treatment, vaccination, and watching. This is the longest part of the road, and the part where good records matter most, because the animal's needs change as it recovers. A frightened animal becomes confident; a thin animal gains weight; a medical problem is found and treated. Each change goes in the file, with a date, so that anyone picking the file up can see where the animal stands today.

Then comes assessment: is the animal ready for adoption, and what kind of home does it need? A good assessment is honest about the animal's needs — a dog that cannot live with cats, a cat that needs a quiet home, an animal that needs medicine for life. The last step is adoption itself: the adopter is checked, the match is made, the paperwork is signed, and the file is closed — but kept, because the rescue's responsibility does not end at the door.

Foster handovers: the moment most rescues lose track

Fostering is where most rescues lose track of their animals. The animal leaves the shelter and goes to a foster's home, and with it goes the daily knowledge: how it eats, how it sleeps, what it is afraid of, what it has learned. If that knowledge stays in the foster's head, it is lost when the animal moves on — to another foster, or to an adopter who starts from nothing.

The answer is a handover note, written every time an animal changes hands. The note is short: what the animal eats and when, what medicine it takes, how it behaves with people and other animals, what it is afraid of, and what it has been learning. The foster writes it when the animal leaves, and the next person reads it before the animal arrives. A handover without a note is a handover with half the information missing.

Foster notes should be dated and kept with the case file, not in a messaging app where they disappear. A short note written the day the animal moves is worth more than a long report written a month later from memory. Make the handover note a rule, not a favour: no note, no handover.

The templates: a case file you can copy

A template is only useful if a tired volunteer can fill it in at the end of a long day, so keep yours short. The case file template below covers one animal on one page. Copy it into a document or a spreadsheet, print it, and keep one copy per animal in a folder or a binder. The fields are the ones that matter most; add your rescue's own fields only if they earn their place.

  • Animal name or ID, species, breed or type, sex, age or estimated age
  • Date and place of intake, and where the animal came from
  • Vet checks: dates, findings, vaccinations, treatments, next due date
  • Food: what it eats, how much, how often, and any food it must not have
  • Medicine: name, dose, times, and what it is for
  • Behaviour: how it acts with people, children, and other animals
  • Foster notes: who fostered it, when, and the handover notes
  • Adoption: adopter checks, match notes, date, and who approved it

The second template is the handover note, and it is even shorter: animal name, date, who it is going to, what it eats, what medicine it takes, and three lines — what it likes, what it fears, and what it is learning. Print a stack of handover notes and keep them where fosters collect animals. A template that is easy to find is a template that gets used.

Recording adoptions properly

An adoption is not finished when the animal leaves. It is finished when the record is complete: who adopted the animal, when, what was checked before the match, what the adopter was told, and what follow-up was agreed. A rescue that cannot answer those questions about an adoption cannot help the adopter later, and cannot show anyone — a donor, an inspector, a worried member of the public — that it does its job properly.

The adoption record should also note the follow-up: a call a week later, a visit a month later, or simply a message asking how the animal is settling in. Follow-ups catch problems early — an adopter who is struggling, an animal that is not eating, a match that is not working — and they show the adopter that the rescue is still there. An adoption without a follow-up is a door closed too soon.

Keep adoption records for years, not months. Animals live a long time, and an adopter may call back after years with a question about the animal's history, or a rescue may need to take an animal back. A record kept for years is a kindness to the animal and to everyone who cares for it.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is no intake record at all. The animal arrives, everyone is busy, and the file is started 'later' — which means never, or a week later from memory. Intake is the one moment that cannot be recovered: where the animal came from, and what condition it was in, are facts that fade within days. Start the file the day the animal arrives, even if it is only a name, a date, and a sentence.

The second mistake is foster notes in someone's head. The foster knows the animal best, and if that knowledge is never written down, it leaves with the foster. Make the handover note a rule, keep it with the file, and thank the foster for writing it — it is as much a part of the care as the feeding.

The third mistake is a system only one person understands. If one volunteer holds all the records in their own notebook or their own phone, the rescue stops working when that person is away. Records belong to the rescue, not to one person: keep them where the team can reach them, in a form anyone on the team can read.

Questions people ask

How do rescues track animals?

Rescues track animals with a case file for each animal — one file from intake to adoption, holding where the animal came from, what the vet has done, what it eats, what medicine it takes, how it behaves, and where it is now. The file is updated every time something changes, with a date. This page explains the method and gives you the templates; it is not a case system itself.

What is a case file?

A case file is the rescue's memory of one animal: a single record, started on the day the animal arrives, holding its history, its medical care, its food and medicine, its behaviour, its foster notes, and its adoption record. One animal, one file. The file is kept even after adoption, because the animal may come back or the new owner may call with a question.

How does fostering work?

Fostering means a volunteer looks after a rescue animal in their own home until it is adopted. The foster feeds the animal, gives its medicine, and learns how it behaves — then writes a handover note when the animal moves on, so the next person does not start from nothing. Good fostering depends on that note: without it, everything the foster learned is lost.

What templates do rescues need?

A rescue needs two templates above all: a case file template (one page per animal, with intake, vet, food, medicine, behaviour and adoption fields) and a handover note template (what the animal eats, what medicine it takes, what it likes, what it fears, what it is learning). Both are in this guide as lists you can copy into a document or a spreadsheet.

How do adoptions get recorded?

An adoption is recorded with the adopter's checks, the match notes, the date, and who approved it — plus the follow-up that was agreed, such as a call a week later. The record is kept for years, because an adopter may call back with a question, or the rescue may need to take the animal back. An adoption without a record is a door closed too soon.

Can a small rescue use this?

Yes — this system was made for small rescues. One page per animal, one handover note per move, and one folder or binder to keep them in is enough to start. You do not need special software or a budget. What you need is the habit: start the file on intake day, write the handover note every time, and keep the records where the whole team can reach them.
