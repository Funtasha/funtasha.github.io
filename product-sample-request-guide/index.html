Product Sample Request Guide

A product sample is a small piece of the real product, sent so you can judge its quality before you order in bulk. This guide shows how to ask for one, what to check when it arrives, and how to spot the scams that target new buyers.

This page is a guide only. It does not send sample requests and it lists no suppliers. Always check a supplier through independent means before you pay anything, and never pay a large fee for a sample — a genuine supplier will explain the sample cost plainly before you commit.

What this is and who needs it

A product sample is one unit, or a small set, of a product that a supplier sends you before you place a full order. It is the same item you would receive in bulk, made the same way, from the same materials — or at least, that is what it should be. The whole point of a sample is to close the gap between a photo on a screen and a real object in your hands. Photos hide weight, texture, smell, stitching, finish and all the small details that decide whether a product is good enough to sell.

Samples matter most to anyone buying goods to resell: shop owners, online sellers, market traders and small brands ordering their first stock. They also matter to buyers ordering custom or printed goods, where a small change in colour or size can ruin a whole batch. If you are ordering something you have never held before, from a supplier you have never worked with, a sample is the cheapest insurance you can buy. Skipping it to save a few days is one of the most expensive shortcuts in buying.

There are a few kinds of sample, and it helps to know which one you are asking for. A stock sample is an existing item taken from the shelf, useful for checking general quality. A pre-production sample is made to your exact specification before the full run starts, and it is the one to approve in writing before bulk production begins. A counter sample is the sample you keep as the agreed reference: the bulk order should match it, and if it does not, the counter sample is your evidence. Knowing these three types changes the conversation with a supplier, because you can ask for the right one by name.

How it works, step by step

Start by writing down exactly what you want to check. A sample is only useful if you know what you are testing: the material, the size, the colour, the weight, the finish, the packaging, or how the item works. Write your list before you contact the supplier, and keep it short and specific. Vague requests get vague samples, and a supplier cannot guess what matters to you.

Then contact the supplier and ask for the sample in plain words. Say what product you want, how many pieces you need, and what you plan to check. Ask three questions in the same message: what the sample costs, how long it will take to arrive, and whether the sample cost comes off your first bulk order. A clear request gets a clear answer, and the supplier's reply tells you a lot about how they do business. Slow, evasive or pushy replies are a warning sign before you have spent anything.

  1. Agree the sample in writing: the exact product, the quantity, the cost, the delivery time and who pays for shipping.
  2. Pay only through a traceable method, and keep the receipt and the supplier's written confirmation together.
  3. When the sample arrives, check it against your list the same day, while the details are fresh in your mind.
  4. Photograph the sample from several angles, measure it, and note anything that differs from what was promised.
  5. Reply to the supplier with your verdict: approve it, ask for changes, or walk away — always in writing.
  6. If you approve, keep the sample safe as your counter sample and refer to it in the bulk order.

The written record matters more than most new buyers expect. Memory fades, messages get lost, and a supplier who promised one thing may later say another. A short written thread — what was agreed, what was paid, what arrived — protects both sides and makes any dispute much easier to settle. Keep it in one place, and do not rely on chat messages alone if you can keep copies elsewhere too.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Sample costs vary widely. Some suppliers send a stock sample free and ask you to pay only the shipping. Others charge for the sample itself, often at a price close to the single-unit retail price, and some take the sample cost off your first bulk order. All three are normal. What is not normal is a large upfront fee for a sample of an ordinary product, or a fee that keeps growing with new charges. If the sample fee feels out of proportion to the product, step back and compare with other suppliers before you pay.

Shipping is often the bigger cost, especially for a single item sent across a border. Ask for the shipping cost before you agree, and ask which delivery service will be used so you can track the parcel. Delivery time depends on where the supplier is and how the sample is sent: a stock sample can arrive in days, while a pre-production sample has to be made first and can take weeks. Build that time into your plans. Rushing a supplier to skip the sample stage is how bad bulk orders happen.

Before you start, gather the details that make your request clear: the product name or code, the colour and size you want, the quantity you plan to order in bulk, and the address the sample should go to. If the product is custom, have your design, measurements or artwork ready. The more complete your request, the faster the supplier can answer, and the more seriously they will take you. Suppliers deal with many enquiries, and a buyer who knows what they want stands out.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is ordering in bulk without a sample at all. It usually happens because the price looks good and the buyer is in a hurry. A photo cannot show you a rough seam, a weak clasp, a colour that is two shades off, or a smell that customers will notice at once. By the time the bulk order arrives, the money is spent and the options are few. A sample would have shown all of it for a fraction of the cost.

The second mistake is checking the sample carelessly. People open the parcel, glance at the item, and approve it on the spot. Then the bulk order arrives and the size is wrong, the colour varies between pieces, or the packaging is different. Check the sample against your written list, measure it, test it the way a customer would, and look at it in daylight as well as indoor light. Colours shift under different light, and a sample approved under a yellow bulb can look very different on a shop shelf.

The third mistake is approving a sample by voice or chat without a written record. If the bulk order differs, you need proof of what was agreed. A short written message — 'I approve the sample as received on this date, with the changes listed here' — costs a minute and can save a dispute. The fourth mistake is treating the sample as a promise the supplier cannot break. A sample shows what the supplier can make, not what they will make at scale. That is why the counter sample, the written approval and a clear bulk order all matter together.

  • Never approve a sample you have not held and measured yourself.
  • Never pay a sample fee that the supplier cannot explain in plain words.
  • Never skip the written approval — a chat message is better than nothing, but a clear written note is best.
  • Never assume the bulk order will match the sample: state in the order that it must match the approved sample.
  • Never throw the sample away after approval: it is your reference if the bulk order differs.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist for every sample, whether it is your first order or your fiftieth. Work through it in order, and tick each step off as you go. A checklist turns a habit into a system, and a system catches the mistakes that memory misses.

  • Write down what you want to check: material, size, colour, weight, finish, function and packaging.
  • Ask the supplier for the sample cost, the shipping cost and the delivery time, in writing.
  • Confirm whether the sample cost comes off your first bulk order.
  • Pay by a traceable method and keep the receipt and the written agreement together.
  • When the sample arrives, check it the same day against your written list.
  • Measure it, weigh it if weight matters, and test it the way a customer would use it.
  • Photograph it from several angles in daylight and keep the photos with your notes.
  • Compare the colour, size and finish with what the supplier described.
  • Write your verdict to the supplier: approve, ask for changes, or decline — always in writing.
  • If you approve, store the sample safely as your counter sample and name it in the bulk order.
  • If the bulk order arrives, compare it with the counter sample before you accept it.

If any step on the list cannot be done — the supplier will not put the price in writing, the sample cannot be tracked, or the item arrives different from what was described — treat that as information. A supplier who makes the sample stage difficult will usually make the bulk order difficult too. It is better to learn that from a sample than from a full shipment.

Tips from real practice

Ask for more than one sample when the product varies. If you are ordering in several colours, ask for a sample of each colour, because dye lots vary. If you are ordering in several sizes, ask for the smallest and the largest, because grading errors show up at the ends of the range. A single sample of a single variant tells you about that one piece only.

Keep a sample shelf or box. Label each approved sample with the supplier name, the date and the order it relates to. Over time this becomes a small library of what good looks like for your business, and it makes comparing new suppliers much faster. When a new supplier's sample arrives, you can hold it next to the ones you already trust.

Build the sample stage into your schedule from the start. Tell customers and partners that you allow time for sampling, so nobody pressures you to skip it. The buyers who get into trouble are usually the ones who ran out of time and ordered blind. A sample takes days; a bad bulk order takes months to fix, if it can be fixed at all.

Finally, remember that sampling is a two-way test. You are testing the product, and you are also testing the supplier: how fast they reply, how clearly they explain costs, how carefully they pack, and how they handle a question or a change. A supplier who is careful with a sample is usually careful with a bulk order. A supplier who is careless with a sample has already told you what to expect.

Questions people ask

Why should I ask for a sample first?

Because a photo cannot show you weight, texture, finish, colour in daylight, or how the item actually works. A sample lets you check all of that for a small cost before you commit to a bulk order. Skipping the sample is the most common reason buyers end up with stock they cannot sell.

How do I request a product sample?

Write to the supplier with the exact product, the colour and size you want, and how many pieces you need. Ask what the sample costs, how long delivery will take, and whether the sample cost comes off your first bulk order. Get the answers in writing before you pay anything.

Should a sample be free?

Sometimes, but not always. Some suppliers send stock samples free and charge only shipping; others charge for the sample, often at a price close to the single-unit price. What matters is that the cost is explained clearly before you pay. A large unexplained fee for an ordinary sample is a warning sign.

What should I check when the sample arrives?

Check it against the list you wrote before ordering: material, size, colour, weight, finish, function and packaging. Measure it, test it the way a customer would, and look at it in daylight as well as indoor light. Photograph it from several angles and keep the photos with your notes.

What if the bulk order differs from the sample?

Compare the bulk order with the counter sample you kept, and photograph every difference. Then contact the supplier in writing, describe the differences clearly, and ask how they will fix it. This is why the written approval and the kept sample matter: without them, a dispute is your word against theirs.

How do I avoid sample scams?

Be careful with suppliers who ask for large upfront fees, refuse to put costs in writing, pressure you to pay quickly, or cannot be checked through independent means. Pay by a traceable method, never pay more than the sample is worth, and walk away if anything feels rushed or secretive. A genuine supplier is patient with questions.
