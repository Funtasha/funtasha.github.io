What a good return policy must say

A good return policy answers four questions, in plain words, with no room for argument. The first is the window: how many days does a customer have to return something? State the number clearly, and say when the counting starts, usually from the day the order arrives. A policy that says returns are accepted soon is not a policy at all, because soon means something different to everyone.

The second is the condition: what state must the item be in? Most shops ask for the item to be unused, with its tags and packaging. Some shops accept items that have been tried on or opened. Whatever you choose, say it plainly, so the customer knows before they buy. The third is the refund: does the customer get their money back, an exchange for another item, or credit to spend in your shop? Each choice has its fans, and each is fair if it is stated honestly before the sale.

The fourth is the shipping: who pays to send the item back? If the customer pays, say so. If the shop pays, say so. If the shop pays only when the item is faulty, say that too. A policy that hides the shipping cost is one of the fastest ways to lose a customer's trust, because the customer finds out at the worst possible moment, when they are already unhappy about the item.

The questions explained

The return window is the number of days a customer has to change their mind. Seven days is short and can put buyers off. Fourteen days is common for small shops. Thirty days is generous and builds trust, and it is the standard the generator uses when you leave the field blank. A longer window sounds risky, but most returns happen in the first few days anyway, so the extra days cost little and win a lot of goodwill.

The condition question is about what you will accept back. Unused means the item is in the state it arrived in, with tags and packaging intact. Any condition means you will take it back even if it has been used or opened. The second choice is braver and rarer, and it works best for shops selling low cost items where a return is cheaper than an argument. The refund method question is about what the customer gets. A refund gives the money back. An exchange swaps the item for another one. Store credit gives the customer money to spend in your shop, which keeps the sale but can frustrate a customer who wanted their money back.

The shipping question is about who pays the postage on a return. If your shop pays, customers feel safe buying, but your costs go up. If the customer pays, your costs stay low, but some buyers will walk away. Many shops split the difference: the shop pays when the item is faulty or wrong, and the customer pays when they simply changed their mind. The exceptions question is about items you cannot take back. Food, opened cosmetics, and items made to order are common exceptions, and it is fair to list them, as long as you list them before the sale.

A worked example: a small clothing shop

Imagine a small clothing shop called Maple and Thread. The owner wants a return policy that is fair and simple. She chooses a return window of thirty days, because clothes are often bought as gifts and may sit in a drawer for a week before they are tried on. She asks for items to be unused, with tags still attached, because she cannot resell a shirt that has been worn and washed. She offers a refund, because she believes customers who get their money back will come back and buy again.

For shipping, she decides the customer pays, because her shop is small and free returns would eat her profit. She writes this plainly in the policy, so no customer is surprised. Her exceptions are underwear and sale items sold at half price or less. She lists both, in plain words, in the policy itself, not hidden in small print at the bottom of another page.

The finished policy is short enough to read in one minute. It says the window, the condition, the refund, the shipping, and the exceptions, each in its own line. A customer reading it knows exactly where they stand before they buy. That is the whole point of a return policy: it is not a legal shield, it is a promise, and a promise works best when it is short and clear.

Common mistakes in return policies

The first mistake is not stating a window at all. A policy that says returns are accepted gives the customer no idea whether they have a week or a year, and it gives the shop owner no protection either. Always state the number of days. The second mistake is saying no returns with no reason. Some shops do this because they fear returns, but it scares away far more buyers than it saves in returned goods. A customer who cannot return a faulty item will tell their friends, and the shop loses far more than one sale.

The third mistake is hiding the policy. Some shops bury it three clicks deep, or print it in tiny text at the bottom of the page. Customers notice, and they read it as a warning: this shop does not want you to know your rights. Put the policy where it is easy to find, ideally linked from every product page and from the checkout. The fourth mistake is writing the policy in legal language that nobody can understand. Short sentences and plain words are better than long sentences full of jargon, because a policy only works if the customer actually reads it.

The fifth mistake is copying another shop's policy word for word. Another shop sells different products, with different costs and different customers. Their policy may promise things you cannot deliver, like free return shipping on heavy items. Use a generator like this one, answer the questions honestly for your own shop, and the policy will fit.

Where to show your return policy

A return policy only builds trust if customers can find it before they buy. The best place is a link near the buy button on every product page, so the customer can check the rules in one tap. The second best place is the checkout page, where the customer is about to pay. A short line at checkout, such as free returns within thirty days, can be the last push a hesitant buyer needs.

Also put a link in the footer of your site, where customers look for it when they have a question after buying. If you sell through messages or social media, keep a copy of the policy ready to paste when a customer asks. The policy the generator makes is plain text, so it pastes cleanly into a message, a website page, or a printed notice in a physical shop. Wherever you show it, keep the wording the same everywhere, so customers never find two different versions with two different windows.

What this page cannot do

This page writes a starting template, not legal advice. Return rules differ from place to place, and some products and some kinds of sale have special rules that a simple generator cannot cover. If your shop is growing, or if you sell high value goods, have a lawyer check your policy before you rely on it. The policy this page makes is a clear, honest starting point, and for many small shops it is all they need, but it is not a substitute for professional advice.

This page also cannot enforce your policy or settle a dispute. A policy is a promise, and promises only work when both sides keep them. If a customer asks for something outside the policy, the decision is yours. The generator gives you the words; the relationship with your customer is built by how you use them.

Questions people ask

What is a return policy?

A return policy is a clear statement of the rules for sending something back: how many days the customer has, what condition the item must be in, whether they get a refund or an exchange, and who pays the return shipping.

How many days should I allow?

Thirty days is a common and generous choice, and it is what this generator uses if you leave the number blank. Fourteen days is common for small shops. Seven days is short and can put buyers off.

Who pays for return shipping?

That is your choice. Many small shops ask the customer to pay, while bigger shops often pay it themselves to win trust. A fair middle ground is for the shop to pay when the item is faulty or wrong, and the customer to pay when they simply changed their mind.

Can I refuse a return?

You can refuse a return that falls outside the rules you stated before the sale, for example an item returned after the window or an item listed as an exception. You should not refuse a return for a faulty item, and you must always follow the rules where you sell.

Should I offer refunds or exchanges?

A refund is the simplest promise and builds the most trust. An exchange suits shops where customers often want a different size or colour. Store credit keeps the sale in your shop but can frustrate a customer who wanted their money back.

Is this legal advice?

No. This page writes a starting template, not legal advice. Return rules differ from place to place, so if your shop is growing or you sell high value goods, have a lawyer check your policy before you rely on it.
