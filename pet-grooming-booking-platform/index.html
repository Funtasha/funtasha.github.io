How to use this guide

This guide is for anyone booking a groomer for the first time, and for anyone whose last booking did not go well. It explains how booking works, what to ask, how to prepare your pet, what changes the price, and the warning signs that mean you should pick a different groomer. Read it in order the first time, then use the sections as a checklist before each booking.

How grooming booking works

Most groomers take bookings by phone, and many also take them through their own website or a messaging app. The first booking usually takes longer than later ones, because the groomer needs to know about your pet: the breed or size, the coat type, the pet's age and temper, any health problems, and what you want done. A bath and brush is not the same job as a full groom, and the groomer needs to know which one you are booking before they can give you a time.

After the first call, the groomer will offer a time and tell you how long the visit takes. A bath and brush for a small dog may take an hour, while a full groom for a large long-coated dog can take three hours or more. The groomer will also tell you what to bring — usually the vaccination record — and when to arrive. Some groomers ask you to arrive a few minutes early so the pet can settle before the work starts.

On the day, you drop the pet off, confirm what you want done, and agree a pickup time. The groomer works through the job — bath, dry, brush, cut, nails, ears — and calls or messages you when the pet is ready. Payment is usually at pickup. If anything is unclear when you collect your pet, ask before you leave: a good groomer will happily explain what was done and what to watch for at home.

Questions to ask before you book

The questions you ask before booking tell you more about a groomer than any advert. A good groomer answers plainly and welcomes the questions, while a groomer who rushes you, dodges a question, or gets annoyed is telling you something too. Ask these questions on the first call, before you book, when you can still walk away.

Start with the practical questions: what is included in the groom, how long it takes, and what it costs. Then ask about the pet's safety: how many pets the groomer handles at once, whether the pet is ever left alone, and what happens if the pet gets stressed or hurt. The answers matter more than the price, because a cheap groom from a careless groomer is no bargain.

  • What is included in the groom — bath, cut, nails, ears, teeth?
  • How long will the visit take, and when do I pick up?
  • What do you need from me — vaccination record, medical notes?
  • How do you handle a nervous or frightened pet?
  • What happens if my pet is hurt or becomes very stressed?
  • Can I see where the grooming happens before I book?

Preparing your pet for the appointment

A little preparation makes the visit easier for the pet, the groomer, and you. Start with a walk or a play session before the appointment: a pet that has burned off energy is calmer on the grooming table than one that has been indoors all day. Feed the pet at the normal time, but not right before the visit — a full stomach and a grooming table do not mix well.

Bring the vaccination record. Most groomers ask for it, and some will not groom a pet without it, because grooming brings pets into close contact in a shared space. If your pet has a health problem, a sore spot, a recent injury, or a medicine it takes, tell the groomer before the visit starts — not at pickup, when it is too late to change how the pet is handled.

If this is the pet's first groom, or the pet is nervous, ask the groomer about a short first visit: a bath only, or even just a visit to the salon to sniff around and meet the groomer. A gentle first visit builds trust, and a pet that trusts the groomer is a pet that stands still, stays calm, and comes home happy. Arrive on time, stay calm yourself, and leave promptly — a long goodbye makes the pet more anxious, not less.

What changes the price

Grooming prices vary widely, and the price you pay depends on the pet, the job, and the groomer. The biggest factor is the pet itself: a large dog takes longer and uses more shampoo than a small one, and a long or matted coat takes far longer than a short smooth one. A full groom — bath, dry, cut, nails, ears — costs more than a bath and brush, because it is more work.

The second factor is condition. A coat that is brushed at home every week is quick to groom, while a coat that is matted can take twice as long, and some groomers charge extra for the extra time. This is fair: the groomer is selling time and skill, and a matted coat needs both. Regular brushing at home is the cheapest way to keep grooming costs down.

The third factor is the groomer and the area. Prices differ from place to place, and an experienced groomer with a busy book charges more than a new groomer building a client list. Ask what the price includes before you book, and compare like with like: a bath-only price and a full-groom price are not the same thing. Treat every price as a quote from that groomer, for your pet, on that day — never as a fixed rate.

Red flags: when to walk away

Most groomers are careful, kind professionals. A few are not, and the warning signs are usually visible before you book. Trust what you see and hear on the first call or visit: a groomer who will not answer questions, will not show you the grooming area, or rushes you off the phone is not a groomer to trust with your pet.

The strongest red flag is secrecy. A good groomer is happy for you to see where the work happens, happy to explain what they do, and happy to talk about how they handle a frightened pet. If a groomer refuses any of that, or if the salon looks dirty, smells bad, or has pets waiting in poor conditions, walk away and find another groomer. There are always other groomers.

  • The groomer will not show you the grooming area
  • No vaccination record is asked for
  • The price is given without asking about your pet's size or coat
  • The groomer handles many pets at once with no help
  • Your questions are rushed or brushed aside
  • The salon is dirty, or waiting pets look stressed and uncared for

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is booking the cheapest groomer without checking anything else. Price matters, but it is the last thing to compare, not the first. A groomer who is cheap because they rush, skip steps, or handle too many pets at once is not a bargain — the pet pays the difference in stress, and you may pay it in a second visit to fix a bad cut.

The second mistake is arriving without the vaccination record. Most groomers need it, and without it the booking may be refused on the day — a wasted trip for you and a wasted slot for the groomer. Keep the record somewhere you can find it, and check the expiry dates before you book, not after.

The third mistake is saying nothing when something is wrong. If the cut is not what you asked for, or the pet comes home upset, say so — calmly, and soon. A good groomer wants to know, and will usually offer to fix it. Silence helps nobody: the groomer keeps making the same mistake, and you keep paying for a groom you are not happy with.

Questions people ask

How do I book pet grooming?

Call the groomer or use their own booking page, and have your pet's details ready: breed or size, coat type, age, temper, and what you want done. The groomer will offer a time, tell you how long the visit takes, and say what to bring — usually the vaccination record. This page does not take bookings; it teaches you how to book with a real groomer.

What should I ask before booking?

Ask what the groom includes, how long it takes, what it costs, and what you need to bring. Then ask the safety questions: how the groomer handles a nervous pet, how many pets are handled at once, and what happens if the pet is hurt or stressed. A groomer who answers all of these plainly is a groomer you can trust.

How much does grooming cost?

It depends on the pet, the job and the groomer. A large dog with a long coat costs more than a small dog with a short coat, and a full groom costs more than a bath and brush. Matted coats take longer and often cost more. Ask for a quote for your pet before you book, and compare like with like.

How do I prepare my pet?

Walk or play with your pet before the visit so it arrives calm, feed it at the normal time but not right before, and bring the vaccination record. Tell the groomer about any health problem, sore spot or medicine before the work starts. For a first visit, ask about a short gentle introduction.

What if my pet is nervous?

Tell the groomer before you book — a good groomer has ways to help a nervous pet, including a short first visit, a calm space, and extra time. Arrive on time, stay calm yourself, and leave promptly, because a long goodbye makes the pet more anxious. If the pet is very frightened, ask your vet for advice before the next booking.

How do I pick a good groomer?

Look for a groomer who answers your questions plainly, asks about your pet before quoting a price, shows you the grooming area, and asks for the vaccination record. Ask other pet owners who they use, and trust what you see on a first visit. If anything feels wrong, pick a different groomer.
