Animal Facts Encyclopedia

This encyclopedia holds more than sixty true facts about animals, grouped by where they live, from the deep ocean to your own home. Every fact is a standard, widely published one, written in plain words with the animal named.

The facts here are standard, widely published facts from field guides and wildlife sources — this page does not invent records. Where a number is approximate, the fact says so, and the page is a starting point, not a full field guide.

How to use this encyclopedia

Every fact in this encyclopedia names its animal and says where the animal lives, so you can search by animal, by word, or by habitat. Type a word in the search box — try "whale", "owl" or "camel" — or pick a habitat from the list and browse what lives there. The surprise button picks one fact at random, which is a good way to start if you are just curious. Each fact is written in plain words, and where a number is approximate, the fact says so: "about 300 kilometres an hour", "around 20 hours a day".

The facts are standard, widely published facts — the kind you will find in field guides, wildlife books and nature programmes. This page does not invent records, and it does not dress up a guess as a fact. Where scientists are still unsure about something, such as why scorpions glow under ultraviolet light, the fact says so honestly. Think of this encyclopedia as a starting point: a place to find something surprising, and a reason to open a field guide and learn more.

Ocean animals

The ocean holds the largest animal that has ever lived, the blue whale, and some of the strangest: the octopus with three hearts, the seahorse father who carries the babies, the jellyfish with no brain at all. Ocean facts in this encyclopedia come from marine field guides and wildlife sources, and they share a theme — life in water works differently from life on land. Animals breathe, move, sleep and raise their young in ways that seem impossible until you read how they do it.

When you read an ocean fact, ask what problem the animal is solving. The dolphin sleeps with half its brain awake because it must keep swimming and breathing. The sea turtle returns to the beach where she hatched because it is a safe, known place to lay eggs. The coral polyp builds a reef grain by grain because a reef is a home, a shelter and a hunting ground all at once. The facts are surprising on their own, but they make more sense when you see the reason behind them.

Forest animals

Forests are crowded, dark and full of watchers, so forest animals are masters of hiding, listening and patience. The sloth hides by moving so slowly that algae grows in its fur. The fox hunts by hearing, pouncing on sounds it cannot see. The owl turns its head almost all the way around so it never has to move its body and give itself away. The facts in this group come from forest field guides and wildlife writing, and they reward slow reading — many of them are about animals that succeed by being still.

Try this with the forest group: pick three animals and compare how each one finds food. The woodpecker drums on bark, the chameleon shoots out a tongue longer than its body, and the squirrel buries nuts it will later forget. Three animals, three completely different answers to the same question. That is what makes a forest interesting — every animal has found its own way to live in the same crowded place.

Desert animals

Deserts are dry, hot by day and cold by night, and the animals that live there are experts at saving water. The kangaroo rat never drinks at all. The camel stores fat in its humps and drinks in huge gulps when it finds water. The thorny devil drinks through its skin. Desert facts in this encyclopedia come from field guides and desert wildlife sources, and almost every one of them is a story about water — how to find it, how to store it, and how to live without it.

Desert animals also show how much behaviour matters. Meerkats take turns standing guard. The sidewinder moves sideways to cross sand that would be too hot and too loose to cross any other way. The fennec fox's huge ears do two jobs at once: they release heat and they hear prey underground. When you read a desert fact, notice how often one body part solves two problems — in a hard place, nothing is wasted.

Sky animals

The sky group covers birds, bats and insects, and it holds some of the most famous records in the animal world: the fastest animal, the bird that can fly backwards, the insect that is older than the dinosaurs. The facts come from bird guides and wildlife sources, and they are worth reading with a question in mind — how does this animal stay in the air, and what does it do there that it could not do on the ground?

Flight changes everything. A swift can stay airborne for months. An albatross can cross an ocean without flapping. A bee can tell its sisters where the flowers are with a dance. Even the animals that fly at night, like bats, have their own way of seeing the world — by listening to echoes. If you only read one group in this encyclopedia, read this one twice: it is full of animals that broke the rules of what a body can do.

Farm animals

Farm animals are the animals people know best and think about least. A cow has four stomach compartments. A pig can learn its own name. A sheep can remember a face for years. The facts in this group come from farming and animal behaviour sources, and they share a quiet surprise: the animals we live closest to are more interesting than we give them credit for.

Children often enjoy the farm group most, because they can check the facts for themselves. A horse really does sleep standing up. A goat's pupils really are rectangular. A rabbit's teeth really never stop growing. If you visit a farm, take three facts from this group and look for the animals yourself — it turns a walk into a small expedition, and the facts stick far better when you have seen the animal with your own eyes.

Animals at home

The home group is about the animals that share our houses: dogs, cats, goldfish, hamsters, parrots, tortoises and the rest. These facts come from pet care guides and animal behaviour writing, and they answer the questions owners actually ask — why does my cat always land on its feet, does my goldfish remember me, why does my guinea pig jump straight up in the air?

One fact in this group corrects a very common myth: the goldfish does not forget everything in three seconds. Goldfish remember things for months and can learn simple tasks. That is the spirit of the whole encyclopedia — the truth is usually more interesting than the myth, and the animals we think we know best still have surprises in them.

Using this encyclopedia with children

Start small: pick one habitat, read three facts together, and ask the child to pick a favourite and say why. Then ask a question the fact raises — if a dolphin sleeps with half its brain awake, what does the other half do? If a squirrel forgets its nuts, how do the trees grow? Questions matter more than answers at this age, and this encyclopedia is built to raise them. Let the child press the surprise button and read the fact aloud — owning the discovery makes it stick.

For older children, turn the encyclopedia into a game. One person reads a fact without naming the animal, and the other guesses. Or pick two animals from different habitats and argue about which one is more surprising. Draw the animal from the fact, then check a picture and see what you got right. The facts are short on purpose: each one is a doorway, and the best use of a doorway is to walk through it into a book, a walk outside, or a visit to a zoo or a farm.

Common animal myths, corrected

Myths about animals spread because they are simple and memorable — and often wrong. Goldfish do not forget in three seconds. Bats are not blind; most see well and use echolocation as well. Ostriches do not bury their heads in the sand. Lemmings do not march off cliffs in great crowds. Each of these myths has a grain of truth — a goldfish's memory was once underestimated, a bat's eyes are small — but the myth grew far past the truth, and the truth is better.

How do you tell a fact from a myth? Ask where it comes from. A fact you can find in a field guide, a wildlife book or a nature programme, stated the same way in more than one place, is worth trusting. A claim that appears only in jokes, cartoons or forwarded messages is worth doubting. This encyclopedia follows that rule: every fact here is a standard, widely published one, and where the science is still open, the fact says so. That honesty is what makes the surprising facts worth believing.

Questions people ask

What is the fastest animal?

The peregrine falcon is the fastest animal on Earth — in a hunting dive it can pass 300 kilometres an hour. On land, the cheetah is fastest, reaching about 60 to 70 kilometres an hour in short bursts. In water, the sailfish is among the quickest, at around 100 kilometres an hour.

Do goldfish really forget in 3 seconds?

No — that is a myth. Goldfish remember things for months, not seconds, and they can learn simple tasks, such as swimming through a hoop for food, and recognise the people who feed them. Their memory was once underestimated, and the three-second story stuck even though it is wrong.

Which animal sleeps the most?

Koalas sleep for around 20 hours a day, which is among the longest of any animal, because eucalyptus leaves give them very little energy and take a long time to digest. Brown bats sleep for about the same length of time. At the other end, giraffes and horses sleep for only a few hours a day.

Can animals really talk to each other?

Animals communicate in rich ways — calls, songs, dances, scents and gestures — but they do not use language the way people do. Honeybees tell each other where flowers are with a waggle dance, whales sing songs that travel for kilometres, and prairie dogs use different alarm calls for different predators.

What is the biggest animal on land?

The African savanna elephant is the largest land animal, with large males weighing around six tonnes and standing about three metres tall at the shoulder. The largest animal of all, on land or in water, is the blue whale, which can weigh far more than any land animal.
