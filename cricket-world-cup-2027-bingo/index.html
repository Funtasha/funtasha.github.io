How to play cricket bingo

Cricket bingo is simple enough for anyone to join in, even if they have never watched a full one-day match before. Each person gets one card, and every square on the card names a small moment that might happen during a match: a six, a wicket, a diving stop in the field, or a review asked for by the captain. You watch the match together, and whenever one of the moments on your card happens, you mark that square. The middle square is free, so it is marked from the start.

The first person to mark a full row, a full column, or a full diagonal line calls out bingo and wins the round. If two people finish at the same moment, share the win and start a fresh card for the next match. There is no need to keep score across the whole tournament unless your group wants to: a simple tally of wins on a sheet of paper is enough to crown a family bingo champion by the time the final is played on 21 November 2027.

Because every card is different, nobody has an advantage. The generator shuffles a pool of cricket moments and picks twenty-four of them for each card, so even people sitting side by side will be watching for different things. That is part of the fun: one person is waiting for a stumping while another is hoping for a century, and everyone has a reason to watch the quiet overs as closely as the big moments.

What is on the cards

Every card holds twenty-four cricket moments plus the free centre square. The moments are the everyday happenings of a one-day match: boundaries and wickets, appeals and reviews, diving stops and direct hits, new balls and drinks breaks. Nothing on the card depends on which teams are playing, so the same cards work for every match of the tournament, from the opening games on 2 October 2027 to the final on 21 November 2027.

The moments are deliberately small and frequent. A card full of rare events would leave everyone staring at empty squares, so most of the moments happen several times in a normal match. A four, a wide, a diving stop, a batter tapping the bat on the ground: these are the bread and butter of cricket, and they keep the game moving. A few rarer squares, such as a century or a run out, add the excitement of a long wait that might pay off at any second.

Because the cards never name a team, a player, or a score, they stay fair and friendly for every group. Nobody needs to know the squads, and nobody is put on the spot for supporting the wrong side. The game is about watching the match closely and enjoying the little moments that make cricket fun, whatever the result turns out to be.

Bingo for a family watch party

A watch party is the perfect place for bingo, because it gives everyone in the room a job to do. Younger children who might drift away from a seven-hour match suddenly have a card to watch over, and grandparents who rarely follow cricket can join in without needing to know what lbw means. Give each person a card before the first ball, hand out pens or small counters for marking, and agree the prize before the match starts.

Keep the prizes light and free: the winner picks the snack for the next match, chooses the music at the innings break, or gets to pick which match the family watches next. Avoid anything that costs money or turns a friendly game into a competition with stakes. The point of bingo night is to laugh together, not to keep a ledger.

If you have a big group, print one card per person and let the generator make a different card each time. If you are short on paper, play on phones and tablets instead: tap a square on the screen to mark it, just as you would cross it off on paper. Either way, read the squares aloud before the first ball so younger players know what to watch for, and help them spot the first few moments so they feel part of the game from the start.

Bingo for a classroom or cricket club

Teachers and club coaches can use bingo to turn a match into a gentle learning activity. Before the game, talk through the squares and explain the cricket words on them: what a maiden over is, what lbw means, and why a bowler changes ends. Children learn the vocabulary of the game by watching for it, which is far more memorable than reading a list.

The tournament itself is a good starting point for wider learning. The 2027 World Cup is hosted by South Africa, Zimbabwe, and Namibia, and the twelve venues stretch from Cape Town in the south to Victoria Falls in the north. A quick look at a map, a few facts about the host countries, and a discussion about teamwork and fair play can turn a fun game into a morning of learning that children actually enjoy.

For a group, print a stack of cards in advance and hand them out at random. Agree the rules clearly before the first ball: one card per person, no swapping cards, and the first completed line wins. If the group is large, play for a full house instead of a line, or run two rounds with a fresh card each time so more children get a turn at winning.

The tournament in brief

The Cricket World Cup 2027 runs from Saturday 2 October to Sunday 21 November 2027, hosted by South Africa, Zimbabwe, and Namibia. Fourteen teams play fifty-seven matches across twelve venues, in the fifty-over one-day format. It is the first Cricket World Cup in Africa since 2003, and the first ever for Namibia.

The format has four stages. First, the Super Series: the three lowest-ranked qualifiers play a round robin in Namibia, and the winner advances. Then the group stage: twelve teams in two groups of six, with Group A made up of India, Australia, Pakistan, Afghanistan, Zimbabwe, and Qualifier A, and Group B made up of New Zealand, South Africa, Sri Lanka, England, Bangladesh, and Qualifier B.

Then comes the Super 7: the top three in each group plus the best fourth-placed team play a round robin of twenty-one matches, and the top four reach the semi-finals, in Cape Town on 17 November and Centurion on 18 November. The final is on 21 November 2027. Australia are the defending champions, after beating India in the 2023 final in Ahmedabad, and they have won the tournament six times.

Cricket words on the cards

If you are new to cricket, a few of the squares may need explaining, and that is part of the fun. A four is scored when the ball reaches the boundary after bouncing, and a six when it clears the boundary without bouncing. A maiden over is an over of six balls in which no runs are scored. A fifty is fifty runs by one batter, and a century is one hundred.

A wicket can fall in several ways. Bowled means the ball hit the stumps. Caught means a fielder caught the ball before it bounced. Run out means the fielding side broke the stumps while the batters were running. Stumped means the wicketkeeper broke the stumps while the batter was out of their crease. Lbw, which stands for leg before wicket, means the ball would have hit the stumps but for the batter's body being in the way.

A wide is a ball too far from the batter to hit, and a no ball is a ball bowled illegally, usually because the bowler overstepped the line. A free hit follows a no ball: the batter cannot be out except by run out, so they usually swing hard. A review is when a team asks the third umpire to check a decision, using television replays. Knowing these words makes every match more enjoyable, and bingo is a cheerful way to learn them.

Tips for a fair game

A little preparation keeps bingo fair and fun for everyone. First, agree the winning line before the first ball: a row, a column, or a diagonal, or a full house if your group prefers a longer game. Second, agree what counts: a square is marked only when the moment clearly happens, and if the group disagrees, the majority decides. Third, one card per person, and no peeking at other people's cards to block them.

Fourth, decide the tie-breaker in advance. If two people complete a line on the same moment, the fairest answer is a shared win, but some groups prefer a quick decider, such as who marked the free centre square first, or a simple coin toss. Whatever you choose, decide it before the match starts, not in the heat of the moment.

Finally, remember that bingo is a side game, not the main event. If a square is missed because everyone was cheering a wicket, that is fine: mark it late, or let it go. The match is the star of the day, and the card is there to make watching it more fun, not to turn the living room into a courtroom.

Questions people ask

Do I need to print the bingo card?

No. You can play on a phone or tablet by tapping a square to mark it, or you can print the card and mark it with a pen. Both ways work, so use whichever suits your group and your printer.

Can children play cricket bingo?

Yes. Bingo is suitable for all ages, and younger children enjoy it most when an adult reads the squares aloud before the first ball and helps them spot the first few moments. The game needs no cricket knowledge to start.

How many squares are on each card?

Each card has twenty-five squares in a five by five grid: twenty-four cricket moments drawn at random from a pool, plus a free centre square that is already marked when the game starts.

What counts as a win?

The usual win is a completed row, column, or diagonal line of five marked squares. Your group can also agree to play for a full house, where every square must be marked before someone wins.

When is the Cricket World Cup 2027?

The tournament runs from Saturday 2 October to Sunday 21 November 2027 in South Africa, Zimbabwe, and Namibia. The semi-finals are on 17 and 18 November, and the final is on 21 November.

Do the bingo cards name real teams or players?

No. The cards use general cricket moments, such as a six or a wicket, so they work for every match and never depend on which teams are playing or who is in a squad.
