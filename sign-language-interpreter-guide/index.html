Sign Language Interpreter Guide

A sign language interpreter turns spoken words into sign language and back again, so deaf and hearing people can talk together. This guide explains what interpreters do, how to find a qualified one, what to ask before booking, and how to work well together. It names no individual interpreters, because the right choice depends on your country, your language and your event.

What this is and who needs it

A sign language interpreter is a trained professional who converts spoken language into sign language and sign language into spoken language, in real time. They make conversation possible between deaf people who use sign language and hearing people who do not. They work in meetings, classrooms, hospitals, courtrooms, job interviews, weddings, funerals and public events: anywhere two people need to understand each other and one of them signs.

This guide is for anyone who needs to book an interpreter or work with one: employers, teachers, event organisers, healthcare staff, lawyers, family members and community groups. It explains what interpreters do, how to find a qualified one, what to ask before booking, how to work together well, and what it usually costs. It names no individual interpreters and lists no agencies, because the right choice depends on your country, your sign language and your event. Sign languages differ by country and region, just as spoken languages do, so an interpreter qualified in one sign language cannot simply work in another.

Interpreters are not helpers or volunteers. They are skilled professionals who train for years, follow a code of ethics, and deserve to be booked and paid properly. Treating them as professionals from the first contact sets the tone for a booking that works well for everyone, most of all for the deaf person whose conversation depends on them.

How it works, step by step

Booking an interpreter starts with the deaf person. Ask them which sign language they use, whether they have a preferred interpreter, and what the setting will be. A medical appointment needs different skills from a wedding, and a court hearing needs different skills from a classroom. The deaf person's preference matters: a good match between interpreter and client makes the whole conversation smoother.

Next, find a qualified interpreter through the official professional body or register in your country. These bodies list interpreters who have met their standards, and they can usually point you to interpreters who work in your area and your setting. Contact the interpreter or the booking service with the details: the date, time and place, the kind of event, the sign language needed, how long the session will last, and whether any preparation material is available. For sessions longer than about an hour, two interpreters usually take turns, because interpreting is intensely tiring work and accuracy falls when one person works too long alone.

Before the event, share your material with the interpreter: slides, an agenda, a list of names and specialist terms, and any handouts. An interpreter who has seen the material in advance will do a far better job than one who meets it cold. Agree the fee, the travel arrangements and the cancellation terms in writing. On the day, introduce the interpreter to everyone, seat them where they can see the speaker and be seen by the deaf person, and let them get on with their work. Speak directly to the deaf person, not to the interpreter, and speak at a normal pace in short, clear sentences.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Fees for interpreting vary widely by country, by the length of the booking and by how specialist the work is, so this guide quotes no figure. Most interpreters charge by the hour or by the session, with a minimum booking length, and travel is usually charged separately. Specialist work, such as legal or medical interpreting, usually costs more than a straightforward meeting. Evening, weekend and short-notice bookings may also cost more. Always get the full fee in writing before the event, including what you pay if you cancel or if the event runs over time.

Time matters as much as money. Book as early as you can, especially for specialist events or busy dates. A few weeks ahead is a sensible minimum for a straightforward meeting; conferences, legal work and medical appointments may need longer. Late booking narrows your choice and may leave you with an interpreter who is not the best match for the job. If you organise events regularly, build a relationship with a booking service or a small group of interpreters so you are not starting from scratch each time.

Check whether the deaf person has a preferred interpreter or any access needs of their own, such as a seat near the front or a clear view of the stage. Check too who is paying: in many countries an employer, a school, a hospital or a government scheme pays for interpreting, and the rules differ by place. Find out early so the booking is not delayed by a dispute over the bill.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is booking too late. Interpreters are in demand, and the best ones are booked weeks ahead. A last-minute booking may find no one at all, or an interpreter who is not qualified for your setting. Book early, and confirm the booking a few days before the event. The second mistake is booking an unqualified person because they know some signs. Knowing a little sign language is not the same as interpreting: a trained interpreter manages meaning, tone, cultural context and confidentiality, and an untrained volunteer can misunderstand badly at the worst moment.

The third mistake is talking to the interpreter instead of the deaf person. Say you are welcome to the deaf person, not tell them they are welcome to the interpreter. The interpreter is a bridge, not a participant, and speaking through them as if the deaf person were not there is rude and confusing. The fourth mistake is forgetting breaks. Interpreting demands total concentration, and accuracy falls sharply after an hour without rest. Plan breaks, and book two interpreters for long sessions.

The fifth mistake is giving the interpreter no preparation material. An interpreter who meets your specialist terms for the first time in the room will struggle, however skilled they are. Share slides, agendas and word lists in advance. The sixth mistake is poor seating: an interpreter hidden behind a plant, in dim light, or facing away from the speaker cannot do the job. Give them a clear view, good light, and a place where the deaf person can see them easily.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this list every time you book an interpreter. It covers the steps that matter most.

  • Ask the deaf person which sign language they use and whether they have a preferred interpreter.
  • Check the interpreter's qualifications with the official professional body or register in your country.
  • Confirm the date, time, place, length and kind of event in writing.
  • Agree the full fee, travel costs and cancellation terms before the event.
  • Book two interpreters for sessions longer than about an hour.
  • Share slides, agendas, names and specialist terms with the interpreter in advance.
  • Plan breaks into the schedule and tell the interpreter when they will be.
  • Seat the interpreter where they can see the speaker and be seen by the deaf person, in good light.
  • Speak directly to the deaf person at a normal pace, in short, clear sentences.
  • After the event, thank the interpreter and note what worked well for next time.

If anything on this list is missing, fix it before the event, not during it. A booking that is prepared well runs itself; a booking that is not prepared will show it in the first five minutes.

Tips from real practice

Learn a little about the sign language used in your country, even if you never learn to sign fluently. Knowing that sign languages are full languages with their own grammar, and that they differ by country, changes how you think about interpreting. It also helps you avoid the common error of assuming that one interpreter can work in any sign language. If you work with deaf people regularly, a basic sign language course is one of the best investments you can make.

Build a relationship with the interpreters you use. A regular interpreter who knows your organisation, your people and your vocabulary will do better work than a stranger, however qualified. Keep notes on what worked: which interpreter suited which setting, what preparation helped, and what the deaf person thought of the session. Share those notes with colleagues so the knowledge does not leave when you do.

Finally, remember who the interpreting is for. It is easy, in the rush of organising, to treat the interpreter as the main task and forget the deaf person at the centre of it. Ask them how it went, listen to the answer, and adjust next time. The best measure of a good booking is not that it ran on time but that the deaf person left feeling fully part of the conversation. That is the standard worth aiming for, every time.

Questions people ask

What does a sign language interpreter do?

A sign language interpreter converts spoken language into sign language and sign language into spoken language, in real time. They work in meetings, classrooms, hospitals, courtrooms and public events, and they follow a professional code that includes confidentiality and neutrality. They translate meaning, not just words, including tone, emphasis and cultural context.

How do I find a qualified interpreter?

Start with the official professional body or register for interpreters in your country, which lists interpreters who have met its standards. Ask the deaf person involved who they prefer to work with, because a good match matters. Avoid booking anyone whose qualifications you cannot check, and be wary of anyone who offers to interpret without training.

What should I ask before booking one?

Ask which sign language they work in, what qualifications they hold, and whether they have experience with your kind of event. Ask about fees, travel, breaks for long sessions, and what happens if the event runs late. Ask too whether they need any preparation material, such as slides or a list of specialist terms.

How far ahead should I book?

Book as early as you can, especially for specialist events or dates when demand is high. A few weeks ahead is a sensible minimum for a straightforward meeting; conferences and legal or medical work may need longer. Late booking narrows your choice and may leave you with an interpreter who is not the best match for the job.

What does interpreting usually cost?

Fees vary widely by country, by the length of the booking and by how specialist the work is, so this guide quotes no figure. Most interpreters charge by the hour or by the session, with a minimum booking length, and travel is usually charged separately. Always get the full fee in writing before the event, including what you pay if you cancel.

How should I work with an interpreter at an event?

Speak directly to the deaf person, not to the interpreter, and speak at a normal pace in short, clear sentences. Give the interpreter a good view of the speaker and the room, allow breaks during long sessions, and share your material in advance. Two interpreters usually take turns for sessions longer than about an hour, so plan for that.
