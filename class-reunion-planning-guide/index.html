Class Reunion Planning Guide

This guide walks you through planning a class reunion, from forming a committee and finding classmates to the venue, program, memory display, shared costs and photos. Use the checklist to keep track of every task, and pick a committee role to see what it involves.

Form a small planning committee

A reunion starts with a small group of willing classmates — three to five people is plenty. Each person takes a role: one finds classmates, one handles the venue, one plans the program, and one keeps track of the money. Short, regular check-ins keep the plan moving, and a shared list of tasks stops jobs from being forgotten.

Start planning months ahead, and agree the big decisions early: the date, the venue and the size of the event. A committee that decides quickly and communicates clearly will keep the whole reunion on track.

Find your classmates

Finding classmates is the biggest job, and it takes patience. Start with the people you already know, and ask each of them for the names and contact details of two more. School networks, alumni groups and social media all help, and a simple message — who you are, what you are planning, and how to reply — is all it takes to start.

Keep a single list of everyone you find, with a note of how to reach them. Some classmates will be easy to find and some will take months, so start early and celebrate every name you add. Even a reunion with a modest turnout is a joy when the people who come are glad to be there.

Choose a venue

A school hall is a wonderful venue for a reunion — walking back through the old corridors is half the fun. A restaurant, community hall or garden venue works well too, especially for a larger group. Whichever you choose, check the practical details: enough seating, a table for the memory display, somewhere quiet for conversation, and parking or transport nearby.

Book the venue as soon as the date is set, and confirm what it provides — tables, chairs, a kitchen and a sound system save a lot of work. Visit the venue if you can, and picture the day there before you commit.

Shape the program

A simple program keeps the reunion flowing without feeling stiff. Welcome everyone, share food and conversation, then gather for a short round of memories — a teacher's welcome, a few stories from classmates, and a group photo. Two or three hours is plenty, and a printed running order helps the host keep things on track.

Leave generous gaps between the set moments. Reunions are full of conversations that have waited years to happen, and people need time to talk. A program that leaves room for catching up is better than one packed with activities.

Set up a memory display

A memory display is the heart of a reunion. Gather old photos, a yearbook if you have one, and a timeline of the school years, and arrange them along a table with short captions. Ask classmates to send a photo or a memory in advance, and print the best ones.

Leave a pen and a notebook beside the display so guests can add a memory or a message. It becomes a keepsake for the class, and it costs almost nothing to set up. A display of then-and-now photos — school days beside today — always gets people talking.

Share the costs fairly

Reunion costs are usually shared among everyone who comes. Work out the total for the venue, food and decorations, then divide it by the number of guests and tell people the amount early, so nobody is surprised. Keep the amount modest so nobody is kept away by it, and be open about what the money covers.

One person should keep track of the money, with a simple list of what comes in and what goes out. If there is money left over, agree in advance what happens to it — a donation to the school or a small fund for the next reunion are both kind options.

Photos on the day

Ask one person to be the photographer, so the committee can enjoy the day. A group photo of everyone who came is the picture that matters most, so gather people for it midway through the event, when everyone has arrived. A second person taking candid photos catches the conversations and the laughter.

After the reunion, share the photos with everyone who came — and with classmates who could not make it. A shared album or a simple message with the photos keeps the class connected until the next reunion.

Questions people ask

How far ahead should we start planning a reunion?

Months ahead is best, especially for finding classmates. Start with the committee and the date, then work through the list.

How do we find classmates we have lost touch with?

Start with the people you know and ask each of them for two more contacts. School networks, alumni groups and social media all help.

How do we share the costs?

Work out the total for the venue, food and decorations, divide it by the number of guests, and tell people early. Keep the amount modest.

What if only a few people can come?

A small reunion is still a joy. The people who come are the ones who wanted to be there, and that matters more than the number.

Should families be invited to a class reunion?

That is the committee's call. Some reunions are classmates only, and others welcome partners and children — decide early and say so clearly.
