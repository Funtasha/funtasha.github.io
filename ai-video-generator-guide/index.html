By the Funtasha team · Published 20 November 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026

A text-to-video generator turns a few written words into a moving video. This guide shows how it works, how to write a video prompt, and what to make first.

Never use AI video to pretend a real person said or did something they did not. Keep it fun, keep it honest, and say it is AI-made when you share it.

What text-to-video is

Text-to-video is what it sounds like: you type a few words, and an AI tool turns them into a moving video. A lighthouse on a rocky coast, waves rolling in, clouds drifting overhead — a scene that would take a film crew a day to shoot appears in under a minute, from a single sentence.

The tools read your prompt the way an image tool reads one, but they have an extra job: every frame has to follow from the one before it. The lighthouse must stay a lighthouse as the camera moves, the waves must keep rolling in the same direction, and the light must not jump from noon to sunset halfway through. That is why video is harder than pictures, and why the results are more surprising when they work.

Most people start with a short clip of a single scene — a place, an animal, a moment — and that is exactly the right place to begin. Short and simple beats long and complicated, every time.

How a video prompt differs from a photo prompt

A photo prompt describes a still moment. A video prompt describes a moment that moves, so it needs two extra things: the movement and the camera.

Movement is what happens in the scene. Waves roll in, a cat leaps, a train crosses a bridge. Name one clear movement and the tool can build the clip around it. Name three movements at once and the clip often turns into a muddle, because the tool has to decide which one matters most.

Camera is how the scene is filmed. A slow zoom draws the eye toward the subject. A pan sweeps across a landscape. An orbit circles the scene and shows it from every side. One camera move per clip is the rule — a clip that zooms and pans and orbits at the same time usually ends up wobbly.

Everything else is the same as a photo prompt: the subject, the style, the light, and the mood. The builder at the top of this page puts all of it together — pick a scene, a camera move and a mood, and it assembles a video prompt you can copy.

The tools people use

Three tools come up again and again when people talk about AI video, and they are the only ones this guide names, because they are the ones verified in the sources.

Google Flow is Google's AI filmmaking tool, and it is surging: about 823,000 monthly searches, up 809% in a year. It is built around scenes and camera moves, so it suits people who want to plan a little film rather than a single clip.

Sora is known for smooth, cinematic clips from a short description. It is a good first stop when you want a single beautiful scene without much planning.

CapCut is the editor of the three: many people generate a clip in one tool and shape it in CapCut, trimming, adding music and text, and stitching short clips into something longer. Generating and editing are different jobs, and CapCut is where the second one happens.

Many free AI tools also make video, with limits on length or on how many clips you can generate. Check the tool's own pages before you start — some need an account, and some charge for longer clips or higher quality.

What makes a good first video

Your first video should be short, simple, and about one thing. Pick a scene you can picture clearly, give it one movement, and let the tool do the rest.

  • One scene. A lighthouse, a beach, a garden. One place, one moment. Scenes that change halfway through are for later.
  • One movement. Waves rolling in, leaves falling, a cat stretching. One movement the tool can repeat smoothly.
  • One camera move. A slow zoom or a gentle pan. The camera should be almost boring — the scene is the star.
  • A clear mood. Calm, dreamy, playful. Mood words guide the light and the pace, and they cost you nothing to add.

Run the prompt, watch the clip twice, and then change one thing — the mood, or the camera move — and run it again. The comparison teaches you what each word does, and your second video will be better than your first.

Common failures and how to prompt around them

AI video fails in recognisable ways, and each one has a way around it.

Melting faces. Faces are the hardest thing in video, because they have to stay the same across every frame. Keep faces small in the frame, or pick a scene with no face at all, until you know what your tool handles well.

Extra fingers and limbs. Hands and feet confuse video tools just as they confuse image tools. Ask for a wide shot rather than a close-up, and avoid prompts that put hands in the centre of the frame.

Wobbly motion. A clip that shakes or warps usually has too many instructions — too many movements, too many camera moves, or a scene that changes too fast. Simplify: one movement, one camera move, one scene.

Objects that appear and vanish. A cup on a table may be there in one frame and gone in the next. Keep the scene simple and the objects few, and the tool has less to keep track of.

Video is the fastest-moving part of AI media

Images had their moment first, and video is having its moment now. The tools improve visibly from month to month: clips get smoother, faces hold together longer, and the prompts that worked last year look dated beside the ones that work today.

That speed is good news for beginners, because it means the tools are forgiving and the learning curve is short. It also means that what you learn matters more than which tool you learn it on — the habits of a good video prompt, one scene, one movement, one camera move, will still be true when the tools have changed again.

It also means you should not wait for the perfect tool. The best time to make your first video is now, with whatever tool you have, because every clip you make teaches you something the next one will use.

Sharing your first videos

When a clip comes out well, share it — but share it honestly. Say it is AI-made, in the caption or the description, so nobody mistakes it for real footage. That matters most with anything that looks like a real place or a real person: an AI lighthouse is a delight, an AI clip passed off as a real event is a deception.

Short clips suit chats, comments and party pages, where people watch with the sound off and move on quickly. A clip that works without sound — clear movement, a strong mood — will travel further than one that needs explaining.

And keep your prompts. When a clip works, save the exact words that made it, just as you would with a picture prompt. Your second film will be easier than your first, because you will already know which words your tool listens to.

Questions people ask

What is a text-to-video generator?

A text-to-video generator is an AI tool that turns a written description into a moving video. You describe a scene, a movement and a mood, and the tool builds a short clip frame by frame, keeping the scene consistent as the camera moves.

How long can an AI video be?

Most AI video tools make short clips, and the exact limit depends on the tool and the plan you are on, so check the tool's own pages for the current limit. Short clips are also the best place to start — one scene, one movement, one camera move.

What should a video prompt include?

A good video prompt names the scene, one clear movement, one camera move, and a mood. For example: a lighthouse on a rocky coast, waves rolling in, slow zoom, calm mood. The builder at the top of this page assembles prompts in exactly that shape.

Why do AI videos look wobbly?

Wobble usually comes from too many instructions at once — several movements, several camera moves, or a scene that changes too fast. Simplify the prompt to one movement and one camera move, and keep the scene simple, so the tool has less to keep track of.

Can I make a video for free?

Many AI video tools offer a free tier with limits on how many clips you can make or how long they can be. Check the tool's own pages before you start — some need an account, and some charge for longer clips or higher quality.

Can I use AI video on social media?

Yes, if it is your own creation — just say it is AI-made when you share it, so nobody mistakes it for real footage. Never use AI video to pretend a real person said or did something they did not, and check the platform's rules before you post.
