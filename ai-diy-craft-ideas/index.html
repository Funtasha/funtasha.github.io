AI Craft Ideas: DIY Projects for Rainy Days

By the Funtasha team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

Rainy days are perfect for making things, and AI can help you dream up projects matched to the ages around your table. This guide gives you twelve craft projects in three age groups, each with a materials list and simple steps, plus prompt patterns for inventing more.

A rainy day with children can feel long, but a craft table changes everything. Paper, glue, paint, and a few household odds and ends are enough for hours of making, and the best projects are the ones children can finish and feel proud of.

AI can help you plan the afternoon. Describe the ages, the materials you have, and the time you can spare, and it will suggest projects that fit. This guide starts you off with twelve tried-and-true ideas, four for each age group, and shows you how to invent your own.

Craft safety first: young children need blunt scissors and washable glue, and an adult should handle sharp scissors, hot glue, and craft knives. Keep small parts, beads, and buttons away from children under three, and cover the table before paint comes out.

Setting up a craft table

A good craft table needs three things: a covered surface, materials within reach, and a place for finished work to dry. An old tablecloth, a plastic sheet, or even opened-out newspaper will protect the table. Put the materials in the middle where everyone can reach them, and keep a damp cloth nearby for sticky fingers.

Keep a basic craft box ready for rainy days. Paper in a few colours, child-safe scissors, glue sticks, crayons, markers, tape, string, and a few cardboard boxes will cover most projects. Add paint, buttons, beads, and googly eyes for older children, and keep them in separate pots so the table stays tidy.

Start each session by choosing one project and laying out only what it needs. Too many materials at once can overwhelm young children and slow older ones down. When the project is done, tidy together, then choose the next one, or let the children invent something freeform with the leftovers.

Little hands: ages 3 to 5

Young children need projects with big pieces, simple steps, and room for mess. The goal is the doing, not a perfect result, so praise the effort and let the colours and shapes go where they will.

  1. Paper plate sunshine. Materials: paper plate, yellow paint, glue, strips of yellow and orange paper. Steps: paint the plate yellow, glue paper strips around the edge as rays, and draw a smiling face in the middle.
  2. Sponge print garden. Materials: kitchen sponge cut into simple shapes, washable paint, large paper. Steps: dip the sponge shapes in paint, press them onto the paper to make flowers and leaves, and add stems with a crayon.
  3. Cardboard tube animals. Materials: empty cardboard tubes, coloured paper, glue, crayons. Steps: wrap a tube in coloured paper, glue on paper ears and a tail, and draw a face. Try a cat, a dog, or a rabbit.
  4. Torn paper collage. Materials: coloured paper, glue stick, a sheet of card. Steps: tear the paper into small pieces, sort them by colour, and glue them onto the card to make a picture, a rainbow, or a pattern.

Growing makers: ages 6 to 9

Children in this age group can follow several steps, use scissors with care, and enjoy projects that take a little longer. Give them choices within each project, such as colours or patterns, so the result feels like theirs.

  1. Paper lanterns. Materials: coloured paper, scissors, glue, string. Steps: fold a sheet of paper in half, cut slits from the fold almost to the edge, unfold it, roll it into a tube, and glue the edges. Add a string handle and hang it up.
  2. Friendship bracelets. Materials: embroidery thread or yarn in three colours, tape. Steps: knot three strands together, tape the knot to the table, and braid the strands. Tie the ends and trim, then swap bracelets with a friend.
  3. Cardboard castle. Materials: cardboard boxes, scissors, glue, paint, paper. Steps: cut a large box into castle walls with towers, glue on paper flags and windows, and paint the stonework. Add toy figures to play with inside.
  4. Nature weaving frame. Materials: a forked stick or cardboard frame, string, leaves and flowers. Steps: wrap string around the frame to make a loom, then weave leaves, flowers, and grass through the strings to make a natural wall hanging.

Confident crafters: ages 10 and up

Older children can handle finer work, longer projects, and a little more independence. These projects take an hour or more and produce something worth keeping or giving as a gift.

  1. Hand-stitched felt pouch. Materials: felt sheets, needle, thread, buttons, scissors. Steps: cut two matching felt shapes, stitch the edges together with a simple running stitch, add a button and a loop for a fastening, and decorate with small felt shapes.
  2. Paper star mobile. Materials: stiff coloured paper, scissors, string, a stick or hoop. Steps: fold and cut paper stars in three sizes, thread them onto strings at different lengths, and tie the strings to a stick or hoop to hang.
  3. Decorated photo frame. Materials: a plain wooden or cardboard frame, paint, shells, buttons, or dried flowers, glue. Steps: paint the frame, arrange the decorations, glue them in place, and let it dry before adding a favourite photo.
  4. Miniature cardboard room. Materials: a shoebox, coloured paper, small boxes, glue, scissors. Steps: turn the shoebox on its side to make a room, paper the walls, and build tiny furniture from small boxes and paper. Add a paper rug and tiny pictures on the walls.

Inventing more projects with AI

When you have finished the twelve projects above, AI can invent more to fit your exact situation. A good prompt names the ages, the materials you have, the time available, and the kind of project you want. For example: suggest three craft projects for two children aged seven and nine, using only paper, glue, and scissors, each taking under an hour.

Ask for a materials list and numbered steps for each idea, and ask the AI to flag anything that needs adult help. If an idea uses something you do not have, ask for a swap: what can we use instead of paint, or instead of googly eyes? The AI will usually offer a household alternative.

You can also ask for themed projects: crafts for a birthday, for a season, or for a school topic. A theme gives the afternoon a shape and makes the finished pieces feel like a set, which children love to display together.

Displaying and gifting what you make

Finished crafts deserve a place to be seen. A string with pegs across a wall makes a simple gallery, and a shelf or a windowsill works for three-dimensional pieces. Rotate the display when new work arrives, and keep a photo of each piece before it is given away or recycled.

Handmade crafts make lovely gifts. A felt pouch, a decorated frame, or a paper star mobile can be wrapped in brown paper with a hand-drawn card, and the gift will mean more than anything bought. Let the child choose who receives it, and help them write a short note to go with it.

Finally, keep a craft journal. A notebook with a photo or a sketch of each project, plus a line about what the child enjoyed, becomes a lovely record of rainy days spent making things together.

Questions people ask

What materials should I keep for rainy-day crafts?

Paper in a few colours, child-safe scissors, glue sticks, crayons, markers, tape, string, and cardboard boxes cover most projects. Add paint, buttons, and beads for older children.

How do I keep crafts safe for young children?

Use blunt scissors and washable glue for under-fives, keep small parts away from children under three, and let an adult handle sharp tools, hot glue, and craft knives.

How long should a craft session last?

One project of thirty to sixty minutes suits most children. Younger children may manage twenty minutes, while older children can happily spend an hour or more on a detailed project.

What if a project does not turn out as planned?

That is part of crafting. Praise the effort, ask what the child likes about their piece, and let them change the plan. A wonky lantern is still a lantern, and it is theirs.

Can AI suggest crafts using only what I have at home?

Yes. List the materials you have, the ages of the children, and the time available, and ask for projects that use only those materials, with steps and a materials list.
