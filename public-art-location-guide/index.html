Public Art Location Guide

Public art is the sculpture, mural and installation you meet on streets, in parks and on building walls, free for anyone to see. This guide explains what it is, how to find it in any city, how to read a work when you find it, and how to visit it respectfully.

What this is and who needs it

Public art is art made for a public place: a sculpture in a square, a mural on a building wall, an installation in a park, a memorial on a street corner. Unlike the art in a museum, it needs no ticket, no opening hours and no permission to see. You meet it on the way to work, on a walk with friends, or on a visit to a city you have never seen before, and it is part of what makes a place feel like itself.

This guide is for anyone who wants to find and enjoy public art. You may be planning a trip and want to know what to look for in a new city. You may live somewhere and have walked past the same sculpture for years without knowing what it is. You may be a student, a photographer, a teacher planning a walk, or simply someone who likes looking up. Whatever brings you to it, public art rewards a little curiosity: every work has a maker, a reason for being there, and a story.

Public art takes many forms. There are the great civic sculptures that become symbols of their cities: the Little Mermaid on the waterfront in Copenhagen, the Angel of the North standing over the road near Gateshead in England, and Cloud Gate, the polished steel bean that reflects the skyline in Millennium Park in Chicago. There are murals painted on the sides of buildings, like the long painted stretch of the Berlin Wall that survives as the East Side Gallery. And there are thousands of smaller works — a mosaic in a subway, a carved bench, a statue in a market square — that belong to their neighbourhoods and are loved by the people who live near them.

How to find public art in a city, step by step

Finding public art is mostly a matter of walking, looking and asking. Start before you arrive, and keep your eyes open once you are there.

  1. Check the city before you go. Many cities publish an art trail, a public art map or a list of works on the official city or tourism page. Search for the city's name together with public art, sculpture trail or mural walk, and save the places that interest you. Check whether any work you want to see is inside a building with opening hours.
  2. Ask at the visitor centre. Visitor centres and local libraries often know about works that no guidebook lists: the mosaic in a housing estate, the sculpture in a hospital garden, the mural down a side street. Ask what is new, and ask what local people love.
  3. Walk the busy places first. Squares, parks, waterfronts, railway stations, university campuses and market streets hold most of a city's public art. Works are placed where people gather, so follow the crowds and look at the centre of every open space.
  4. Look up and look down. Murals live on upper walls, sculptures stand on rooftops and plinths, and mosaics are set into pavements and subway walls. The best public art is often above eye level or under your feet, and easy to miss if you only look straight ahead.
  5. Read the plaque. Most public works carry a small plaque with the artist's name, the title and the date, and often a line about why the work was made. Read it, note the name, and photograph the plaque as well as the work: it is the quickest way to learn more later.
  6. Follow one work to the next. Artists, styles and themes repeat across a city. If you like one mural, ask who painted it and where else their work stands. If a sculpture moves you, look for the artist's other pieces. One good find leads to another.

Give yourself time. A sculpture seen in a hurry is a shape; a sculpture seen slowly is a work of art. Walk around it, look at it from far away and from close up, and notice how it changes with the light and with the people moving past it. Public art is made to be lived with, and it repays a second look.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Public art is one of the cheapest pleasures a city offers: most works stand outdoors and are free to see at any hour. The costs are the ordinary costs of a day out — travel, food, and perhaps an entry fee if a work stands inside a museum, a station or a building that charges. A walking route costs nothing but shoe leather, and a self-guided trail is as good as a paid tour if you have done a little reading first.

Allow half a day for a first art walk in a new city, and a full day if you want to see works spread across different neighbourhoods. Wear comfortable shoes, carry water, and take a camera or a phone with a charged battery. A small notebook is useful for artist names and titles, and a paper map is a good backup when your phone loses signal among tall buildings.

Check access before you travel, especially for works inside buildings, in parks that close at night, or in areas under construction. Public art changes: works are moved, covered for restoration, or removed altogether, and a trail published a few years ago may no longer match the street. The official city or venue page is the most reliable place to check, and a quick check the day before you go can save a wasted journey.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

Public art belongs to everyone, and that brings responsibilities. The mistakes below are the ones that spoil the work for other people, or spoil the visit for you.

  • Touching or climbing on works. Bronze, stone and paint are damaged by hands, feet and weather, and a sculpture is not a climbing frame. Unless a sign clearly says you may touch or sit on a work, look with your eyes. Children need the same rule, explained kindly.
  • Blocking the way for a photograph. A sculpture in a busy square is also a thoroughfare. Step aside for other people, keep your bag out of the path, and take your photograph quickly when the spot is crowded.
  • Assuming every wall painting is public art. A mural is usually commissioned or permitted by the building owner or the city. Graffiti is usually painted without permission. Both can be art, and the line between them can blur, but only a mural is meant to stay: photograph graffiti while it is there, and do not be surprised if it is gone next month.
  • Trusting an old trail. Murals are painted over, sculptures move for restoration, and whole streets are rebuilt. Check the date on any trail or list you use, and check the official page before you travel.
  • Visiting without thinking about the neighbourhood. Public art stands in real places where real people live. Keep your voice down in quiet streets, do not photograph people without asking, and leave the place as you found it.
  • Rushing past the small works. The famous pieces are worth seeing, but the small ones — the mosaic in a subway, the carving over a doorway, the bench shaped like a wave — are often the ones you remember. Slow down and look around.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist to plan an art walk, whether in your own city or somewhere new.

  • Search the official city or tourism page for a public art trail or map.
  • Save the works you want to see, and note which are indoors.
  • Check opening hours and access for any indoor or fenced works.
  • Ask the visitor centre or the local library what is new or loved locally.
  • Plan a walking route that groups nearby works together.
  • Wear comfortable shoes and carry water and a charged phone.
  • Read the plaque on every work, and photograph the plaque too.
  • Note the artist's name and the title for each work you like.
  • Look up, look down, and look behind you as you walk.
  • Leave every work untouched, and every place as you found it.
  • When you get home, look up the artists and plan your next walk.

A short list is better than a long one. Five works seen slowly will stay with you longer than twenty seen in a rush, and a walk that leaves you wanting more is a walk you will repeat.

Tips from real practice

Go early in the day. Public art in the morning light, before the crowds arrive, is a different experience from the same work at noon: the shadows are longer, the square is quieter, and you can stand and look without feeling in the way. Late afternoon is the other good hour, when low sun brings out the texture of stone and bronze.

Learn a little about the artist. A sculpture makes more sense when you know who made it and why: the Angel of the North, for example, was made by Antony Gormley as a symbol for a region that had lost its coal mines and shipyards, and knowing that changes how the huge steel figure feels. A few minutes of reading before you visit turns a shape into a story.

Visit the same work more than once. Public art changes with the weather, the season and the hour, and a work you barely noticed on a grey day can be startling in sunshine or snow. The people around it change too: a sculpture is a meeting place, a landmark and a backdrop to daily life, and watching how a city uses its art is part of the pleasure.

What this page cannot do

This page teaches you how to find and enjoy public art. It is not a map and not a complete list: no page could list every work in every city, and public art changes all the time. Works are moved, restored, painted over or removed, and a list written today may be out of date tomorrow.

For the current state of any work, check the official city, tourism or venue page before you travel, and ask at the visitor centre when you arrive. If a work you wanted to see is gone, treat it as a reason to look for the one that replaced it.

Public art changes — works are moved, covered or removed, and access rules change. Check the official city or venue page before you travel, and never climb on or touch a work unless a sign says you may.

Questions people ask

What is public art?

Art made for a public place, free for anyone to see: sculptures in squares, murals on building walls, installations in parks, memorials on street corners and mosaics in stations and subways. It is commissioned by cities, developers, charities or communities, and it belongs to the place where it stands.

Where can I find public art in a city?

Start with squares, parks, waterfronts, railway stations, university campuses and market streets, where most works are placed. Check the official city or tourism page for an art trail or map, ask at the visitor centre, and look up: murals and sculptures often sit above eye level.

Who pays for public art?

Cities and local authorities, property developers, charities, businesses and community groups, often working together. Many places run schemes where a small share of a building project's budget goes to art for the public. Artists are paid a fee for the work, and the city or the owner usually pays for its upkeep.

Can I photograph public art?

For personal photographs, usually yes: public art is made to be seen and shared. Rules differ by country and by work, and commercial use — selling a photograph or using it in an advertisement — may need permission from the artist or the owner. Always respect signs, and do not photograph people without asking.

What is the difference between a mural and graffiti?

A mural is usually commissioned or permitted by the building owner or the city, and it is meant to stay. Graffiti is usually painted without permission, and it may be removed at any time. Both can be art, and the line between them can blur, especially where street artists are later invited to paint legally.

How do I learn more about a sculpture I found?

Read the plaque, which usually gives the artist's name, the title and the date, and photograph it. Then search the city's public art register or the local library, ask at the visitor centre, or look for the artist's name: most public artists have other works nearby, and one find often leads to another.
