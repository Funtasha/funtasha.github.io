AI Avatar and Face Trends

By the Funtasha team · Published 20 November 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026

AI face trends turn one photo of you into a 90s yearbook portrait, a Polaroid with your younger self, an anime version, or a neon cyberpunk portrait. This guide shows how each look works, how to make it, and the one rule that matters: use your own photo.

Use your own photo, never someone else's — making AI pictures of other people without asking is not fair and can cause real harm. Keep your face recognisable, and remember that an AI picture is a fun version of you, not a real photo.

Why AI faces went viral

Something changed about profile pictures this year: the best ones are no longer photographs. They are AI versions of a real person — the same face, dropped into a different decade, a different style, or a different world. Scroll any feed in November 2026 and you will see them everywhere: friends as 90s school students, friends hugging their younger selves, friends redrawn as anime characters.

The reason is simple. A photo shows how you look; an AI face shows how you could look, and that is more fun to share. The looks spread because they are personal — everyone recognises themselves in the result — and because they are easy. One photo in, one striking picture out, and the whole thing takes less time than choosing a filter.

Images are the most common thing people use ChatGPT for: in a study of 118,163 prompts published on 17 November 2026, images were 17.6% of conversations, ahead of information and entertainment (14.4%) and writing (12.7%). Face trends are a big part of that — they are the most shared, most copied kind of AI picture there is.

The 90s yearbook portrait

The 90s yearbook portrait is the gentlest of the trends, and maybe the most charming. It takes your face and puts it in a school yearbook photo from the 1990s: soft studio lighting, a plain backdrop, a sweater or a denim jacket, and the slightly faded colour of a printed school photograph. The result looks like a memory, even though it never happened.

To make one, start with a clear front-facing photo — good light, no sunglasses, hair out of your eyes. Then describe the format: a 90s high-school yearbook portrait, soft flash, plain background, film photo look. The picker at the top of this page gives you the full prompt to copy. ChatGPT and Gemini both handle this format well, because it is mostly about lighting and framing rather than fantasy detail.

The usual failure is a face that looks too smooth or too young. If that happens, add the words natural skin texture and realistic proportions to your prompt, and try again with a different photo. One change at a time is the rule for fixing any AI picture.

Hugging your younger self

The most emotional trend of the month is the younger-self Polaroid: you, as you are now, hugging a younger version of yourself — a child, or a teenager — in a faded Polaroid-style photo. It is the kind of picture that makes people stop scrolling, because it is about time and memory as much as it is about a face.

It works because the format is forgiving. A Polaroid is soft, a little washed out, and slightly imperfect by nature, so small AI mistakes hide in the style. The prompt describes the scene — two versions of the same person, an arm around each other, warm indoor light, instant-film look — and the tool does the rest. The picker above has the full wording ready to copy.

A word of care: this trend uses childhood photos, so it is worth thinking about what you share. Your own childhood photo is yours to use. A photo of someone else's child is not. Keep it personal, keep it kind, and keep the pictures of other people out of it unless they have said yes.

The anime version of you

Hand-painted anime countryside is one of the trending formats of November 2026, and the anime version of a person rides the same wave. Your face, redrawn with the soft colours and clean lines of hand-painted anime: big gentle eyes, warm light, and a background that looks like a painted hillside or a quiet street.

Anime is the most forgiving style for faces, because it is not trying to be a photograph. The AI has room to simplify, and the result still reads as you — the shape of your face, your hair, your smile. That is why anime avatars make such good profile pictures: they are recognisable without being a real photo, and they look deliberate rather than accidental.

To get a good one, name the style clearly in your prompt — hand-painted anime, soft watercolour light, detailed background — and keep the framing simple: head and shoulders, facing the viewer. If the face drifts away from yours, shorten the prompt and try again. Long prompts pull the picture in too many directions at once.

Neon cyberpunk street

The loudest look of the four is the neon rain cyberpunk street: you, standing in a rainy city street at night, lit by pink and blue neon signs, reflections on wet pavement, a jacket collar turned up against the rain. It is the most dramatic of the trends, and the one that looks most like a film poster.

Cyberpunk works because the setting does the heavy lifting. The neon light, the rain and the reflections give the picture atmosphere before the face even matters, so the result looks striking even when the likeness is loose. That makes it a good first trend to try if you are worried about the face coming out wrong.

The prompt needs three things: the person, the street, and the light. Describe yourself simply — same face, same hair, dark jacket — then the scene — rainy street at night, neon signs, wet pavement — and the mood — cinematic, moody, film still. The picker above puts it together for you, ready to copy into ChatGPT or Gemini.

The one-photo rule

Every face trend in this guide follows one rule: use your own photo. It matters for three reasons. First, it is yours to use — nobody else's face is yours to put into a generator. Second, the results are better, because you know your own face and can tell when the picture has drifted. Third, it keeps the fun honest: an AI picture of you is a playful version of a real person, not a trick played on someone who never agreed to it.

The same rule applies to children. Your own childhood photo is fine for the younger-self trend. A photo of someone else's child — a niece, a nephew, a friend's kid — is not yours to feed into a generator, however cute the result would be. Ask first, and if the answer is no, or if you cannot ask, do not do it.

And a note on safety: uploading a photo means trusting a tool with your face. Use a tool you know — ChatGPT or Gemini — and check what it says about your uploads before you start. Avoid unknown apps that ask for your photo and give nothing back in return, and never upload a photo to a site you would not trust with your name.

What to do with your new face

Once you have a look you like, the fun is in using it. A 90s yearbook portrait makes a brilliant profile picture — it is recognisable, it starts conversations, and it stands out in a feed full of selfies. An anime version works the same way, with a softer feel.

Birthday posts are the natural home for the younger-self Polaroid: it says something about the year that has passed, and it is the kind of picture friends actually comment on. The cyberpunk portrait suits a party invite or a poster — print it, frame it, or drop it into a group chat and watch the reactions.

Whatever you make, keep one thing in mind: the best AI face is the one that still looks like you. If friends cannot tell it is you, the picture has drifted too far — shorten the prompt, use a clearer photo, and try again. The trends are fun because they are personal, and personal starts with a face people recognise.

Questions people ask

What is the 90s yearbook AI trend?

It turns your photo into a school yearbook portrait from the 1990s — soft studio lighting, a plain backdrop, a sweater or denim jacket, and the faded colour of a printed school photograph. Start with a clear front-facing photo and describe the format, and ChatGPT or Gemini will do the rest.

How do I make a younger-self Polaroid?

Use a clear photo of yourself and describe the scene: you now, hugging a younger version of yourself, in a faded Polaroid-style photo with warm indoor light. The picker at the top of this page gives you the full prompt to copy. Use your own childhood photo, not someone else's.

Can I use someone else's photo?

No — not without their clear permission. Making AI pictures of other people without asking is not fair and can cause real harm. Use your own photo, and ask first if a picture involves anyone else, especially children.

Why does my AI face look different from me?

Usually because the photo was unclear, or the prompt was too long and pulled the picture in too many directions. Use a clear, front-facing photo in good light, keep the prompt short, and change one thing at a time until the face comes back to yours.

Which app makes the best AI avatar?

ChatGPT and Gemini both handle the trending face formats well — the yearbook portrait, the younger-self Polaroid, the anime version and the cyberpunk street all work in both. Pick the one you already have an account with, and avoid unknown apps that ask for your photo.

Is it safe to upload my photo?

With a tool you know, yes — but check what it says about your uploads first, and never upload a photo to a site you would not trust with your name. Avoid unknown apps that ask for your face and give nothing back, and keep pictures of other people out of generators unless they have said yes.
