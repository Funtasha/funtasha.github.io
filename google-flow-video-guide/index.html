Google Flow is Google's AI filmmaking tool, and its free tier gives you 50 credits a day, enough for five 8-second clips made with the Veo 3.1 Lite model. This guide walks you through your first clip, the credit maths, and the honest limits, so you know exactly what free gets you.

Flow's free tier needs a Google account, and limits change — treat every number here as true as of November 2026 and check inside Flow before you plan a big project. Never upload photos of real people without their permission, and never present an AI clip as real footage.

What Google Flow is (and what it is not)

Google Flow is Google's AI filmmaking tool: you describe a scene in words, or hand it a few starting images, and it turns them into a short moving clip. It is built on Google's Veo video models, and it is aimed at people who want film-like clips without a camera, a set, or editing skill. As of November 2026, searches for google flow run at about 1.0 million a month, which tells you how many people are trying it.

What Flow is not matters just as much. It is not a full video editor, and it does not make long videos: its clips run 8 seconds at a time with the Veo 3.1 Lite model, so a minute of video means several clips stitched together. It is not a download-and-keep tool for other people's footage either — every clip starts from your words or your own images, and the result is new footage, not a copy of something that already exists.

Flow's free tier is genuinely usable, which is why this guide exists. You get 50 free credits a day, and five 8-second Veo 3.1 Lite clips fit inside that allowance. Free clips carry no visible watermark on most exports — instead Flow embeds an invisible SynthID mark, which identifies the clip as AI-made without spoiling the picture. The rest of this guide shows you how to spend those credits well.

Getting started: your first clip in 5 steps

Your first Flow clip takes about five minutes once you know the steps. You need a Google account and a browser — nothing to install, nothing to download.

  1. Open Flow in your browser and sign in with your Google account. The free tier is tied to the account, so use the one you plan to keep.
  2. Choose how to start: type a description (text-to-video), or add your own images as ingredients (ingredients-to-video) if you want a specific look or character to carry through.
  3. Pick your shape before you generate: 9:16 for vertical feeds like TikTok and Reels, 16:9 for a wide screen, 1:1 for a square post. Changing shape later means generating again.
  4. Write your prompt: one subject, one action, one camera move, one line about the light. Short prompts beat long ones — one clear idea per clip.
  5. Generate, watch, and keep or re-roll. If the clip is close but not right, change one word and try again; if it is wrong in every way, shorten the prompt and start over.

That is the whole loop, and it never changes: describe, generate, judge, adjust. The skill is not in the buttons — it is in learning which words move the picture, and the only way to learn that is to spend a few days of free credits experimenting.

The credit maths: how many clips a day

Flow's free tier gives you 50 credits a day, and that buys five 8-second clips made with the Veo 3.1 Lite model. Credits reset every day, so a project that needs twenty clips takes four days of free credits — or fewer, if you plan well and re-roll less.

The maths shapes how you should work. A 30-second video needs about four 8-second clips; a 60-second video needs about eight. Sketch the whole video on paper first, then generate the clips in order, one scene at a time. Random generating burns credits on clips you will never use, while a plan turns five daily clips into a finished video in two days.

Flow also offers a heavier, higher-quality model alongside the lighter Veo 3.1 Lite. The heavier model uses more credits per clip, so your 50 free credits will not stretch to five clips on it. Use the lighter model for drafts and everyday clips, and save the heavier one for the single shot that matters most — the opening frame, or the clip your whole video hangs on.

One more thing about credits: they are daily, not bankable. Unused credits do not roll over to tomorrow, so a small daily habit — five clips a day, every day — builds a library of footage faster than a single big session once a month.

Prompt patterns that work in Flow

Flow reads prompts the way a film director reads a shot list: it wants to know what is in frame, what moves, where the camera goes, and what the light feels like. The pattern that works is always the same — subject, action, camera, light, style — in one or two sentences.

Camera words do the heaviest lifting. A static camera holds the frame still; a slow pan sweeps across a scene; a dolly zoom pushes in on a subject; an orbit circles it; a crane shot rises above it. Pick one camera move per clip — two moves in one prompt usually fight each other, and the clip comes out confused.

Light words set the mood faster than anything else. Warm golden-hour light makes a scene feel gentle; cool morning light feels fresh; neon night light feels electric. Add the light to every prompt, even when the subject feels like the important part — the light is what makes two clips of the same subject feel like they belong in the same video.

Here are three prompts that follow the pattern. Copy them, run them, then change one word at a time and watch what moves.

A red bicycle leaning against a brick wall, gentle breeze moving the leaves, static camera, warm golden-hour light, cinematic style.

A small boat crossing a calm lake at dawn, slow dolly zoom toward the boat, cool morning light, realistic style.

A street market at night, crowds moving between stalls, slow pan left, neon signs glowing, cinematic style.

Notice what each prompt leaves out: no backstory, no dialogue, no second subject. One scene, one movement, one light. When a prompt fails, it is usually because it asked for two scenes at once — split it into two clips and the problem disappears.

Stretching a story across 8-second clips

Eight seconds is short, but it is enough for one clear moment: a door opening, a face turning, a product spinning into frame. A story is just a row of those moments, and Flow's job is to make each moment well — your job is to plan the row.

Plan backwards from the ending. Decide the last shot first — the finished result, the smile, the product in hand — then work out the three or four moments that lead to it. Write one prompt per moment, and repeat the same subject words and the same light words in every prompt. Repeated words are what make separate clips feel like one video.

Ingredients-to-video helps here. If you start each clip from the same reference image — the same product photo, the same character portrait — Flow keeps the look steady across clips in a way pure text prompts cannot. Use ingredients when consistency matters, and plain text when each clip stands alone.

Stitch the clips in CapCut, which is free and built for exactly this: drop the clips in order, trim the joins, add captions and a 9:16 frame for vertical feeds. Add narration or music last, so the sound follows the pictures rather than fighting them. A 60-second story made this way is eight clips, a voice, and an afternoon — no camera required.

Flow vs Google Vids, and when Flow asks you to pay

Google has two free video tools, and they solve different problems. Flow is the film tool: 50 free credits a day, 8-second cinematic clips, built for creative work. Google Vids is the work tool: 6 free AI clips a month on a personal account, each 3 to 10 seconds, built for presentations, explainers and quick workplace videos.

Pick by the job, not by the brand. If you are making something to watch — a short film, a product clip, a story — Flow's daily credits give you far more footage. If you are making something to present — a slide deck with moving pictures, a team update — Vids is the faster route, because it is built around slides and scripts rather than shots.

Sooner or later Flow will ask you to pay: when the day's 50 credits are gone, or when you want the heavier model for every clip. That is the honest shape of the free tier — generous for learning and for small projects, not unlimited. Plan around the daily reset, keep your prompts tight so fewer clips are wasted, and the free tier will carry a surprising amount of real work.

One last note for anyone arriving from Sora: Sora is gone. OpenAI announced the shutdown on 24 March 2026, the Sora app and site shut down on 26 April 2026, and the API followed on 24 September 2026, with no successor named. Flow is where many former Sora users have landed — not because it is the same tool, but because its free tier is the closest honest starting point for short creative clips.

Questions people ask

Is Google Flow really free?

Yes, as of November 2026 Flow gives you 50 free credits a day, enough for five 8-second clips made with the Veo 3.1 Lite model. You need a Google account, and the free tier has limits — credits reset daily and do not roll over — but you can make real clips without paying.

How long is a Flow clip?

A Flow clip made with the Veo 3.1 Lite model runs 8 seconds. Longer videos are made by planning several clips and stitching them together in CapCut, with narration and captions added on top.

Does Flow put a watermark on free clips?

No visible watermark appears on most free clips. Flow embeds an invisible SynthID mark instead, which identifies the clip as AI-made without spoiling the picture.

What is SynthID?

SynthID is Google's invisible mark for AI-made media. You cannot see it in the picture, but tools can detect it, so a clip can always be identified as AI-made. It does not change how the clip looks or plays.

Flow or Google Vids — which free tier is better?

Flow gives you 50 free credits a day for film-style clips, while Google Vids gives you 6 free AI clips a month, each 3 to 10 seconds, built for work videos and presentations. Pick Flow for creative clips and Vids for quick workplace videos.

What happened to Sora?

Sora is gone. OpenAI announced the shutdown on 24 March 2026, the Sora app and site shut down on 26 April 2026, and the API followed on 24 September 2026. No successor was named, and videos people had already exported remain theirs.
