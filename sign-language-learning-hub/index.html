Sign languages differ by country — read this first

The most important fact on this page is also the most surprising: there is no single, universal sign language. American Sign Language (ASL) is not the same as British Sign Language (BSL), which is not the same as Auslan in Australia, or the sign languages of France, Japan, or Bangladesh. Each one is a full language of its own, with its own signs, grammar, and history.

This hub teaches ASL fingerspelling and everyday signs as a starting point, because ASL has the most learning material available online. Every sign on this page is labelled ASL, and the fingerspelling chart is the ASL alphabet. If you live in the United Kingdom, Australia, or elsewhere, the signs you will actually use are your local ones — the last section links to resources for other countries.

That said, fingerspelling is a useful first step anywhere: it teaches you to use your hands to communicate, it gives you a way to spell any name or word, and it builds the hand control that every sign language needs. Start here, then find your local sign language and learn it properly.

What fingerspelling is — and how to start

Fingerspelling is spelling words out letter by letter, one handshape per letter. It is used for names, places, and words that have no sign of their own — and it is the first thing most learners learn, because the alphabet is small, fixed, and easy to check. The chart above shows all 26 ASL letters with a plain-words description of each handshape.

Start with your own name. Find each letter in the chart, make the shape slowly, and say the letter aloud as you go. Then try the names of your family, your town, and your favourite food. Short words first; long words come later, when your hand knows the shapes without looking.

Two letters move: J and Z. J draws a J shape in the air with the pinky finger up; Z draws a Z with the index finger. Every other letter is a still shape. Keep your hand relaxed and in front of your chest, at a comfortable height — not up by your face, not down by your waist. A relaxed hand makes clearer shapes than a tense one.

The everyday signs on this page

Below the chart are 30 everyday ASL signs, grouped so you can find them: greetings and manners, family and people, food and drink, feelings and everyday life, and numbers 1 to 10. Each sign is described in plain words — which hand, what shape, what movement — so you can learn it from the description alone.

Start with five: hello, thank you, please, yes, and no. These five cover most polite conversation, and they are the signs deaf people most appreciate a learner knowing. Add help, sorry, and good next, and you can already have a short, friendly exchange.

The numbers are worth learning early too, because they appear everywhere — times, prices, ages, dates. Numbers 1 to 5 are simple: hold up that many fingers. Numbers 6 to 9 touch the thumb to a finger — pinky for 6, ring finger for 7, middle finger for 8, index finger for 9 — and 10 is a thumb up, wiggling side to side. Practise counting from 1 to 10 until it flows, then try your phone number.

How to practise (a little every day, with a mirror)

Practise in front of a mirror. You cannot see your own hands the way other people see them, and the mirror shows you what they actually see — whether your shapes are clear, whether your hand is in the right place, whether you are rushing. Five minutes with a mirror beats twenty minutes without one.

Practise a little every day rather than a lot once a week. Fingerspelling is a physical skill, like handwriting: the shapes become automatic through daily repetition, not through long study sessions. Learn three new letters a day, review yesterday's three, and you will know the alphabet in about ten days.

Use the quiz below the signs to test yourself. It shows a letter and asks you to pick the right handshape description from three options. Ten rounds, instant feedback, and a score at the end. When you can score ten out of ten without thinking, your fingers know the alphabet — and that is the moment to start spelling real words.

Worked example: fingerspelling your name

Take the name "Sara". S: a fist with the thumb across the front of the fingers. A: a fist with the thumb up along the side. R: index and middle fingers crossed. A again. Spell it slowly, one shape at a time: S — A — R — A. Then try it faster, letting the shapes flow into each other.

Notice what happens when you speed up: the shapes get smaller and quicker, and that is normal — fluent fingerspelling is fast and compact, not big and slow. But speed comes after accuracy. If your R looks like a U, slow down and make the cross clear. Clarity first, speed later.

Now try a longer word: "thank you" is a sign, not a fingerspelled word — flat hand to the chin, moving forward toward the other person. Learning when to sign and when to spell is part of the skill. Common words have signs; names and unusual words get spelled. Both belong in your practice.

Learning path: what to learn after this page

After the alphabet and the 30 signs here, the next step is simple sentences: "Hello, my name is..." (fingerspell your name), "Thank you", "Please help me", "I am learning sign language". Put the signs you know into short sentences and practise them until they feel natural.

Then learn the signs for the words you use every day: your family, your work, your town, your hobbies. A sign language grows the way any language grows — from the words of your own life, not from a textbook list. Write down twenty words that matter to you and learn their signs one by one.

Finally, find people. A language is for communication, and signs learned alone stay stiff until they are used with real people. Look for a local sign language class, a deaf community event, or an online practice group. Introduce yourself, say you are learning, and ask for help — deaf communities are used to learners and, in our experience, generous with them.

Sign languages in other countries

If you live outside the United States, your local sign language is the one to learn. In the United Kingdom, that is British Sign Language (BSL) — a different language from ASL, with a two-handed alphabet and its own signs. In Australia, it is Auslan. In Bangladesh, it is Bangladeshi Sign Language. Each has its own learning resources, and the cards below are a starting point.

Lifeprint is a free, well-known ASL learning site with video lessons for every sign, and it is the best place to see the signs on this page performed by a real teacher. For BSL, the British Sign Language resources below cover the alphabet and everyday signs. For Auslan, the Australian site below does the same.

Wherever you are, the path is the same: learn the alphabet, learn a few everyday signs, practise a little every day, and find real people to sign with. The language you learn matters less than the fact that you are learning one — every sign you know is a door opened to a conversation that would otherwise stay closed.

Questions people ask

Is sign language the same everywhere?

No. Every country has its own sign language — ASL in the United States, BSL in the United Kingdom, Auslan in Australia, and many more — and they differ in signs, grammar, and even the alphabet. There is no universal sign language. This page teaches ASL as a starting point and links to resources for other countries.

What should I learn first?

The fingerspelling alphabet, then a few everyday signs: hello, thank you, please, yes, no, help, sorry, and good. Those eight cover most polite conversation. Learn three letters a day, review yesterday's, and you will know the alphabet in about ten days.

How long does it take to fingerspell?

You can learn all 26 handshapes in about a week of short daily practice. Fingerspelling fluently — reading other people's spelling at speed — takes months, because reading is harder than spelling. Practise both: spell words yourself, and watch videos of other people spelling.

Can I learn sign language online?

Yes, for the basics: the alphabet, everyday signs, and simple sentences are all easy to learn from videos and descriptions. But a language is for communication, and real practice with deaf people is what turns knowledge into skill. Use online learning to prepare, then find people to sign with.

Is it rude to practise with deaf people?

No — if you are respectful. Introduce yourself, say you are learning, and ask if they mind chatting. Most deaf people are glad to meet someone learning their language. Do not stare, do not grab someone's hands to correct a sign, and do not treat deaf people as free teachers — a short, friendly chat is welcome; a long lesson is not.

What is the difference between ASL and BSL?

They are different languages from different countries. ASL uses a one-handed alphabet and its own signs; BSL uses a two-handed alphabet and different signs — even for the same words, like hello and thank you. Knowing one does not mean knowing the other, just as knowing English does not mean knowing French.
