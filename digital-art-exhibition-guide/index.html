Digital Art Exhibition Guide

A digital art exhibition is a show of art presented online, where visitors look at the works on a screen instead of walking through a gallery. This guide explains how to choose a theme, select works, write about them, and share the show with an audience.

This page is a guide only. It hosts no exhibition and uploads nothing. Only show work you own or have permission to show: check the rights before displaying anyone else's art online, and credit every artist clearly.

What this is and who needs it

A digital art exhibition is a curated show of art that people visit online. Instead of hanging work on gallery walls, the organizer presents photographs or digital files of the works on a web page, in a slide show, or in a virtual gallery space. Visitors look at the works on their own screens, read about them, and sometimes leave comments or share the show with others. The art is real; only the room is virtual.

Digital exhibitions suit many people. Artists who want to show their work without the cost of a physical gallery use them. Schools and community groups use them to show student and member work to families far away. Collectors use them to share a collection that is spread across several places. And anyone who has made a body of work and wants the world to see it can put together a digital show with modest tools and no gallery at all.

A digital show has real advantages over a physical one. It costs far less, it can be seen by anyone with an internet connection, and it can stay open for as long as the organizer likes. A physical show lasts a few weeks and is seen by the people who can travel to it; a digital show can run for months and reach people on the other side of the world. It also has limits: a screen cannot show the true size of a work, the texture of a surface, or the feeling of standing in front of it. A good digital show accepts those limits and works with them.

The key word is exhibition, not gallery. A gallery shows everything; an exhibition chooses. A digital art exhibition has a theme, a selection of works that fit that theme, and words that help the visitor understand what they are seeing. That curation — the choosing and the explaining — is what makes a show worth visiting, and it is what this guide teaches.

How it works, step by step

Start with the theme. A theme is the idea that holds the show together: a subject, a place, a feeling, a question, or a period of work. A strong theme gives the visitor a reason to look at the works together rather than one by one. 'Paintings of the sea' is a theme; 'my paintings' is not. The theme can be simple, but it should be real — something the works genuinely share, not a label stuck on afterwards.

Next, choose the works. Fewer works, well chosen, make a better show than many works thrown together. Aim for a number the visitor can look at in one sitting without tiring — for most online shows, that means somewhere between ten and thirty works. Choose works that fit the theme, that look good together, and that show the range of the idea. Leave out anything that does not earn its place, however much you like it.

  1. Choose a theme: the idea that holds the show together.
  2. Select the works that fit the theme, keeping the number small enough for one sitting.
  3. Photograph or scan each work well, in even light, with true colours.
  4. Write a short introduction to the show: what it is about and why it matters.
  5. Write a few lines about each work: its title, its medium, its size, and what the visitor should notice.
  6. Arrange the works in an order that tells a story or builds a feeling.
  7. Choose where to share the show, and check the rights for every work you include.
  8. Share the show, invite people to look, and be ready to answer questions about the work.

Photographing the work is the step that decides how good the show looks. Photograph in even, natural light, straight on, filling the frame with the work and nothing else. Avoid flash, which washes out colour and creates reflections, and avoid photographing at an angle, which distorts the shape. Check each photograph on a screen before you use it: the colours should match the real work as closely as you can manage, and the image should be sharp. A well-photographed work looks professional; a badly photographed one makes even good art look careless.

Writing about the work matters as much as showing it. Visitors to a digital show cannot ask you a question across the room, so the words have to do that work. Write plainly: what the work is, what it is made of, how big it is, and what you want the visitor to notice. Avoid jargon and avoid long, abstract sentences. A few honest lines about each work are worth more than a page of theory that nobody reads.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

A digital exhibition costs very little in money. The main costs are time and, if you want them, a few tools: a decent camera or a good phone camera, even lighting, and somewhere to put the show online. Many people already have everything they need. There is no gallery hire, no transport, no framing bill and no opening-night catering. That low cost is one of the reasons digital shows have become so popular.

Time is the real investment. Choosing a theme, selecting works, photographing them well and writing about them takes days or weeks, not hours. Photography takes the longest for most people, because each work needs to be photographed well, checked, and sometimes photographed again. Allow generous time for this step, and do not rush it: the photographs are the show, and a show is only as good as its images.

Before you start, gather the works and the information about them. For each work, you need its title, the medium it was made in, its size, and the year it was made if you know it. You also need to know the rights: if you made the work, the rights are yours; if someone else made it, you need their permission to show it online, and you should credit them clearly. Never show another artist's work online without permission — it is their work, and the decision to share it is theirs.

You also need to decide where the show will live. The options range from a simple page on an existing site to a dedicated exhibition platform. What matters is that the show is easy to find, easy to look at on a phone as well as a computer, and easy to share. A show that is hard to open will not be seen, however good the art is.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is showing too much. An organizer who loves every work wants to include them all, and the show becomes a long scroll that visitors abandon halfway. A show with fifteen strong works is better than a show with sixty works of mixed quality. Choosing is hard, but it is the organizer's job, and the visitor will thank you for it. If a work does not fit the theme, leave it out — it can have its own show another time.

The second mistake is poor photographs. Blurry images, crooked framing, glare from a flash, colours that do not match the real work — all of these make the art look worse than it is. Photograph in daylight, straight on, without flash, and check every image on a screen before you publish it. If a photograph is not good enough, take it again. The photograph is the only version of the work the visitor will ever see, so it deserves the time.

The third mistake is no words at all. A show of images with no introduction and no notes about the works leaves the visitor guessing. People want to know what they are looking at, why it matters, and what to notice. A few plain lines per work — title, medium, size, and one honest sentence about it — transform a set of pictures into an exhibition.

  • Never show work you do not have the right to show — get permission first, in writing.
  • Never include a work only because you feel you should — every work must earn its place.
  • Never publish a photograph you have not checked on a screen first.
  • Never use flash on a framed work — it creates glare and washes out colour.
  • Never write about the work in jargon — plain words reach more people.
  • Never forget the credits — name every artist clearly, and thank anyone who helped.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist to plan and build your digital exhibition, from the first idea to the shared show. Work through it in order, and do not skip the steps that feel slow — they are usually the ones that matter most.

  • Choose a theme: the idea that holds the show together.
  • Select the works that fit the theme, keeping the number small enough for one sitting.
  • For each work, gather its title, medium, size and year, and check the rights.
  • Get written permission for any work you did not make yourself.
  • Photograph each work in even daylight, straight on, without flash.
  • Check every photograph on a screen: sharp, true in colour, and filling the frame.
  • Write a short introduction to the show: what it is about and why it matters.
  • Write a few lines about each work: title, medium, size, and what to notice.
  • Arrange the works in an order that tells a story or builds a feeling.
  • Choose where to share the show, and test it on a phone as well as a computer.
  • Share the show, invite people to look, and answer questions about the work.
  • Keep a record of the show: the works, the words and the photographs, saved together.

The last step — keeping a record — is easy to forget and worth doing. A saved copy of the show, with its photographs and words, is a record of your work at a point in time. It is useful for future shows, for applications, and for your own memory of what you made and why.

Tips from real practice

Show the work at its best size. Online, that means large enough to see detail, but not so large that the page loads slowly. Test the show on a phone on a slow connection: if the images take too long to load, visitors will leave before they see anything. A show that loads quickly and looks good on a phone will be seen by far more people than one that only works on a fast computer.

Order the works with care. The first work sets the tone, and the last work is what the visitor remembers. Put a strong work first, build through the theme, and end with a work that stays in the mind. Think of the order as a walk through a gallery: the visitor should feel a journey, not a random pile.

Invite people properly. A show that nobody knows about is a show nobody visits. Tell the people who would care: friends, fellow artists, local groups, and anyone who follows your work. A short personal invitation, with one good image and a line about the theme, brings more visitors than a mass message sent to everyone at once.

Finally, be present. A digital show is not finished when it goes online — it is finished when people have seen it and talked about it. Answer questions, thank people who visit, and ask what they thought. The conversation around a show is part of the show, and the artists who stay for the conversation learn more than the ones who walk away.

Questions people ask
