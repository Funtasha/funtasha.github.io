How to use this guide

This guide explains how to find a voiceover artist in plain English: where to look, what to listen for in a demo, how rates work, and how to write a brief that gets the read you want. It is written for people making videos, adverts, explainers and phone messages who need a professional voice and have never hired one before. It lists no real artists, because a list printed here would go out of date, and because the right voice depends on your project, not on a ranking.

Read the sections in order the first time through. The first sections explain where artists can be found and how to listen to a demo properly. The middle sections explain rates and the brief, which is the document that makes or breaks the recording. The last sections cover working with the artist you choose and the mistakes that waste time and money. This page helps you find an artist; it is not a script writer, and it does not write voiceover scripts for you.

Where to look for voiceover artists

Voiceover artists are found in a few kinds of place, and each suits a different kind of project. Agencies represent professional artists and handle the booking, the contract and the payment, which suits larger projects and tight deadlines. Online casting sites let you post a job and receive auditions from artists around the world, which suits projects where you want to compare many voices quickly. Many artists also work through their own websites, where you can hear their demos and contact them directly, which suits projects where you already know the kind of voice you want.

Recommendations are worth as much as any listing. Ask other video makers, podcasters and producers who they have used and would use again, because a recommendation carries information no demo can: whether the artist was easy to work with, delivered on time, and took direction well. Wherever you look, the checks are the same. Listen to the demo, check the artist's experience with your kind of project, and agree the price, the delivery date and the number of revisions before any recording starts.

  • Agencies: professional artists, with booking and payment handled for you.
  • Casting sites: post a job and compare auditions from many artists.
  • Artists' own websites: hear the demos and contact the artist directly.
  • Recommendations: ask other makers who they would hire again.

Listening to demos

A demo is a short recording that shows what an artist can do, usually a minute or two of different styles: a warm read, an energetic read, a serious read, a character read. Listen to the whole demo, not just the first ten seconds, because the first ten seconds are the easiest to make sound good. Listen for the things that matter to your project: clarity at speed, warmth without effort, and the ability to sound natural while reading words that were written to be read, not spoken.

Listen twice, once for the voice and once for the craft. On the first listen, ask whether this is a voice your audience would trust and enjoy for the length of your project. On the second listen, ask whether the artist varies pace and emphasis, or reads everything in the same flat tone; whether the breathing is natural; and whether the recording itself is clean, with no room echo, hiss or pops. A beautiful voice in a noisy recording is useless, and a plain voice with perfect craft will serve most projects better than a striking voice with none.

  • Listen to the whole demo, not just the opening seconds.
  • First listen: is this a voice your audience would trust?
  • Second listen: pace, emphasis, breathing, and recording quality.
  • A clean recording matters as much as the voice itself.
  • Match the demo style to your project, not to your taste alone.

How rates work

Voiceover rates vary widely, and they are usually worked out from the length of the script, the use of the recording, and the time the artist needs. A short script for a single video costs less than a long script for a series. A recording used in one place costs less than one used everywhere, and a recording used in advertising costs more than one used in training, because advertising reaches more people and earns more money. Urgent work costs more than work with a comfortable deadline, and a well-known voice costs more than a new one.

Ask for the full price before the work starts, and ask what it includes: the recording, how many revisions, the delivery format, and the uses covered. A price that looks low but covers only one use can end up costing more than a higher price that covers them all. Never agree a price by the hour of studio time when the work is priced by the finished minute, and never assume revisions are free. Get the price, the uses, the revisions and the delivery date in writing, in one short message, before the artist records a word.

  • Length of script, use of the recording, and turnaround set the price.
  • Advertising use costs more than training or internal use.
  • Ask what the price includes: revisions, format, uses covered.
  • Get the price, uses, revisions and delivery date in writing first.

Writing a good brief

The brief is the document that tells the artist what you want, and a good brief is the difference between a recording you can use and a recording you have to redo. It starts with the basics: what the recording is for, who will hear it, and how long the finished recording should be. Then it describes the voice you want, in words the artist can act on: warm and steady, bright and quick, calm and low, not vague words like nice or good. Then it gives the script, marked with any words that need special care: names, numbers, and words that can be read two ways.

Include the practical details too: the delivery date, the file format, and how many revisions are included. Say how the recording will be used, because that affects the price and the licence. And give the artist something to aim at: a recording you like, a sentence that captures the tone, or a note about what the listener should feel at the end. A brief that takes twenty minutes to write saves hours of revisions, because the artist records what you actually wanted the first time.

  • What it is for, who hears it, and how long it should be.
  • The voice in actable words: warm and steady, bright and quick.
  • The script, with tricky names and numbers marked.
  • Delivery date, file format, revisions, and how it will be used.
  • One example of the tone you want, in words or in sound.

Working with the artist you choose

Once you have chosen an artist, the work is a partnership, and small courtesies make a large difference. Send the brief and the script together, in one message, so nothing is missed. Answer questions quickly, because an artist waiting for an answer is an artist not recording. When the first recording arrives, listen to it in full, in a quiet place, before you reply, and give your notes in one message, not in a drip of small messages over a day.

Give direction in the language of the brief: more warmth in the second paragraph, slower on the list of prices, a pause before the last line. Vague notes like make it better help nobody, and neither does asking for a completely different voice after the recording is done. If the read is wrong, say what is wrong and what you want instead, in the same message. Most artists include a number of revisions in the price, and a clear set of notes uses them well. Pay on time, and say thank you: the artist you treat well is the artist who answers your next call.

  • Send the brief and the script together, in one message.
  • Answer questions quickly, and give notes in one message.
  • Direct in actable words: slower here, warmer there, a pause before the end.
  • Use revisions well, pay on time, and say thank you.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is picking a voice by price alone. The cheapest artist is often the cheapest because the recording is noisy, the delivery is slow, or the revisions cost extra, and a recording you cannot use is the most expensive recording of all. The second mistake is a brief with no direction in it: a script sent with no note about tone, pace or audience leaves the artist guessing, and the guess is rarely the read you wanted.

The third mistake is skipping the custom demo when the project matters, and finding out too late that the artist's voice does not suit your script. The fourth is agreeing a price without agreeing the uses, and discovering that the recording cannot be used where you need it. And the fifth is giving vague feedback and then blaming the artist for the result. Every one of these is avoided the same way: listen carefully, brief clearly, agree everything in writing, and direct in words the artist can act on.

  • Never pick by price alone; a recording you cannot use costs the most.
  • Always send direction with the script, not just the script.
  • Ask for a custom demo when the project matters.
  • Agree the uses, not just the price, before recording starts.
  • Give feedback in actable words, in one message.

Questions people ask

Where do I find voiceover artists?

Agencies represent professional artists and handle booking and payment. Casting sites let you post a job and compare auditions. Many artists work through their own websites, where you can hear demos and contact them directly, and recommendations from other makers are worth as much as any listing.

What should I listen for in a demo?

Listen to the whole demo, not just the opening seconds. Listen for clarity, warmth, and the ability to sound natural while reading written words. Listen again for pace, emphasis and breathing, and for recording quality: a clean recording matters as much as the voice itself.

How much does voiceover cost?

Rates vary widely, and they are usually worked out from the length of the script, how the recording will be used, and how fast you need it. Advertising use costs more than training use, and urgent work costs more than work with a comfortable deadline. Ask for the full price, and what it includes, before recording starts.

How do I write a brief?

Say what the recording is for, who will hear it, and how long it should be. Describe the voice in actable words, such as warm and steady or bright and quick. Include the script with tricky names and numbers marked, plus the delivery date, file format, revisions and uses.

Should I ask for a custom demo?

Yes, when the project matters. A custom demo is a short read of your own script, and it shows whether the artist's voice suits your words, not just their own demo script. It is the best way to avoid finding out too late that the voice does not fit.

What if the read is wrong?

Say what is wrong and what you want instead, in one message, using the language of the brief: slower here, warmer there, a pause before the end. Most artists include revisions in the price. Vague notes help nobody, so be specific about what to change.
