What this page does

Every measurement is a comparison: a length against a ruler, a weight against a scale, a temperature against a fixed point like freezing water. This page is a reference for the units people actually meet — length, weight, volume, temperature, area and speed — with plain-words explanations, everyday comparisons, and a converter for the most-used pairs.

Search the reference to find a unit and see what it means: how long is a metre, how heavy is a kilogram, how much is a cup. Use the converter when you need an exact answer, and read the guide below to learn how to convert in your head. The page covers both systems of measurement — metric and imperial — because the real world uses both, often on the same road sign, recipe or parcel label.

Metric vs imperial: two systems, one world

The metric system is built on tens. A metre divides into 100 centimetres and 1,000 millimetres; a kilometre is 1,000 metres; a kilogram is 1,000 grams. Converting inside the metric system means moving a decimal point, which is why most of the world uses it for science, trade and everyday life. The system was designed in France in the 1790s to be simple and universal, and it largely succeeded.

The imperial system grew up over centuries in Britain, and it is built on the human body and on old trades: the inch was once the width of a thumb, the foot the length of a foot, the yard the distance from nose to fingertip. Its conversions are irregular — 12 inches in a foot, 3 feet in a yard, 1,760 yards in a mile — which makes mental maths harder but gives each unit a size that feels natural to the body. Britain itself now mixes the two systems, and the United States still uses imperial units for most everyday measurements.

Neither system is going away, so the practical skill is not choosing one but moving between them. Learn one anchor in each pair — a metre is about a yard plus a little, a kilogram is about two and a fifth pounds, a litre is a little more than a quart — and you can estimate anywhere. How to convert in your head

Mental conversion works best with anchors and round numbers, not with long division. Pick one easy fact per pair and build from it. For length: a mile is about one and a half kilometres, so ten miles is about sixteen kilometres. For weight: a kilogram is about two pounds, so five kilograms is about eleven pounds. For temperature: double the Celsius figure and add thirty to get roughly Fahrenheit — 20 degrees becomes 70, against the true 68, which is close enough for weather.

For better accuracy, learn the exact anchor and one step. A kilometre is 0.621 miles, so multiply kilometres by six and divide by ten for a good estimate. A pound is 0.454 kilograms, so halve the pounds and take off a tenth. A litre is 0.264 US gallons, so a quarter of the litres is close to the gallons. These shortcuts get you within a few per cent, which is enough for cooking, travel and shopping — and when it is not enough, the converter is right above.

Length: from millimetres to miles

Length is the most measured thing in daily life: the height of a child, the length of a room, the distance to work. The metric ladder runs millimetre, centimetre, metre, kilometre — each step a factor of ten or a thousand. The imperial ladder runs inch, foot, yard, mile — each step a factor of twelve, three, or 1,760. The inch is now defined exactly as 25.4 millimetres, which fixes every imperial length unit to the metric system by definition.

Everyday comparisons help more than numbers. A millimetre is about the thickness of a credit card. A centimetre is about the width of a fingernail. A metre is about a long stride. A kilometre is about a twelve-minute walk. An inch is about the width of a thumb. A foot is about the length of a school ruler. A mile is about a twenty-minute walk. Tie each unit to something you can see or hold, and the numbers stop being abstract.

Weight: grams, kilograms, ounces and pounds

Weight is what a scale reads: the pull of gravity on a mass. In the metric system the base unit is the kilogram — a litre of water weighs almost exactly one kilogram, which is a useful anchor. A gram is a thousandth of a kilogram, about the weight of a paperclip. A tonne is a thousand kilograms, about the weight of a small car.

In the imperial system the pound is the everyday unit, defined exactly as 0.45359237 kilograms. An ounce is a sixteenth of a pound, about 28.35 grams — about the weight of a slice of bread. The stone, still used for body weight in Britain and Ireland, is fourteen pounds. For most conversions, one kilogram is about two and a fifth pounds, and one pound is about 454 grams.

Volume: litres, cups and gallons

Volume measures how much space something fills, and it is the unit that causes the most trouble in kitchens, because cups, pints and gallons are not the same everywhere. A litre is a thousand millilitres, and a millilitre is a thousandth of a litre — simple. The trouble starts with the cup: a US cup is 236.588 millilitres, a metric cup is 250 millilitres, and a UK cup is about 284 millilitres. A recipe that says "two cups" can mean three different amounts depending on where it was written.

Pints and gallons differ too. A US pint is 473.176 millilitres; a UK pint is 568 millilitres. A US gallon is 3.78541 litres; a UK gallon is 4.546 litres. This page uses US measures for cups, pints and gallons, and says so on every entry, because most online recipes do the same. If your recipe comes from a British book, check which pint it means before you start pouring.

Temperature: Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin

Temperature scales are fixed by two points. On the Celsius scale, water freezes at 0 and boils at 100. On the Fahrenheit scale, water freezes at 32 and boils at 212. On the Kelvin scale, used in science, zero is absolute zero — the coldest temperature possible — and water freezes at 273.15. To convert Fahrenheit to Celsius, subtract 32, multiply by 5, and divide by 9. To go the other way, multiply by 9, divide by 5, and add 32.

The two everyday scales cross at minus 40, where minus 40 Celsius equals minus 40 Fahrenheit — the only temperature where the two scales agree. Room temperature is about 20 Celsius or 68 Fahrenheit. A hot summer day is about 30 Celsius or 86 Fahrenheit. Body temperature is about 37 Celsius or 98.6 Fahrenheit. Learn those three anchors and you can read weather in either scale at a glance.

Area and speed

Area is length times length, and its units square with it. A square metre is a square one metre on each side. A hectare is ten thousand square metres — about the size of a rugby pitch. An acre is 4,046.86 square metres — about three-quarters of a football pitch. A square kilometre is a million square metres, and it is the unit for towns, forests and lakes. Area conversions catch people out because the factor squares too: there are about 10.76 square feet in a square metre, not 3.28.

Speed is distance over time. Kilometres per hour and miles per hour are the road units; metres per second is the scientific unit; knots — one nautical mile per hour — are the sea and air unit. One mile per hour is 1.609344 kilometres per hour, and one metre per second is 3.6 kilometres per hour. A brisk walk is about 1.4 metres per second, a car on a motorway about 30, and a passenger jet about 250.

Worked examples

A recipe. A cake recipe from an American website calls for two cups of flour and one cup of milk. Two US cups are 473 millilitres, and one cup is 237 millilitres. But flour is better weighed than measured by volume — two cups of flour weigh about 250 grams, while two cups of milk weigh about 470 grams. The same cup holds different weights of different things, which is why serious bakers use a scale.

Travel. A road sign in the United States says the next town is 12 miles away, and the speed limit is 55 miles per hour. Twelve miles is about 19 kilometres, and 55 miles per hour is about 89 kilometres per hour. The drive takes about thirteen minutes. Knowing the two anchors — a mile is about one and a half kilometres, and 60 miles per hour is about 100 kilometres per hour — makes every American road sign readable.

DIY. A shelf needs to be 1.2 metres long, but the timber is sold in feet. One metre is 3.28 feet, so 1.2 metres is about 3.94 feet — just under four feet. A four-foot board, cut down by about two centimetres, does the job. Measuring in the unit the shop uses, and converting once, beats converting every cut.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up weight and volume. A litre of water weighs a kilogram, but a litre of flour weighs about half that. Cups measure volume, not weight — never swap them without checking.
  • Mixing up US and UK measures. A US pint and a UK pint differ by almost a fifth. Check which country your recipe or manual comes from.
  • Forgetting that area factors square. One metre is 3.28 feet, but one square metre is 10.76 square feet. Square the factor when you square the unit.
  • Converting temperature by multiplying. Fahrenheit and Celsius have different zero points, so you must add or subtract 32 as well as multiplying. Doubling a Celsius temperature does not double the Fahrenheit one.
  • Trusting a rough anchor for exact work. "A mile is about one and a half kilometres" is fine for a road sign and wrong for a race. Use the converter when the number matters.

Questions people ask

How many centimetres are in a metre?

One hundred. A metre divides into 100 centimetres, and each centimetre divides into 10 millimetres. Moving between metric length units is just moving the decimal point, which is why the metric system is easy to use.

What is the difference between weight and mass?

Mass is how much matter something contains, and it does not change when you move it. Weight is the force gravity exerts on that mass, so it changes with gravity — you would weigh less on the Moon but your mass would stay the same. In everyday speech the two words are used interchangeably, and scales measure weight.

How do I convert Fahrenheit to Celsius?

Subtract 32, then multiply by 5 and divide by 9. For example, 77 Fahrenheit minus 32 is 45, times 5 divided by 9 is 25 Celsius. To go the other way, multiply by 9, divide by 5, and add 32.

How much is a cup in millilitres?

A US cup is 236.588 millilitres, a metric cup is 250 millilitres, and a UK cup is about 284 millilitres. Most online recipes use the US cup. When a recipe just says cups, check which country it comes from — the difference can change a cake.

Why do some countries use miles?

The imperial system grew up in Britain over centuries, and the United States kept it after independence. Most other countries switched to the metric system in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries because its base-ten structure makes maths simpler. Changing a whole country's road signs and habits is slow and expensive, so the old units survive where they are already in place.
