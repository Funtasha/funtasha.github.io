Art Exhibition Submission Directory

Submitting your art to an exhibition is how your work meets its audience, and a strong entry starts long before the deadline. This guide shows you where to find open calls, how submissions work, and what juries actually look for.

This guide teaches you how to find and enter exhibitions — it lists no exhibitions or open calls, because they open and close all year. Check the details of every call yourself, and never pay a fee you do not understand.

How to use this guide

Every artist who exhibits started with a first submission, and every artist who exhibits regularly was rejected far more often than they were accepted. Submitting work is a skill, separate from making it: finding the right calls, reading the rules, presenting the work well, and handling the answer, whatever it is. This guide teaches that skill.

Work through the sections in order. First, where to find open calls that fit your work. Then how submissions work — the forms, the photographs, the fees, and the delivery. Then what juries look for, and how to build a strong entry. Then photographing your art, which matters more than most artists expect. Finish with the common mistakes, and you will submit with confidence.

One honest line: this guide lists no exhibitions or open calls. Calls open and close all year, and any list printed here would be out of date before you read it. What does not change is how to find them, how to read them, and how to present your work well — and that is what this page teaches.

Where to find open calls

Open calls are published by galleries, art organisations, artist networks, and local authorities, and many list them online. Start with the organisations in your own area: a local gallery's open exhibition is often the best first step, because the theme is broad, the jury is local, and the delivery is easy. From there, widen to regional and national calls that fit your medium and your subject.

Ask other artists where they apply. Artists share calls the way they share suppliers — a recommendation from someone who has exhibited there tells you what the listing does not: how the jury behaves, how the work is hung, whether the sales are real. Keep a note of the calls that fit your work, with their usual opening months, so you are ready when they open again. Most calls repeat yearly, and the artist who is ready early submits calmly.

Be selective. A call that does not fit your work wastes your time and the jury's, and a scatter of random submissions teaches you nothing. Choose calls whose theme, medium, and spirit match what you make, and submit your best work to a few, rather than your average work to many.

How submissions work

Most submissions follow the same shape. You find the call, read its rules, and submit through the channel it names — usually an online form with photographs of your work, a short statement, and sometimes a fee. The jury reviews the submissions, often from the photographs alone, and the accepted artists are told what to deliver, when, and how.

Read the rules carefully before you apply. Check the theme, the medium, the size limits, the number of pieces you may submit, and the deadline — including the time zone, if the call is international. Check the fee, what it covers, and whether it is refunded if your work is not accepted. Check the delivery arrangements: who pays for shipping, who carries the risk in transit, and when unsold work must be collected.

Keep a record of every submission: the call, the date, the pieces, the fee, and the answer. A simple list, kept up to date, stops you submitting the same piece to two shows at once, and it shows you over a year which calls are worth repeating. Submit early where you can — forms fail, photographs upload slowly, and a deadline is a deadline.

What juries look for

A jury looks for three things: work that fits the theme, work that is well made, and work that is presented well. The theme comes first — a beautiful piece that ignores the theme will lose to a good piece that answers it. Read the theme as a question, and ask yourself whether your work answers it honestly. If it does not, choose another piece, or another call.

Quality is judged from the photograph, which is why the photograph matters so much. The jury cannot touch your work, see its true scale, or watch how the light moves across it. What they see is the image you sent. A well-made piece in a bad photograph will lose to a lesser piece in a good one, and that is not unfair — it is the nature of judging from images.

Juries also look for a clear idea. A short artist statement that says what the work is about, in plain words, helps the jury see what you see. Keep it short, honest, and specific: what the piece is, why you made it, and what you hope a viewer takes from it. Avoid grand claims and art jargon — a jury reads hundreds of statements, and the plain ones stand out.

The strong entry

A strong entry is a complete entry: the right pieces, the right photographs, the right words, submitted on time. Start by choosing your pieces. Submit your best work, not your most recent — a strong older piece beats a weak new one. If the call allows three pieces, submit three that sit well together, rather than three that show your range. A jury remembers a group; it forgets a scatter.

Write your statement after you choose the pieces, not before. Say what the work is, why you made it, and what connects the pieces you chose. Keep it under a few hundred words, and read it aloud — if it sounds grand or vague, rewrite it until it sounds like you talking about your own work.

Check everything before you send it: the photographs are the right files, the right size, and the right way up; the titles and dimensions are correct; the fee is paid; the form is complete. A strong entry is not a lucky one — it is a careful one, checked twice and sent early.

Photographing your art

Photograph in soft daylight, near a large window or outside on a cloudy day — never in direct sun, which creates glare, and never under room lights, which change the colours. Hang the work straight, stand straight in front of it, and fill the frame with the work, leaving a small margin around the edges. Check that the photograph is level, in focus, and free of shadows, reflections, and anything else in the frame.

Use a plain background: a white or neutral wall, with nothing else showing. If the work is three-dimensional, photograph it from the front and from one side, against a plain background, with soft light from one side to show the form. Check the colours on your screen against the real work — if they do not match, adjust the light, not the photograph.

Send the file size the call asks for, in the format it asks for, named the way it asks for. A photograph that is too small cannot be judged; a photograph that is too large may not upload. Crop out the edges of the frame and the wall, straighten the image, and check it once more at full size before you send it. Your photograph is your work, as far as the jury is concerned — make it worthy of the piece.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is submitting work that does not fit the theme. A jury reads the theme first, and work that ignores it is set aside, however good it is. Choose the piece that answers the theme, or choose another call.

The second is bad photographs of good art. A blurred, crooked, or glaring photograph hides the work it is meant to show. Photograph in soft daylight, straight on, and check the image at full size before you send it.

The third is missing the deadline. Forms fail, uploads stall, and a deadline is a deadline. Submit early, and keep a copy of everything you sent.

The fourth is a vague statement. A statement full of grand claims tells the jury nothing. Say what the work is, why you made it, and what connects your pieces, in plain words.

The fifth is taking rejection personally. Every artist is rejected far more often than accepted, and one jury's decision is not a verdict on your art. Ask for feedback where it is offered, keep the work for the next call, and submit again.

Questions people ask

How do I submit art to an exhibition?

Find an open call that fits your work, read its rules carefully, and submit through the channel it names — usually an online form with photographs of your work. Check the deadline, the fee, and the delivery arrangements before you apply.

Where do I find open calls?

Art organisations, galleries, and artist networks publish open calls, and many list them online. Ask other artists where they apply, and keep a note of the calls that fit your work, so you are ready when they open again.

What do juries look for?

Juries look for work that fits the theme, is well made, and is presented well — and for a clear idea behind it. Good photographs matter as much as good work, because the jury usually sees the photograph first.

How do I photograph my art?

Photograph in soft daylight, straight on, with the work filling the frame and no glare or shadow across it. Use a plain background, check the focus, and send the file size the call asks for.

Is there a fee to submit?

Some exhibitions charge a small entry fee, and some are free. Read the call carefully: a fee should be plainly stated, and you should know what it covers before you pay.

What if my work is rejected?

Rejection is normal — every artist is rejected far more often than accepted. Ask for feedback where it is offered, keep the work for the next call, and do not take one jury's decision as a verdict on your art.
