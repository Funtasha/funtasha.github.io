Museum Collection Catalog Guide

A museum catalog is the record of everything a museum owns: what each object is, where it came from, what condition it is in, and where it is kept. This guide explains how museums build and use a catalog, and how a small museum or a private collector can use the same system.

This page is a guide to general museum practice. It lists no collections and shows no real museum records. Every museum has its own rules, and valuable or fragile objects should always be handled with advice from a trained conservator.

What this is and who needs it

A museum collection catalog is the master record of a museum's holdings. Every object the museum owns — a painting, a tool, a dress, a coin, a photograph — has a catalog record. The record says what the object is, when and where it was made, how the museum got it, what condition it is in, and where it is stored or displayed. Without a catalog, a museum is a warehouse with interesting contents and no memory. With one, the museum knows what it holds, can find anything in minutes, and can share its knowledge with researchers and visitors.

This guide is for small museums, local history groups, school collections and private collectors who want to organize what they own. Large museums have dedicated staff and specialist software, but the ideas are the same at every size: one record per object, consistent details, a photograph, and a clear location. A small collection cataloged well is more useful than a large collection cataloged badly.

A catalog serves several purposes at once. It is an inventory: proof of what the museum owns, which matters for insurance and for accountability. It is a research tool: a researcher can search the catalog to find objects about a topic without opening every drawer. It is a care tool: the condition notes tell staff which objects need attention. And it is a loan tool: when another museum borrows an object, the catalog record travels with it, so everyone knows what was lent and in what condition.

For a private collector, the same system brings the same benefits on a smaller scale. A catalog turns a pile of interesting things into a collection with a story: what each piece is, where it came from, and why it matters. It also makes the collection far easier to pass on, sell or donate one day, because the knowledge is written down instead of living only in one person's memory.

How it works, step by step

Cataloging starts the moment an object arrives. The first step is to give it an accession number — a unique code that identifies the object for as long as the museum owns it. The number is usually written on a small tag tied to the object, or marked on the object itself in a discreet place, using a method that does no harm. The accession number is the key that links the physical object to its catalog record, so it must be unique: no two objects share a number.

Next comes the record itself. A good record answers the basic questions about the object: what it is, what it is made of, when and where it was made, who made it if that is known, and how the museum acquired it. The record also notes the object's size, its condition, and where it is kept. Every detail is written in plain, consistent words, because a catalog that uses five different words for the same thing cannot be searched properly.

  1. Give the object a unique accession number and mark it safely.
  2. Write the catalog record: what it is, what it is made of, when and where it was made, and how it arrived.
  3. Measure the object and note its dimensions in the record.
  4. Photograph the object from several angles, including any marks, labels or damage.
  5. Note the condition honestly, and flag anything that needs a conservator's attention.
  6. Record where the object is stored or displayed, and update the location whenever it moves.
  7. File the record so it can be found by number, by name and by topic.

Photography deserves special attention, because a good photograph is often the most-used part of a catalog record. Photograph the object against a plain background, in even light, from the front and from any angle that shows an important detail. Photograph marks, signatures, labels and damage close up. Keep the photographs with the record, labelled with the accession number, so that a researcher can see the object without handling it. A photograph also proves the object's condition at a point in time, which matters for loans and insurance.

The last step is the one most often skipped: keeping the catalog up to date. Objects move, conditions change, and new information comes to light. A catalog is a living record, and a record that is five years out of date is barely better than no record. Build a habit of updating the record whenever an object moves or its condition changes, and check the catalog against the shelves on a regular schedule.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Cataloging costs time more than money. The basic needs are modest: a way to write records, a way to photograph objects, and a place to store the records safely. A small museum can start with a simple spreadsheet and a camera, and many do. Specialist collection software exists and brings real benefits for larger collections — searching, loan tracking, linked photographs — but it is not needed to begin. What matters is consistency: one record per object, the same fields every time, the same words for the same things.

Time is the real cost. A thorough record for one object takes time: examining it, measuring it, photographing it, and writing the record. A collection of hundreds of objects can take months to catalog properly. That is normal, and it is better to catalog slowly and well than to rush and create records that cannot be trusted. Start with the most important objects, or with the objects most at risk, and work through the rest in a sensible order.

Before you start, decide on your fields and your words. Choose the fields every record will have — the template in the checklist section below is a good starting point. Then agree on the words you will use: the same name for the same kind of object, the same way of writing dates, the same way of describing condition. Write these choices down as a short style note and keep it with the catalog, so that anyone who adds a record later follows the same rules. A catalog built by five people using five different habits is five catalogs, not one.

You also need a safe place for the records. Paper records need a dry, safe store. Digital records need a backup: a second copy kept somewhere else, updated regularly. A catalog that exists in one copy only is one accident away from being lost, and a lost catalog can take years to rebuild — if it can be rebuilt at all.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is starting without a plan. Someone begins cataloging with enthusiasm, records a dozen objects in detail, and then runs out of time. The next person starts differently, and soon the catalog is a mix of styles that cannot be searched. The fix is simple: agree the fields and the words first, write them down, and follow them from the first record. A slow, consistent start beats a fast, chaotic one.

The second mistake is writing records that only the writer can understand. Short notes like 'old pot, brown' are useless to anyone else, including the writer five years later. Write records for a stranger: full sentences where they help, full words instead of private abbreviations, and enough detail that someone who has never seen the object can picture it. If you would not understand your own record in ten years, rewrite it now.

The third mistake is forgetting the location. An object whose location is not recorded is an object that is lost, even if it is sitting on a shelf somewhere. Record the location in the catalog, and update it every time the object moves — to display, to a loan, to conservation, or to a new store. A simple habit — update the record before you move the object, not after — prevents most losses.

  • Never give two objects the same accession number — uniqueness is the whole point.
  • Never mark an object in a way that could damage it — ask a conservator if you are unsure.
  • Never write a record from memory alone — examine the object and measure it.
  • Never skip the photograph — it is the part of the record that gets used most.
  • Never keep the catalog in one copy only — back it up and keep the backup elsewhere.
  • Never let the catalog fall years behind the shelves — update records when objects move.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist for every object you catalog. Below it is a simple catalog template: the fields that every record should have, with a note on what each one means. Copy the template and adapt it to your collection — add fields if you need them, but keep the same fields for every object.

  • Give the object a unique accession number and mark it safely.
  • Write the record using the same fields and words as every other record.
  • Measure the object and record its dimensions.
  • Photograph the object from several angles, including marks and damage.
  • Note the condition honestly and flag anything needing a conservator.
  • Record the location, and update it whenever the object moves.
  • File the record so it can be found by number, by name and by topic.
  • Back up the catalog and keep the backup somewhere else.

Accession numberThe unique code for this object, used nowhere else.

Maker or artistWho made it, if known; write unknown rather than guessing.
Date madeWhen it was made, as exactly as you know it, or a range.
Place madeWhere it was made, if known.

DimensionsHeight, width and depth, with the unit you use.

The template is a starting point, not a rule. A collection of photographs needs different fields from a collection of tools, and a natural history collection needs different fields again. Adapt the template to your collection, but once you have chosen your fields, use the same ones for every object. Consistency is what makes a catalog searchable and useful.

Tips from real practice

Catalog in small, regular sessions rather than rare marathons. An hour a week, every week, catalogs a collection steadily and keeps the work enjoyable. A weekend blitz catalogs a lot at once and then stops, because nobody wants to do it again. The collections that get cataloged are the ones where the work becomes a habit, not an event.

Write down what you do not know. A record that says 'maker unknown, date uncertain' is honest and useful. A record that guesses is worse than a blank, because the guess becomes fact in the next person's mind. Questions are part of a good catalog: they show the next researcher where to look.

Use the catalog. A catalog that nobody reads will slowly stop being updated. Search it when a visitor asks a question, use it to plan a display, and share interesting records with your visitors. Every time the catalog answers a question, its value becomes obvious, and the work of keeping it up to date feels worthwhile.

Finally, plan for the catalog to outlive you. Write records a stranger could use, keep the style note with the catalog, and make sure someone else knows where the catalog and its backup live. A collection's memory should not depend on one person's memory. That is, in the end, what a catalog is for.

Questions people ask

What is in a museum catalog record?

A record usually has a unique accession number, the object name, the maker or artist if known, the date and place it was made, the materials, the dimensions, how the museum acquired it, its condition, its location, and photographs. The exact fields vary by collection, but one record per object and consistent details are the rule everywhere.

How do museums photograph their objects?

Against a plain background, in even light, from the front and from any angle that shows an important detail. Marks, signatures, labels and damage are photographed close up. The photographs are labelled with the object's accession number and kept with the record, so researchers can see the object without handling it.

What is a museum loan?

A loan is when one museum lends an object to another museum for a display, usually for a set period. The catalog record travels with the object, including its condition, so both museums know what was lent and in what state. Loans are agreed in writing, with clear terms about care, insurance and return.

How do small museums organize a collection?

With the same ideas as large museums, on a smaller scale: one record per object, consistent fields and words, a photograph, and a clear location. A simple spreadsheet and a camera are enough to start. What matters is consistency and keeping the catalog up to date, not expensive software.

What details matter most in a record?

The accession number, because it links the record to the object; the object name and a plain description; the condition; and the location. Without the location, an object is effectively lost even if it is on a shelf somewhere. Everything else adds value, but these four make the record work.

Can a private collector use the same system?

Yes, and it brings the same benefits: a record of what you own, where each piece came from, and what condition it is in. It makes the collection easier to insure, to share with others, and to pass on one day, because the knowledge is written down instead of living only in one person's memory.
