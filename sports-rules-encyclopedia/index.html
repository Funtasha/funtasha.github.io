Team sports and individual sports

Team sports — football, cricket, basketball, volleyball, hockey, rugby, baseball — share a pattern: two sides, a shared ball or puck, and a contest for territory. The rules mostly exist to keep that contest fair: offside rules stop players camping by the goal, foul rules stop the stronger team simply pushing the weaker one around, and time rules stop a winning team from hiding the ball forever. Learn one team sport well and the next one comes faster, because the ideas repeat.

Individual sports — tennis, badminton, table tennis, swimming races, athletics — are simpler to follow but harder to play well. There is no teammate to cover a mistake, and the rules are mostly about the contest itself: the ball must land in, the serve must be fair, the start must be clean. The scoring in these sports is often the confusing part — tennis goes 15, 30, 40 for reasons nobody quite agrees on — so this encyclopedia spends extra time on how scoring works in each one.

How scoring works

Scoring systems fall into a few families. The simplest is goals or runs: one goal is one point, and the team with more wins — football, hockey and baseball all work this way, with runs in cricket and baseball adding variety. Then there is points scoring, where different actions are worth different amounts: basketball gives 2, 3 or 1 point depending on the shot, and rugby gives 5 for a try, 2 for a conversion and 3 for a penalty or drop goal.

Then there is set scoring, used by tennis, badminton, volleyball and table tennis: win points to win games, win games to win sets, win sets to win the match. The "win by two" rule appears in all of these, because a one-point win at the end of a long contest feels wrong — the winner has to prove it twice. And then there is time and distance: in swimming and athletics, the clock and the tape measure decide, and the rules are mostly about fair starts and legal technique.

Fouls: what they are and why they matter

Every sport has fouls, and every foul exists for one of three reasons: safety, fairness, or the shape of the game. Safety fouls — high tackles in rugby, dangerous play in hockey, charging in basketball — protect players from injury. Fairness fouls — handball in football, double dribbling in basketball, a foot fault in tennis — stop players gaining an advantage the game did not intend. And shape fouls — offside in football, a forward pass in rugby — keep the game looking like itself: without the offside rule, football would become a game of long balls to a striker standing by the goal.

Penalties for fouls scale with seriousness. A small foul usually costs possession or a point. A repeated or serious foul earns a card, a free throw, or a spell off the field. The worst fouls earn a sending off. When you watch a match and the referee blows the whistle, ask which of the three reasons applies — it makes the decision easier to understand, and it makes you a better watcher.

Worked example: following a football match

You are watching your first football match. In the first half, a player is shown a yellow card for a late tackle — a warning, and a second yellow in the same match means a red card and sending off. Then a goal is disallowed for offside: the scorer was ahead of the last defender when the ball was played, which the offside rule forbids. In the second half, a defender handles the ball in the penalty area, and the referee points to the spot — a penalty kick from twelve yards, with only the goalkeeper to beat.

None of these moments needs an expert to understand. The card system, the offside rule and the penalty are all in the football entry above, in plain words. By the end of the match you have seen all three in action, and the next match will be easier still. That is how sports knowledge grows — one rule, one match, one moment at a time.

Worked example: understanding a cricket score

A cricket score looks like a code until someone explains it: "England 287 for 6" means England have scored 287 runs and lost 6 of their 10 wickets. A wicket is a batter getting out, and a team has ten wickets to spend. When you hear "LBW", it means leg before wicket: the batter is out because the ball would have hit the stumps but hit the batter's leg instead. A "four" is a ball hit to the boundary, worth 4 runs; a "six" is a ball hit over the boundary without bouncing.

With those few facts, a cricket scorecard opens up. You can see who is batting well, who is bowling well, and whether the batting team is ahead or behind. The cricket entry above covers the rest — how runs are scored, the ways to get out, and why matches come in so many different lengths. Cricket rewards the patient watcher more than almost any other sport.

Common rule myths, corrected

Sports are full of myths that sound true. "A football match is always 90 minutes" — no, stoppage time is added for injuries and delays, and knockout matches can go to extra time and penalties. "A tennis player wins a set at 6 games" — usually, but they must win by two games, and a tiebreak decides a set at 6 games all in most formats. "You cannot use your hands in football" — the goalkeeper can, inside the penalty area, and a throw-in is taken with the hands.

Other myths are older. "Cricket takes five days" — only Test matches do; many matches last a few hours. "Basketball players can take as many steps as they like" — no, travelling is a violation, and the rule is enforced. The way to settle any of these is the same: check the official rulebook. Questions people ask

How long is a football match?

A football match lasts 90 minutes, in two halves of 45 minutes, plus stoppage time added by the referee for injuries and delays. Knockout matches that are level after 90 minutes usually go to extra time (two halves of 15 minutes) and then to a penalty shoot-out if still level.

What is LBW in cricket?

LBW means leg before wicket. A batter is out LBW if the ball would have hit the stumps but instead hit the batter's leg (or body) first, provided the ball pitched in line and the batter was not offering a shot in some cases. It is one of the most argued-about rules in cricket.

How does scoring work in tennis?

Points go 15, 30, 40, then game, and a player must win by two points — at 40 all (deuce), a player must win two points in a row. Games make sets, usually first to 6 games by two, and matches are usually best of 3 or 5 sets.

What is a foul in basketball?

A foul is illegal contact with an opponent — pushing, holding, tripping or charging. Personal fouls give the other team free throws or possession, and a player who commits five fouls (six in the NBA) is fouled out of the game. Travelling and double dribbling are violations, not fouls, and give the ball to the other team.

How many players are in a volleyball team?

Six players from each team are on the court at once, and a squad usually has twelve players. Teams use rally scoring to 25 points (win by two), best of five sets, and players rotate positions as they win the serve.

Where do I find the official rules?

The cards on this page link to the official bodies for each sport — FIFA for football, the ICC for cricket, FIBA for basketball, the ITF for tennis, and the rest. Their rulebooks are the final word, and they are free to read online.
