Grammar Learning Games

Learn English grammar by playing. Three games — tenses, articles, prepositions — ten rounds each, with instant feedback and a one-line rule after every answer.

These games teach the most-used rules — the ones you need every day. They do not cover every rule in English, and they are not a full grammar course. The last section links to full grammar references for the rest.

What these games do — and how to play

This page teaches English grammar by playing. There are three games: a tense quiz, an article picker, and a preposition match. Each game has ten rounds. In every round you see a sentence with a gap and three choices; pick one, and you get instant feedback — right or wrong — with a one-line rule that explains why.

Pick a game with the buttons above, answer the first question, and read the rule after each answer, right or wrong. The rule is the lesson: the game shows you the pattern, and the rule names it so you can use it again. At the end of ten rounds you get your score, and your best score for each game is saved on this device.

Play one game at a time. Ten rounds take about five minutes, which is the right length — short enough to stay focused, long enough to learn something. Come back tomorrow and play the same game again; the questions stay in the same order, so you can feel yourself getting faster and surer.

Why games work for grammar

Grammar rules are easy to read and hard to remember, because reading a rule is not the same as using it. Games fix that by making you choose. Every round forces a decision — goes or go, a or the, in or on — and the instant feedback tells you whether your instinct was right. That moment of choosing, right or wrong, is where learning happens.

Games also keep the stakes low. A wrong answer in a game costs nothing, so you are willing to guess — and guessing is useful, because a wrong guess you remember teaches more than a rule you skimmed. The one-line rule after each answer turns the guess into knowledge, whether you were right or wrong.

There is a third reason games work: they give you a score. A score is a small, honest measure of progress. Play the tense quiz today and score six; play it again in a week and score nine. That improvement is real, and it is yours — no teacher needed to tell you that you are getting better.

The three games: tenses, articles, prepositions

The tense quiz asks you to choose the right tense for the sentence: present simple, past simple, present continuous, present perfect, and the forms around them. Tenses are the skeleton of English — get them wrong and even a perfect vocabulary sounds confused. The quiz covers the situations you meet every day: routines, finished actions, actions in progress, and experience up to now.

The article picker asks you to choose a, an, or the. Articles are small words that cause big trouble, because many languages do not have them at all. The game teaches the patterns: an before vowel sounds, the for things already known or unique, a for a first mention. Ten rounds cover the rules you will use hundreds of times a day.

The preposition match asks you to choose in, on, or at. These three little words carry an unfair amount of meaning in English — time, place, and position all depend on them. The game teaches the big patterns: in for months, years, and cities; on for days and surfaces; at for exact times and points. Learn those and most everyday prepositions fall into place.

How to use your score

Your score is a guide, not a grade. Eight or more out of ten means you know this rule well — move on to another game or a harder level of practice. Five to seven means the rule is half-learned: play again tomorrow and watch the score rise. Below five means the rule is new to you, and that is useful information, not failure.

Pay more attention to which questions you miss than to the number. If you always miss the present perfect questions, that one tense needs your attention — read its rule again, write three sentences of your own with it, and play the game again. One weak spot, fixed properly, is worth more than ten easy wins.

Your best score for each game is saved on this device, so you can track your progress over days and weeks. Do not chase a perfect ten for its own sake — chase the feeling that the answers are coming faster and with less doubt. Speed and confidence are the real signs that a rule has sunk in.

Worked examples: three questions, explained

Take this tense question: "She ___ to school every day." The choices are goes, go, going. The answer is goes, because the subject is "she" and the time is a routine — present simple with he, she, or it takes an -s. "Going" would need "is", and "go" would be right for "they" but not for "she". One rule, one letter, one right answer.

Take this article question: "I need ___ hour to finish." The choices are an, a, the. The answer is an — not because of the letter h, but because of the sound: "hour" starts with a vowel sound, since the h is silent. Articles follow sound, not spelling, which is why "an hour" is right and "a hour" is wrong.

Take this preposition question: "The meeting is ___ Monday." The choices are on, in, at. The answer is on, because English uses on for days of the week. It is a pattern worth memorising whole: on Monday, on Tuesday, on Friday — and in for months, at for times. Learn the pattern once and a hundred sentences follow.

Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

The most common mistake is learning rules without examples. A rule like "use the present perfect for experience up to now" means nothing until you have seen it in five real sentences. These games give you the sentences first and the rule second — the right order. When you read a rule in a book, always write your own example before moving on.

The second mistake is playing once and moving on. One game teaches you the questions; playing again teaches you the rules. The second and third plays are where the learning happens, because your brain has to retrieve the rule instead of just recognising it. Retrieval — pulling the answer out of your own memory — is what makes knowledge stick.

The third mistake is guessing without reading the rule. If you tap an answer and skip the feedback, the game becomes a lottery. Read the one-line rule after every answer, especially the ones you got right by luck. A right answer you cannot explain is a rule you do not yet own.

What to do after the games

When a game feels easy, the next step is to use its rules in real sentences. Write five sentences using the tense you just practised, or describe your day using in, on, and at correctly. Games teach recognition; writing and speaking teach production, and production is the skill that matters.

For the rules these games do not cover, full grammar references are the next stop. The Cambridge Grammar reference, linked in the card below, explains every rule with examples, free, and is one of the most trusted grammar sources for learners. British Council LearnEnglish also has grammar pages with explanations and exercises at every level.

Keep playing, too. A game you have mastered is still useful as a warm-up: one quick round before writing or speaking reminds your brain of the patterns. Five minutes of play, then real English — that is a routine that builds grammar without ever feeling like a lesson.

Questions people ask

Can games really teach grammar?

Yes, when each answer teaches a rule — which is how these games work. Choosing an answer forces a decision, instant feedback tells you if your instinct was right, and the one-line rule names the pattern so you can use it again. Games alone are not a full course, but they are one of the fastest ways to learn the most-used rules.

Which tense should I learn first?

Start with the present simple for routines and facts, then the past simple for finished actions, then the present continuous for actions happening now. Those three cover most everyday conversation. The present perfect comes next — it is the tense learners find hardest, because it connects the past to the present.

When do I use a, an and the?

Use a or an for a singular thing mentioned for the first time, or for a job — 'a teacher', 'an apple'. Use an before a vowel sound. Use the for something already known, already mentioned, or unique — 'the sun', 'the best day'. The article game practises exactly these patterns.

In, on or at — how do I choose?

Use in for months, years, cities and countries, and for things inside something. Use on for days of the week and for surfaces. Use at for exact times, for night, and for specific points like a bus stop. Learn those three patterns and most everyday prepositions fall into place.

How long should I play each day?

Ten minutes is enough: one game, ten rounds, reading the rule after each answer. Play the same game again the next day — the second and third plays are where the rules sink in, because your brain has to retrieve the answer instead of just recognising it.

What do I do after the games?
