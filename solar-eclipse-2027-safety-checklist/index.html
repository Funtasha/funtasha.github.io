How the checklist works

The checklist has ten steps, and each one is a small job you can do before eclipse day. Tick a step when it is done, and the progress bar fills up as you go. When all ten steps are ticked, you are ready: your glasses are checked, your viewer is made, your bag is packed, and your group knows the plan.

Work through the list in order if you can, because the steps are arranged from the most important to the most practical. The glasses come first, because without them you cannot watch the partial phases at all. The pinhole viewer comes next, as a backup and as a way for children to watch safely. The rest of the list is about comfort, planning, and knowing the rules of the day.

Do not leave the list until the last minute. Eclipse glasses sell out as the date approaches, and the best viewing spots fill early. Starting a few weeks ahead means you can order glasses calmly, make your viewer with the children, and pack your bag the night before without rushing.

Why eclipse glasses matter

The Sun is dangerous to look at, even when most of it is covered. During a partial eclipse, the part of the Sun that is still visible is just as bright as on any normal day, and looking at it without protection can damage the retina, the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye. The damage is painless at the time, which makes it more dangerous, not less: you would not feel it happening.

The glasses that protect you are certified to the ISO 12312-2 standard, which means they block the harmful light while letting through just enough to see the Sun's disc safely. They look like simple cardboard glasses with very dark lenses, and they cost very little, but they are the only safe way to look directly at the Sun during the partial phases.

Buy them from a trusted seller, and be wary of fakes. If the glasses have no ISO marking, or the marking looks printed on as an afterthought, do not trust them. This page gives general information only: if you are unsure about a pair of glasses, ask a local astronomy group or an optician for advice before the day.

Checking your glasses

New glasses are not automatically safe glasses. Before eclipse day, hold each pair up to a bright light and look through the lenses for scratches, holes, or cracks. If the lenses are damaged in any way, throw the pair away and use a fresh one: even a small hole can let through enough light to harm your eyes.

Check the fit too, especially for children. Glasses that slip down the nose or leave gaps at the sides are a problem, because a child will push them up, peer around them, or take them off entirely. A simple strap, or a pair sized for a smaller face, makes a real difference. Practise wearing them indoors first, so the strange darkness does not surprise anyone on the day.

Pack a spare pair if you can. Glasses get sat on, dropped, and blown away, and a spare in the bag means one accident does not end the day. Keep the spares in their original sleeve or a small envelope so the lenses stay clean and unscratched.

The pinhole viewer

A pinhole viewer is the simplest safe way to watch an eclipse, and it needs no special equipment at all. Take a sheet of card, make a small hole in the middle with a pin, and hold it up so sunlight passes through the hole onto a second sheet held below. You will see a small image of the Sun on the lower sheet, and as the eclipse progresses, the image changes shape with it.

A cardboard box works even better. Cut a hole in one end, cover it with foil, prick a tiny hole in the foil, and tape a sheet of white paper inside the other end. Stand with your back to the Sun, hold the box so light enters the hole, and watch the image on the paper. Children love this, because they build it themselves and it feels like a science experiment.

The pinhole viewer is also your backup. If your glasses are damaged, lost, or forgotten, the viewer lets you follow the whole eclipse safely without ever looking at the Sun. Make one even if you have glasses, and bring it on the day: it costs nothing, weighs nothing, and might save the experience.

Watching with children

Children enjoy an eclipse most when they know what to expect. Before the day, explain in simple words what will happen: the Moon will slide in front of the Sun, the light will change, and for a few minutes it will feel like night in the middle of the day. Show them pictures of a total eclipse, let them practise with the glasses and the pinhole viewer, and agree the rules together.

The rules are simple, and they are worth rehearsing. Glasses on before looking up, and glasses off only after looking away. Never look at the Sun without glasses except during totality, when the Sun's disc is fully covered. Glasses straight back on the moment the Sun starts to reappear. A child who has practised these steps at home will follow them calmly on the day.

Keep the day comfortable for younger children. Bring snacks, water, and something to sit on, and be ready for the wait: the partial phases last over an hour before totality begins. A pinhole viewer, a drawing pad, and a simple explanation of what is happening will keep them interested, and the moment of darkness will be something they remember for years.

The day itself

Eclipse day starts early. The best viewing spots fill up hours before totality, and traffic near popular sites can be slow, so arrive with time to spare and set up calmly. Find an open view of the sky, lay out your mat or chairs, and put your bag where you can reach it without standing up.

Heat is the main comfort problem, especially in Egypt, where Luxor can reach about 43 degrees at midday in August. Drink water regularly, wear a hat, use sun cream, and sit in shade during the long wait before totality. If you feel dizzy or unwell in the heat, move into shade, drink water, and rest: the eclipse will still be there, and your health matters more than a perfect viewing spot.

Agree a meeting point with your group in case you get separated, and keep your phone charged. Check the weather forecast the day before, and know which direction you would move if cloud threatens: the path is about 258 kilometres wide, so a short move usually keeps you inside it.

What to do during totality

When totality begins, the rules change for a few precious minutes. Once the Sun's disc is fully covered, it is safe to look up with the naked eye and see the corona, the Sun's outer atmosphere, shining around the black disc of the Moon. Take your glasses off, look around, and enjoy it: in Luxor, totality lasts about 6 minutes 20 seconds, long enough to take it in slowly.

Watch the sky, the horizon, and the people around you. The horizon glows orange in every direction, stars come out, and the temperature drops. Many watchers say the minutes pass far too quickly, so put the camera down for at least part of it and simply watch with your own eyes.

The moment the Sun starts to reappear, put your glasses straight back on. The first flash of sunlight is bright enough to harm your eyes, and the partial phases that follow need glasses just as much as the ones before. The rule is easy to remember: glasses off only while the Sun is fully covered, and back on the instant it is not.

This checklist is general information only, not medical advice. If your eyes hurt or your vision changes after viewing, seek medical advice promptly. Never look at the Sun through ordinary sunglasses, a camera lens, or binoculars without a proper solar filter.

Questions people ask

What glasses do I need for the eclipse?

You need ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses, bought from a trusted seller and checked for damage before the day. They block the harmful light while letting you see the Sun's disc safely.

Are ordinary sunglasses enough?

No, never. Ordinary sunglasses, no matter how dark, do not block enough light to make looking at the Sun safe. Smoked glass, old camera film, and phone screens are not safe either.

What is a pinhole viewer?

A pinhole viewer is a simple way to watch the eclipse indirectly. Sunlight passes through a small hole in a sheet of card and projects an image of the Sun onto a second sheet, so you never look at the Sun itself.

Can children watch the eclipse?

Yes, with supervision. Practise the rules at home first: glasses on before looking up, glasses off only during totality, and glasses back on the moment the Sun reappears. A pinhole viewer is a good choice for younger children.

When do I take the glasses off?

Only during totality, when the Sun's disc is fully covered. Put them back on the moment the Sun starts to reappear, because the partial phases before and after totality always need glasses.

What if my glasses are damaged?

Do not use them. Even a small scratch or hole can let through harmful light. Use a fresh pair, or watch with a pinhole viewer instead, which needs no glasses at all.
