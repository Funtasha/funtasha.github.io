Luxor, Egypt: the longest show on land

If there is one place every eclipse watcher talks about for 2027, it is Luxor. The city sits almost exactly on the centre line of the path of totality, and the eclipse there lasts about 6 minutes 20 seconds, the longest totality of the whole eclipse on land. The longest totality anywhere on land is about 6 minutes 23 seconds, in the Egyptian desert near Luxor, and this eclipse is the longest total solar eclipse visible from land until the year 2114.

Totality in Luxor runs from about 1:02 p.m. to about 1:08 p.m. local time, with the Sun about 82 degrees high in the sky, almost directly overhead. That height is good news for watchers: you will not need a low horizon or a hilltop, just an open patch of sky. It also means the Sun is at its fiercest, and Luxor in August can reach about 43 degrees in the middle of the day, so shade, water, and a hat are as important as your eclipse glasses.

Egypt also has the clearest skies of any country on the path in August, which is why so many eclipse travellers choose it. Clear skies are never guaranteed, but the odds are better in the Egyptian desert than almost anywhere else on the route. If you go, arrive a day or two early, rest, and enjoy Luxor itself: the temples and the river are worth the trip even without an eclipse.

The other cities on the path

The path of totality is about 258 kilometres wide, and it crosses southern Spain, Gibraltar, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Yemen, and north-eastern Somalia. The cities in this planner sit inside that band, so each of them will see the Sun fully covered for several minutes in the middle of the day.

Cádiz, on the Atlantic coast of southern Spain, is the first major city on the path, and a good choice for watchers who want to stay in Europe. Tangier in Morocco and Tunis in Tunisia are both inside the path and both easy to reach, with warm August weather and plenty of open sky. Sohag in Egypt sits on the Nile south of Cairo, and Jeddah on the Red Sea coast of Saudi Arabia is the largest city on the path after the eclipse leaves Egypt. Sana'a, high in the mountains of Yemen, is also inside the path.

For all of these cities, this page deliberately gives no exact minute or duration. Eclipse timings change from street to street, and a number copied from the wrong source would be worse than no number at all. A local eclipse guide, published nearer the day, will give you the timing for your exact spot, and that is the number to trust.

Aswan and Cairo: what you would see

Two Egyptian cities cause the most confusion, so this planner includes them both with a clear answer. Aswan, in the far south of Egypt, is not in the path of totality. From Aswan you would see a deep partial eclipse: most of the Sun covered, the light dimmed and strange, but the Sun never fully hidden, so the sky would not go dark and the corona would not appear.

Cairo, the capital, is also outside the path. From Cairo you would see only a partial eclipse, with part of the Sun covered and the rest shining as usual. It is still worth watching with proper glasses, and a partial eclipse is a memorable sight in its own right, but it is not the same experience as totality, and anyone travelling to Egypt specially for the eclipse should plan to be inside the path.

If you find yourself in Aswan or Cairo on the day, the honest choice is to enjoy the partial eclipse safely, with certified glasses, rather than to rush across the country at the last minute. But if you are still planning, pick a city inside the path, such as Luxor, and give yourself the full experience: several minutes of darkness in the middle of the day is something most people see only once in a lifetime.

What totality feels like

People who have seen a total eclipse describe it as one of the strangest and most moving things in nature. In the hour before totality, the light changes slowly: shadows sharpen, colours look odd, and the temperature begins to fall. Birds go quiet, and the air feels different, as if evening were arriving hours early.

Then, in the last minute, everything happens at once. The last sliver of Sun vanishes, the sky goes dark, and the corona appears: the Sun's outer atmosphere, a pale crown of light around a black disc. Stars come out, the horizon glows orange in every direction, and the crowd, if there is one, usually gasps and then falls silent. In Luxor this lasts about six minutes and twenty seconds, long enough to take it in slowly, look around, and simply watch.

When totality ends, the first flash of sunlight returns in an instant, and the world brightens again within minutes. Many watchers say the minutes of darkness feel far shorter than they are, so the best advice is to put the camera down for at least part of it and watch with your own eyes. You will remember the feeling long after you have forgotten the photographs.

Packing for eclipse day

A good eclipse bag is small and simple. The one thing you must have is a pair of ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses, checked for scratches or damage before the day. Ordinary sunglasses are never safe for looking at the Sun, and neither are smoked glass, camera film, or a phone screen held up to the sky. If your glasses are damaged, do not use them: a pinhole viewer, made from a cardboard box or a sheet of card with a small hole, is a safe way to watch the partial phases indirectly.

Next comes comfort. In Egypt and Saudi Arabia in August, the heat is serious: water, a hat, sun cream, and shade are essential, especially if you will be standing outside for hours. A light chair or a mat makes the wait easier, and a small bag keeps your glasses safe when you are not wearing them. Bring a phone charger if you plan to take photographs, and agree a meeting point with your group in case you get separated in a crowd.

Finally, bring patience. Eclipse day means crowds, traffic, and waiting, and the best viewing spots fill early. Arrive with time to spare, set up calmly, and enjoy the build-up: the changing light in the hour before totality is part of the show, and the people around you are part of the memory.

Planning your trip

If you are travelling for the eclipse, start early. Flights and hotels in Luxor and along the path will fill up months ahead, and prices rise as the date approaches. Book somewhere you can cancel if plans change, and choose accommodation near your viewing spot so you are not stuck in traffic on the morning of the eclipse.

Check the practical details for your destination: entry rules, local customs, and the weather. August is hot everywhere on the path, and in Egypt it can be extremely hot at midday, so plan your sightseeing for the early morning and the evening, and rest in the shade during the middle of the day. Keep your eclipse glasses somewhere safe and cool, and do not leave them in a hot car.

Most importantly, have a backup plan for cloud. Even in Egypt, the sky can cloud over, so know which direction the path runs and be ready to move a short distance if the forecast changes. A clear sky a few kilometres away is worth a short journey on the morning of the eclipse, and the path is wide enough that a small move usually keeps you inside it.

Questions people ask

Which city has the longest totality in 2027?

Luxor in Egypt has the longest totality on land, about 6 minutes 20 seconds, from about 1:02 p.m. to about 1:08 p.m. local time. The longest totality anywhere on land is about 6 minutes 23 seconds, in the Egyptian desert near Luxor.

Is Aswan in the path of totality?

No. Aswan is not in the path. From Aswan you would see a deep partial eclipse, with most of the Sun covered but the disc never fully hidden, so the sky would not go dark.

What will I see from Cairo?

Cairo sees only a partial eclipse. Part of the Sun is covered, but the disc is never fully hidden, so there is no totality and no darkness. It is still worth watching with certified eclipse glasses.

Do I need eclipse glasses?

Yes. You need ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses for the partial phases, before and after totality. Ordinary sunglasses are never safe for looking at the Sun.

When is the total solar eclipse?

The total solar eclipse is on Monday 2 August 2027. The path of totality runs from southern Spain through North Africa and the Middle East to north-eastern Somalia.

How wide is the path of totality?

The path of totality is about 258 kilometres wide. Only places inside that band see the Sun fully covered; everywhere else sees a partial eclipse or nothing at all.
