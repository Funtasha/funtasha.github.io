What this page does

An academic journal is where new research is published, checked and kept. When a scientist finishes a study, a historian finishes an argument, or a doctor finishes a trial, the work is sent to a journal, read by other experts, and — if it passes — published for anyone to read and build on. Journals are the record of what we know, and this page is a map of the most trusted routes into that record.

The directory below covers six broad subjects: science, medicine, engineering, social science, humanities and education. For each route you will find what it covers and a link to its official home. Some routes are single journals, like Nature and Science. Some are databases that search thousands of journals at once, like PubMed and Google Scholar. Some are publishers with hundreds of journals under one roof. Use the subject filter and the search box to find the routes that match your field.

This page points to journal homes and databases. It does not publish papers, it does not accept submissions, and it does not guarantee acceptance anywhere. Getting published takes good work, patience and a fair amount of rejection — this page can help you aim at the right place, but the work itself is yours.

How to use the directory

Start with your subject. Pick it from the list, and the directory shows only the routes that cover it. Then read what each route covers: a route that publishes physics will not want your history essay, and a medical database will not help you find a paper on poetry. When a route looks right, tap it to open its official site in a new tab — every link here goes to the journal, database or publisher itself, never to a middleman.

If you are looking for something to read, start with the databases: PubMed for medicine and life sciences, Google Scholar for everything, JSTOR for the humanities and social sciences. Search for your topic there, and follow the results to the journals that publish in your area. If you are looking for somewhere to publish, start with the journals you already read and cite — the ones that published the papers your work builds on are usually the right first choice.

Keep the directory beside you while you work. Finding the right journal is not a single search but a habit: check a new route when you start a new topic, and check the directory again when a paper is rejected — the next journal on your list is often a better fit than the first one was.

How peer review works

Peer review is the check that stands between a finished manuscript and a published paper. When an author submits a paper to a journal, an editor reads it first and decides whether it fits the journal at all. If it does, the editor sends it to two or three experts in the field — the reviewers — who read it closely and write back with their judgement: is the work sound, is it new, and is it clearly written?

Reviewers almost never simply accept a paper. The usual answer is "revise and resubmit": the authors must fix problems, answer questions, run an extra check, or rewrite a section, and then send the paper back. Sometimes the paper goes through two or three rounds of this. Sometimes it is rejected outright, and the authors try another journal. The process is slow — weeks to months is normal — and it can feel bruising, but it is the main reason published research is more trustworthy than a claim on a website.

Peer review is not perfect. Reviewers miss things, and occasionally a flawed paper gets through. But a paper in a peer-reviewed journal has been read and challenged by people who know the field, and that is worth a great deal. When you read a paper, check whether its journal uses peer review — and when you choose where to publish, peer review should be at the top of your list of requirements.

How to spot a predatory journal

A predatory journal is one that charges authors to publish while doing little or none of the checking that real journals do. It looks like a journal, it has a website and an editorial board, but it will publish almost anything for a fee. Papers in predatory journals are rarely read, rarely cited, and can damage the author's reputation.

The warning signs are consistent. The journal invites you by email, flattering you and promising publication in days. Its website is vague about who the editors are, or lists editors who have never heard of it. It promises very fast peer review — real review takes weeks. Its fees are hidden until after acceptance, or much higher than the journal's standing would justify. It is not listed in the standard directories, like the Directory of Open Access Journals, and its name is suspiciously close to a famous journal's name.

Before you submit anywhere, run three checks. First, search for the journal in the Directory of Open Access Journals and in the standard databases — a real journal will be listed. Second, read a few papers it has published and ask whether the work looks serious. Third, check the fees and the review process on the journal's own site: real journals state both plainly. If any of those checks fails, walk away. A rejection from a real journal is worth more than an acceptance from a fake one.

How to pick a journal for a first paper

Choosing a journal for your first paper feels like a big decision, and it is — but it is not a once-only decision. Most papers are rejected at least once, and a rejection usually comes with useful feedback and a clearer idea of where the paper fits. Aim sensibly, not fearfully.

Start with fit. Read the journal's scope statement and its recent issues. Does it publish work like yours — the same methods, the same length, the same kind of argument? A paper on classroom reading belongs in an education journal, not in a general science journal, however famous the general journal is. Then check the practical details: does the journal charge a fee, and can you afford it? How long does review usually take? Is the journal open access, and does your funder or university require that?

Then ask someone who knows. A supervisor, a colleague or a librarian will know which journals matter in your field and which to avoid. Librarians, in particular, are underused experts on journals: helping researchers choose where to publish is part of their job, and they will spot a predatory journal faster than anyone. Finally, read the author guidelines before you write the cover letter — journals reject papers for ignoring the guidelines more often than beginners expect.

Worked example: choosing a journal

Imagine a student who has finished a small study on how primary-school children learn to read in a second language. She has three journals in mind: a famous general education journal, a smaller journal on second-language learning, and a new open-access journal that emailed her last week.

She checks the famous journal first. Its scope fits, but its recent papers are all large studies with hundreds of children, and hers has thirty. It would probably be rejected on size alone. The smaller journal is a better fit: it publishes small classroom studies, its review takes about three months, and it charges no fee. The emailed journal fails the checks — it is not listed in the standard directories, it promises review in five days, and its editors cannot be found anywhere else. She deletes the email.

She submits to the smaller journal, gets a "revise and resubmit" after ten weeks, revises, and is accepted two months later. The paper is read by the people who matter — other teachers and researchers in her field — because she chose fit over fame. That is the whole skill of picking a journal: find the place where your readers already are.

Common mistakes

  • Aiming only at the most famous journal. Fame is not fit. A paper in the right specialist journal is read by more of the right people than a paper rejected by a famous one.
  • Ignoring the author guidelines. Journals reject papers that ignore length limits, reference styles and formatting rules. Read the guidelines before you submit, not after.
  • Paying to publish without checking. Some real journals charge fees, but a fee alone proves nothing. Check the journal is listed, read its papers, and confirm the fee is stated openly.
  • Submitting to several journals at once. Journals require that a paper is under review at one place only. Submitting to two at once can get the paper rejected everywhere.
  • Taking rejection personally. Rejection is the normal outcome, even for good papers. Read the reviewers' comments, improve the paper, and send it to the next journal on your list.
  • Trusting an email invitation. Real journals rarely invite unknown authors by email. An invitation promising fast publication is a warning sign, not an honour.

What this page cannot do

This page cannot publish your paper, review it, or guarantee it will be accepted anywhere — those decisions belong to the journals themselves, and to the reviewers they choose. It cannot list every journal in the world: there are tens of thousands, and this directory covers the best-known routes, not all of them. Fees, review times and policies change, so always check a journal's own site for the current details before you submit. What this page can do is point you to trusted places and teach you how to judge the rest — and that is the part that matters most.

Questions people ask

What is an academic journal?

An academic journal is a publication where new research is checked by experts and published for others to read and build on. Journals cover a field or a subject, publish issues on a regular schedule, and keep a permanent record of the research in their area.

What is peer review?

Peer review is the check that stands between a manuscript and publication. An editor sends the paper to two or three experts in the field, who read it closely and judge whether the work is sound, new and clearly written. Most papers are revised at least once before they are accepted.

How do I find a journal for my subject?

Start with the journals you already read and cite, then use the directory on this page to find routes in your subject. Check each journal's scope, recent issues, fees and review time, and ask a supervisor or librarian — they know which journals matter in your field.

What is a predatory journal?

A predatory journal charges authors to publish while doing little or none of the checking real journals do. Warning signs include email invitations promising fast publication, vague editors, hidden fees, and no listing in standard directories like the Directory of Open Access Journals.

Do I have to pay to publish?

It depends on the journal. Many traditional journals charge nothing to publish but charge readers to read. Many open-access journals charge the author a fee and let everyone read for free. Fees vary widely, so check the journal's own site — and be wary of any journal that hides its fees until after acceptance.

How long does publishing take?

Usually months, not days. Peer review alone commonly takes six to twelve weeks, and most papers go through at least one round of revision. From submission to publication, three to nine months is normal in many fields, and a year is not unusual.
