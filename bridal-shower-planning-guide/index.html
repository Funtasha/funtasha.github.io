Bridal Shower Planning Guide

This guide walks you through planning a bridal shower, from choosing a host and building the guest list to themes, food, games, gifts and thank-you notes. Use the checklist to keep track of every task, and pick a theme to get ideas for the day.

Who hosts, and who helps

A bridal shower is usually hosted by a close friend or family member of the couple, sometimes with a few helpers sharing the work. The host makes the big decisions — date, venue, guest list — and the helpers take on jobs like invitations, food, games and decorations. Splitting the work early keeps the planning light for everyone.

Before you plan anything, talk to the couple. Ask whether they want a shower at all, who they would love to see there, and whether they have any wishes or limits. A shower planned around the couple always feels better than one planned around a template.

Build the guest list

The usual rule is simple: invite only people who are also invited to the wedding. It keeps things comfortable for guests and avoids awkward questions later. Start with close family and friends, then add anyone the couple names, and agree on a final number before you book a venue.

Ask the couple about plus-ones and children early, and write the answers down so the invitations are consistent. If the couple has a big family, a slightly larger shower is fine — the rule is about the wedding list, not about a magic number.

Invitation timing

Send invitations four to six weeks before the shower, so guests have time to plan and to buy a gift. Each invitation should carry the date, time, venue, the host's name and a simple way to reply. If there is a gift list, include a short note about where to find it.

A reminder a week before the party is kind, especially for guests who travel. Keep the reminder short: date, time, venue and one line about what to expect. If the shower has a theme, mention it so guests can dress to match if they like.

Choose a theme

A theme gives the shower a look and makes decorating easy. A garden party, a tea party, a brunch, a travel theme or a simple colour palette all work well. Pick one that fits the couple — a travel theme suits a couple who love to explore, while a garden theme suits a relaxed afternoon outdoors.

Keep the decorations simple and repeat the theme in a few places: the table, a welcome sign and the cake. You do not need a new decoration for every corner of the room. A few thoughtful touches, done well, look better than a room full of clutter.

Food and non-alcoholic drinks

Match the food to the time of day. A morning shower suits brunch food — pastries, fruit and egg dishes — while an afternoon shower suits finger sandwiches, salads and cake. A drinks station with sparkling water, fruit juice and a simple punch keeps everyone refreshed, and keeping every drink non-alcoholic means every guest can join in.

Ask about allergies and dietary needs when you send the invitations, and label the dishes on the day. Put out half the food at the start and refill the table midway through, so everything stays fresh and the table never looks empty.

Games that break the ice

Two or three short games are enough, and the best ones help guests get to know each other. A quiz about the couple, a gift-themed bingo and a quick round of couple trivia all work well. Keep the rules short, let people sit out, and hand out small prizes so everyone leaves with a smile.

If the couple is shy about being the centre of attention, choose gentle games that do not put them on the spot. A wishing tree or a message book is a lovely alternative, and the couple keeps it long after the party.

Gifts

A gift list makes choosing easy for guests, so share it with the invitation if the couple has made one. If there is no list, suggest practical items the couple will actually use, and remind guests that a heartfelt card is always welcome on its own. Group gifts are a kind option for larger items.

Opening gifts in front of everyone is traditional at a shower, but it is not required. Ask the couple what they prefer, and if they would rather open gifts later, say so in advance. Keep a list of who gave what either way — it makes the thank-you notes much easier.

Thank-you notes

Thank-you notes are the couple's job, and the host can help by keeping the gift list and noting the giver of each present. Notes should go out within a few weeks of the shower, and a few warm sentences are enough: name the gift, say how it will be used, and thank the giver for coming.

Short handwritten cards work beautifully, and a thoughtful message is fine when time is short. What matters is that every guest feels thanked, not that every note is long.

Questions people ask

Who hosts a bridal shower?

A close friend or family member usually hosts, often with a few helpers sharing the work. The host plans the day and the couple simply enjoys it.

Should everyone at the shower be invited to the wedding?

Yes, that is the usual rule. It keeps things comfortable for guests and avoids awkward questions later.

When should invitations go out?

Four to six weeks before the shower gives guests time to plan and to choose a gift. A short reminder a week before is kind.

What if the couple does not want a gift list?

Then guests can choose something practical or give a heartfelt card. A group gift for a larger item is always a kind option.

How long should a bridal shower last?

Two or three hours is plenty: time to eat, play a game or two, open gifts and say goodbye without rushing.
