What goes in a media kit

A media kit is a one-page summary that answers a brand's questions before they have to ask them. It starts with who you are: your name, your handle and what you make. Then it shows your numbers — followers per platform and the total — so the brand can judge your reach in seconds. Then it describes your audience, because reach without the right audience is worth little to a brand. Keep every line short and factual, so the brand can scan the whole page without slowing down.

After the numbers comes your content: what you post about, and what you have already done with brands. Even a short line of past work helps — one honest collaboration tells a brand more than a long list of claims. If you have no brand work yet, say so plainly. "No brand work yet — open to first collabs" is a stronger line than an empty space or an invented history.

Finally, the kit lists your rates and how to reach you. Rates are plain numbers here: you agree the currency and the details with the brand directly. The contact line uses a placeholder, and you replace it with your own contact details when you send the kit. Nothing else belongs on the page. A media kit is not a CV and not a life story — it is the shortest honest version of why a brand should work with you.

What brands actually look for

Brands look at audience fit first and follower count second. A food brand wants people who cook, not just people who scroll. A study brand wants students. Your audience age and country, and the kind of content you make, tell a brand whether your followers are their customers. That is why this builder puts audience details right next to your numbers.

Brands also look for consistency. An account that posts regularly about one topic looks safer than an account with a bigger number that posts about everything. They read your recent posts, they check whether your comments look real, and they notice whether your content matches what your kit claims. A kit that describes your real content will always beat a kit that describes a bigger, vaguer version of you.

Finally, brands look for someone easy to work with. A clear kit, honest numbers, rates on the page and a short reply time all signal that working with you will be simple. You do not need a huge audience to be easy to work with. Many brands prefer smaller creators precisely because they reply quickly, follow the brief and care about the result.

Writing your rates honestly

Rates are the part most creators dread, and the part brands most want to see. A kit without rates forces the brand to ask, and many will simply move on to the next creator. Put your rates on the page, as plain numbers, one line each for a post, a video and a story. You can always negotiate — a published rate is a starting point, not a final price.

Set your rates by what the work costs you, not by what you think a big creator charges. A video takes filming, editing and your time; a story takes far less. Price the effort honestly and you will be able to stand behind the number when a brand asks why. If you have never charged before, start with a modest number and raise it as your audience and your experience grow.

Never copy another creator's rates and never invent a rate to look expensive. Brands talk to many creators and they know the range. An honest rate that you can explain is far more convincing than a large number with nothing behind it. And remember that rates are not only about money: some early collaborations are worth taking for the experience, the product or the relationship — but that is your choice to make, not the brand's to assume.

She is not the biggest creator they will see this week, but she is one of the clearest — and clarity is what gets a reply. That is the whole job of a media kit: to make the brand's decision easy.

Sending your kit to brands

A kit only works when it reaches the right person. Send it to brands whose products you actually use and whose customers look like your audience. A short message works best: who you are, what you make, why their product fits your audience, and your kit attached or pasted below. Keep it short — the kit does the detailed talking.

Do not send the same message to fifty brands. A brand can tell when a message was written for everyone, and it will be treated as if it was written for no one. Pick a small number of brands, write each message for that brand, and mention one specific thing about their product. Ten thoughtful messages will do more than a hundred copied ones.

Expect most brands not to reply. That is normal, not a verdict on your work. Wait a week, send one polite follow-up, and then move on. Keep your kit updated as your numbers grow, and send it again to the same brands in a few months with your new numbers. Many collaborations happen on the second or third try, not the first.

Common mistakes to avoid

The biggest mistake is inflating your numbers. Brands can see your public follower counts, and a kit that claims more than your profile shows will be ignored — or worse, remembered. Enter your real numbers, even if they feel small. A small honest audience is a real asset; a large invented one is a liability.

The second mistake is leaving out your audience. A kit with only follower counts tells a brand nothing about who those followers are. Age range and country take ten seconds to add and they change how a brand reads your whole kit. Never skip them.

The third mistake is hiding your rates, or having none at all. Brands do not enjoy guessing what you charge, and many will not ask. Put your rates on the page. The fourth mistake is length: a media kit is one page. Ten pages of screenshots and life story will not be read. One page, honest numbers, clear rates, and a way to reach you — that is the whole kit.

Questions people ask

What is a media kit?

A media kit is a one-page summary of you as a creator: your name and handle, your follower counts per platform, who your audience is, what you make, any past brand work, your rates and how to reach you. Brands read it to decide in about thirty seconds whether to work with you. This page builds that one page from the details you enter.

How many followers do I need?

There is no fixed number. Brands work with creators of all sizes, and many prefer smaller creators whose audience matches their customers closely. What matters is fit: a food brand wants people who cook, and an engaged audience of a few thousand can be worth more to them than a large audience that never engages.

Should I show my rates?

Yes. A kit without rates forces the brand to ask, and many will simply move on to the next creator. Listing your rates as plain numbers saves everyone time, and a published rate is a starting point for negotiation, not a final price. You can always discuss the details once a brand is interested.

What if my numbers are small?

Small honest numbers are fine — say so plainly and let your audience details do the work. A brand would rather see real numbers and a clear audience than a big number they cannot trust. Many creators get their first collaborations with a few thousand followers, because their audience was exactly right for the brand.

Do brands check my numbers?

Yes. Your follower counts are public, and brands check them before they reply. That is why this page asks you to enter your real numbers: a kit that claims more than your profile shows will be ignored. Enter what is true, keep your kit updated as you grow, and let the real numbers speak.

How long should a media kit be?

One page. A brand reads dozens of kits, and a long one will not be finished. One page with your name, your numbers, your audience, your content, your past work, your rates and your contact details is everything a brand needs. This builder makes exactly that — one clean page, nothing more.
