How to use the TikTok content planner

Start by picking your niche from the list at the top of the page. The planner has ideas for cooking, comedy, study, fitness, shop accounts and daily life, so choose the one that is closest to what you actually post about. If your videos mix two niches, pick the one you post about most often — the ideas are starting points, and you can bend any of them to fit your own style.

Next, choose how many days a week you want to post. Three days a week is a good starting point for most people: it keeps you visible without turning posting into a second job. If you are just starting out, two days a week is fine too. The planner spreads your posting days evenly across the month, and the days in between become prep days for planning, filming and editing.

Press the button and your 30-day calendar appears. Every posting day has one video idea and one hook line — the first words your viewer hears. Prep days are marked clearly, so you always know whether today is a filming day or a planning day. Use the copy button to take the whole plan with you, and come back to the hook generator whenever you have a topic of your own and need five ways to open it.

What makes a TikTok idea work

A good TikTok idea has one thing at its centre: a single clear promise in the first two seconds. The viewer decides almost at once whether to keep watching, so the hook does the heavy lifting. "Stop overcooking your rice" works because it names a problem the viewer already has and promises a fix. A vague opening like "here is a video about rice" asks the viewer to wait — and most of them will not.

The second ingredient is one idea per video. A video that tries to teach three recipes, tell a story and show a product ends up saying nothing clearly. Pick one idea, say it in the simplest way you can, and stop when it is said. Short videos are not shallow videos: a clip that teaches one thing well beats a long clip that wanders.

Finally, the best ideas come from your own life, not from a trend list. The meal you cook every Tuesday, the mistake you made last week, the question a friend asked you yesterday — these are real, specific and easy to film. Trends can give you a format, but your own experience gives you something to say. The planner's ideas are prompts, not scripts: the more of yourself you put into them, the better they work.

Finding ideas for your niche

Every niche has its own questions that people ask again and again, and those questions are your best source of ideas. A cooking audience asks how to cook faster, cheaper and better. A study audience asks how to remember things and stay focused. A shop audience asks what is new, how things are made and whether a product is worth it. Listen to the questions people already ask you — in comments, in messages, in real life — and turn each one into a video.

Your own habits are another rich source. Film the thing you do every day without thinking: the breakfast you make on autopilot, the warm-up you never skip, the way you organise your desk before you start work. What feels boring to you feels useful to someone who has never done it. The planner's ideas are built on this principle — ordinary moments, framed clearly.

When you are stuck, look backwards instead of forwards. Your older videos, your notes, your photo roll and your own learning journey all hold ideas you have forgotten. The mistake you made as a beginner is a video. The shortcut you found last month is a video. The thing you wish someone had told you when you started is often the best video of all, because someone else is starting right now.

The 30-day plan, explained

The plan runs for 30 days because a month is long enough to build a habit and short enough to finish. Each day is either a posting day or a prep day. Posting days carry one video idea and one hook line. Prep days carry no posting pressure at all — they are for planning the next video, filming ahead, editing, or simply resting. A plan with rest built in is a plan you can actually keep.

Your posting days are spread evenly through the month, so you never face a week with five videos and then a week with none. If you choose three days a week, you get twelve posting days and eighteen prep days. If you choose every day, there are no prep days at all — which is why daily posting is hard to sustain, and why the planner lets you choose a slower pace without guilt.

The ideas rotate through fifteen different prompts per niche, so the same idea never repeats twice in one month at three days a week, and the hook lines rotate through ten different openers. When you build a second month, pick a different niche or a different posting pace, and the plan will look different again. The calendar is a starting point, not a contract: swap any idea for your own, move days around, and skip a day when life gets in the way.

A worked example: a cooking account's first two weeks

Take a small cooking account that posts three days a week. In week one, the plan gives it a posting day with "Cook a meal with only 3 ingredients" and the hook "This 3-ingredient dinner takes 10 minutes". The creator films it on the prep day before, using ingredients already in the kitchen, and posts it on the scheduled day. The second posting day is "The 5-minute breakfast I make every day", and the third is "3 ways to use leftover rice". Three videos, three different hooks, all filmed from ordinary life.

In week two, the plan moves on to "How I store herbs so they last 2 weeks", "The knife skill every beginner should learn first" and "The cheapest meal I know how to make". Notice the pattern: each video teaches one thing, each hook promises one clear benefit, and none of them needed special shopping or a new skill. The creator spent the prep days filming two videos at once, so posting days were just a matter of uploading.

After two weeks the account has six videos, and the creator knows which ones people watched longest. That is the real point of the plan: not six perfect videos, but six attempts that teach you what your audience likes. The second half of the month can then lean into whatever worked — more quick breakfasts, more money-saving meals — while the plan keeps the ideas coming.

Keeping the plan going after day 30

Day 30 is not the end — it is the first checkpoint. Before you build a second plan, look at your month and ask three questions: which videos were easiest to make, which ones got the most comments, and which ones did you actually enjoy filming? The overlap of those three answers is your direction for the next month. A plan you enjoy is a plan you will keep.

Rebuild the calendar with a fresh choice. You can keep the same niche and change the posting pace, or keep the pace and try a second niche if your content has drifted. The hook generator is there for the ideas the calendar does not cover: type in any topic from your own life and get five openings for it. Over time you will need the planner less, because you will have trained yourself to spot ideas everywhere.

If you miss a week, do not try to catch up by posting everything at once. Just start again from today with a fresh 30-day plan. Posting in bursts after a long silence is harder on you and confusing for your audience. A steady, ordinary rhythm — three good videos a week, every week — beats a frantic month followed by nothing.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is copying a trend after it has already peaked. By the time a trend reaches everyone, the audience has seen it twenty times. If you use a trend, use it early and make it yours — your niche, your setting, your twist. A late copy of someone else's video is the fastest way to look like everyone else.

The second mistake is packing ten ideas into one video. A video about your morning routine, your breakfast recipe and your workout plan is three videos, not one. Split them up: each one gets its own hook, its own day and its own chance to be found. Your plan has room for all of them — that is what the thirty days are for.

The third mistake is skipping the hook. Many creators open with "Hi guys, welcome back to my channel" and lose the viewer before the video starts. Say the interesting thing first. You can introduce yourself later, or never — the viewer came for the idea, not the greeting. And finally, do not judge a video by its first hour. Some videos take days to find their audience. Post it, move on to the next idea, and let the plan do its quiet work.

Questions people ask

How many TikToks should I post a week?

Three to five a week is a realistic rhythm for most people, and two a week is fine when you are starting out. Consistency matters more than volume: three videos every week beats ten videos in one week and then nothing for a month. This planner lets you choose anything from one to seven days a week, so pick a pace you can actually keep.

What is a hook?

A hook is the first one or two seconds of your video — the words or image that make someone stop scrolling and keep watching. A good hook names a problem, promises a result, or says something surprising, like 'Stop overcooking your rice'. Every posting day in your plan comes with a hook line, and the hook generator can make five more for any topic you type in.

Can this guarantee views?

No. No planner, tool or trick can guarantee views, and anyone who promises that is not telling the truth. What this page does is remove the hardest part of posting — deciding what to film — so you can post regularly with a clear hook. Regular posting with good hooks gives your videos the best chance, but views always depend on the audience.

What if I run out of ideas?

Use the hook generator with a topic from your own life, look at the comments on your videos for questions people ask you, and re-read the plan for ideas you skipped the first time. Your own habits, mistakes and questions are an endless source: the meal you cook every week, the shortcut you found last month, the thing you wish you had known when you started.

Should I follow trends?

Trends can help, but only if you catch them early and adapt them to your niche. A trend used late, copied exactly as everyone else did it, adds nothing. The stronger habit is to build your videos around your own ideas and use a trend format — a sound, a layout, a challenge — as the wrapping, not the whole video.

How long should a TikTok be?

Most short videos that do well are under a minute, and many are 15 to 30 seconds long. The right length is the length your one idea needs: say it clearly, show it, and stop. A longer video is fine when the idea genuinely needs more time, but length alone never makes a video better — a tight 20 seconds beats a wandering 2 minutes.
