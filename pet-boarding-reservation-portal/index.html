Going away is hard enough without worrying about your pet, and a good boarding stay starts long before the suitcase comes out. This guide explains how boarding works, what to check before you book, what to pack, and how a short trial night can turn a worry into a routine.

This page does not take reservations. It explains how boarding works so you can book with a real boarding kennel or sitter yourself. Boarding rules, prices and what is included vary by place and by provider, so confirm everything with the provider before you book.

How to use this guide

This guide is for anyone leaving a pet in someone else's care for the first time, and for anyone whose last boarding stay did not go as well as hoped. It covers how boarding works, the checks that matter before you book, what to pack, the trial night, and what happens if your pet gets sick while you are away. Read it in order the first time, then use the checklists before each booking.

Be clear about one thing from the start: this page is a guide, not a booking service. It cannot reserve a kennel, hold a place, or take a payment. What it does is teach you how boarding works, so that when you contact a real boarding kennel or sitter, you know what to ask, what to look for, and what to pack. How boarding works

Boarding means leaving your pet in someone else's care while you are away, and there are three common kinds. A boarding kennel or cattery is a place built for the job, with its own kennels or cat rooms, staff, and routines. A pet sitter looks after your pet in the sitter's own home, usually with only a few pets at a time. Home boarding is a sitter who stays in your home, or visits it, while you are away. Each kind suits a different pet.

Booking usually starts with a call or a visit. The provider will ask about your pet — species, breed, age, temper, health, medicines, and how it gets on with other animals — and about your dates. Most providers ask for the vaccination record, and many will not take a pet without it. They will tell you what is included: food, walks, play, medication, and how often the pet is checked. Ask for this in writing, so there is no argument later about what was agreed.

On the day, you drop the pet off with its things, confirm the feeding and medicine instructions, and leave a contact number where you can actually be reached. The provider feeds, walks, plays with and checks the pet on the agreed routine, and contacts you if anything goes wrong. At pickup, ask how the stay went — a good provider will tell you honestly, including anything the pet found hard.

The checks that matter before you book

The checks before you book are the whole point of this guide, because once you have left, it is too late to check anything. Start with the licence and the paperwork: in many places a boarding kennel needs a licence, and a licensed kennel has been inspected. Ask to see it. Then ask about the staff: how many people look after how many pets, whether someone stays overnight, and what happens in an emergency.

Then visit, if you can. A visit tells you more in ten minutes than any website. Look for clean kennels or rooms, fresh water, dry bedding, and space to move. Look at the animals already there: do they look calm and cared for, or stressed and ignored? Smell the air — a well-run kennel smells of cleaning, not of neglect. Ask where your pet will sleep, eat, and exercise, and ask to see all three places.

  • Licence and insurance: is the provider licensed, and insured?
  • Vaccination rules: does the provider ask for the vaccination record?
  • Staffing: how many pets per person, and is anyone there overnight?
  • Vet cover: which vet is used, and how fast can a sick pet be seen?
  • Cleanliness: clean rooms, fresh water, dry bedding, space to move
  • Exercise and rest: how often is the pet walked, played with, rested?

What to pack for the stay

Pack for your pet the way you would pack for a child going to stay with a relative: the familiar things, the necessary things, and nothing you cannot afford to lose. The most important item is the pet's usual food, measured out per meal if you can, with the feeding instructions written down. A sudden change of food upsets a pet's stomach, and an upset stomach in a strange place is miserable for the pet and messy for everyone else.

Next comes medicine, if the pet takes any: the medicine itself, the dose, the times, and what it is for, all written down. Then the comfort items: the pet's own bed or blanket, a familiar toy, and something that smells of home. A familiar smell settles a pet faster than anything a stranger can do. Do not pack the pet's favourite toy if losing it would be a disaster — pack the second favourite.

Finally, pack the paperwork: the vaccination record, your vet's contact details, your own contact number, and the name and number of someone local who can decide for you if you cannot be reached. Put the feeding and medicine instructions on one sheet of paper, in large clear writing, and hand it over in person — do not rely on a message that may never be read.

The trial night: a short test first

A trial night is a short stay — one night, or even one day — before the real booking, and it is the single best thing you can do for a pet that has never boarded before. The pet meets the people, sees the place, sleeps there once, and comes home again. The next time, the place is not strange any more. The provider also learns about your pet: how it eats, how it sleeps, what worries it, and what calms it.

A trial night also tests the provider. You will see how the handover goes, how the provider talks about your pet, and whether the place looks the same on a second visit. If the pet comes home calm and the provider can tell you what the pet ate and how it slept, the trial has done its job. If the pet comes home distressed and the provider cannot say what happened, you have learned something important before the long stay, when it still costs you nothing to change your mind.

Book the trial night a few weeks before the real stay, not the night before. That leaves time to try a different provider if the trial goes badly, and time for the pet to forget any worry before the real booking. Some providers offer a free trial visit or a short day stay — ask. A provider who welcomes a trial is a provider with nothing to hide.

If your pet gets sick while boarding

Pets get sick on holiday too, and a good boarding provider has a plan for it. Before you book, ask three questions: which vet does the provider use, how fast can a sick pet be seen, and will the provider call you before or after taking the pet to the vet? The answers should be clear and immediate. A provider who hesitates, or has no vet arranged, is a provider to avoid.

Leave written permission for emergency vet care, with a limit on what the provider may agree to spend without reaching you, and leave your vet's contact details as well as the provider's. If your pet has a health problem, tell the provider in writing before the stay: what it is, what to watch for, and what to do. A provider who knows is a provider who can act fast.

When you collect your pet, ask how the stay went and watch the pet for a day or two after coming home. A pet that is tired after boarding is normal — new places are tiring. A pet that is still not eating, is vomiting, has diarrhoea, or seems in pain a day later should see your vet. Tell the vet the pet was boarding, so the vet knows where the pet has been.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is booking without visiting. A website can show the best kennel in the world; the real place may be nothing like it. Visit, look, smell, and ask questions. If the provider will not let you visit, do not book — a provider with nothing to hide welcomes visitors.

The second mistake is arriving without the vaccination record. Most providers need it, and without it the booking may be refused on the day — a wasted trip, and a pet with nowhere to go while you are away. Check the record weeks before the stay, not the night before, so there is time to update a vaccination if one has lapsed.

The third mistake is a long, emotional goodbye. Pets read your feelings, and a long farewell tells the pet that something is wrong. Keep the handover short and calm: hand over the pet, hand over the instructions, say a quick goodbye, and go. The pet will settle faster without an audience, and you will feel better too.

Questions people ask

How do I book pet boarding?

Start by finding a provider — a boarding kennel, a sitter, or a home boarder — then call or visit with your pet's details: species, age, temper, health and dates. The provider will ask for the vaccination record and tell you what is included. Visit before you book, and ask for the agreement in writing. This page does not take reservations.

What should I check before booking?

Check the licence and insurance, the vaccination rules, how many pets each person looks after, whether anyone stays overnight, and which vet is used in an emergency. Visit the place: look for clean rooms, fresh water, dry bedding and calm animals. Ask where your pet will sleep, eat and exercise, and ask to see all three.

What should I pack?

Pack the pet's usual food measured out per meal, with the feeding instructions written down, plus any medicine with its dose and times. Add the pet's own bed or blanket and a familiar toy, the vaccination record, your vet's details, and a contact number where you can be reached. Put the instructions on one sheet of paper in large clear writing.

How much does boarding cost?

It depends on the provider, the place, the pet and the length of the stay. A kennel stay, a sitter's home and a home visit are priced differently, and extras like extra walks, play sessions or medicine may add to the cost. Ask what the price includes before you book, and compare like with like.

Should I try a trial night?

Yes — a trial night is the best thing you can do for a pet that has never boarded. The pet meets the people, sees the place and sleeps there once, so the real stay is not strange any more. The provider also learns how your pet eats and sleeps. Book the trial a few weeks before the real stay, so there is time to change provider if it goes badly.

What if my pet gets sick while boarding?

A good provider has a plan: a vet arranged, a fast route to care, and a promise to call you. Before you book, ask which vet is used and whether you will be called before or after the pet is taken in. Leave written permission for emergency care and your vet's details. If your pet is still unwell a day after coming home, see your vet.
