Why these formats spread

Every month, a few image formats take over the feeds. Someone posts a picture made with a clever prompt, everyone asks how it was done, the prompt gets shared, and suddenly the same style is everywhere. November 2026 has six of them, and they all share one thing: they turn an ordinary photo into something that feels personal, funny, or surprising.

It is no accident that images lead the way. People use ChatGPT most for images — about 17.6% of conversations are about pictures, ahead of information and entertainment at 14.4% and writing at 12.7%. ChatGPT is also the most-searched term in the US, with about 93.8 million US searches a month and about 124 million worldwide. When that many people are making pictures, a good format travels fast.

The formats below all work with Gemini and ChatGPT, and each one comes with its full prompt in the picker above. Try them as written first, then change the details to make them yours.

Hugging your younger self (Polaroid)

The most-shared format of the month is also the most emotional: a Polaroid photo of you as an adult, gently hugging yourself as a child. It works because it is a picture nobody could ever take in real life — two versions of the same person, in one frame, in the faded colours of an old instant photo.

To make it, upload a clear photo of yourself as an adult and a childhood photo if you have one, then use the prompt in the picker: a heartwarming Polaroid of you as an adult hugging your younger self, both smiling, vintage faded colours, soft flash, white Polaroid border. The Polaroid style does a lot of the work — the faded colours and soft flash hide small mismatches and make the picture feel like a real memory.

If the faces come out wrong, try again with a simpler prompt and a clearer photo. Faces are the hardest part of any AI picture, and a sharp, front-facing photo gives the tool the best chance. And keep the originals safe — the AI picture is a keepsake, not a replacement for the real photos.

Desk collectible figurine with its box

The second format turns you into a collectible toy: a small figurine standing on a desk next to its own box, like a product photo for a limited-edition figure. It is playful, it is flattering, and it spreads because everyone wants to see themselves as a tiny version of themselves.

The prompt in the picker describes a collectible desk figurine of you in a smart outfit, standing next to its toy box on a wooden desk, shot like a studio product photo. The box is the detail that sells it — without the box it is just a small statue, with the box it is a product. Add one personal detail to make it yours: the outfit you actually wear, a book or a coffee cup on the desk, your favourite colour on the box.

This format is forgiving, because a figurine is allowed to look a little stylised. If the face is not quite you, it still reads as a charming toy version of you, which is the whole point.

90s yearbook portrait

The 90s yearbook portrait takes you back to school photo day: feathered hair, a soft studio backdrop, warm film colours, and a gentle smile. It works because almost everyone has a yearbook photo, and almost everyone laughs at it — so an AI version feels like a shared joke and a warm memory at the same time.

Upload a clear front-facing photo and use the yearbook prompt from the picker. The key words are the ones that set the era: feathered hair, soft studio backdrop, warm film colours. Without them the tool may give you a modern portrait, which misses the point entirely.

If your hair or outfit comes out too modern, name the decade plainly in the prompt and try again. The tool needs the era spelled out — it cannot guess that you want the nineties from a photo taken today.

Neon rain cyberpunk street

Not every trending format is about faces. The neon rain cyberpunk street is pure atmosphere: you standing under a glowing umbrella on a wet street, pink and blue neon signs reflecting in the pavement, night all around. It is the most cinematic format of the month, and the one that looks best as a wallpaper.

The prompt in the picker sets the scene in layers: the person, the umbrella, the wet pavement, the neon signs, the night mood. Each layer adds something, and leaving one out changes the picture more than you would expect — without the rain the street looks dry and flat, without the neon it looks like an ordinary night.

This is a good format for trying a new style, because the mood carries the picture even when the face is small or partly hidden. If portraits frustrate you, start here: a full scene is more forgiving than a close-up.

Hand-painted anime countryside and product splash shots

The hand-painted anime countryside is the calmest format of the month: rolling green hills, a small cottage, cherry blossom trees, soft evening light. It needs no photo of you at all, which makes it the easiest format to try — type the prompt, and a peaceful painted landscape comes back.

Product splash shots are the opposite: sharp, bright and commercial. A perfume bottle with water droplets frozen mid-air, bright studio light, a clean white background. They spread because they look expensive — like an advert — and anyone can make one from a sentence. Small sellers use them for shop photos, and everyone else uses them because they are fun to make.

Both formats reward the same habit: name the light and the mood, not just the subject. Evening light makes the anime hills glow; bright studio light makes the splash shot crisp. Change the light and you change the picture.

How to remix a format — and what to do when it stops working

Copying a format exactly is how you learn it. Remixing it is how you make it yours. Take the younger-self Polaroid and change the setting — a garden instead of a plain background. Take the figurine and change the outfit, the desk, or what is on the box. Take the cyberpunk street and change the weather. One changed detail is a remix; five changed details is a new format.

Never remix with someone else's photo. The formats work with your own pictures, and using another person's photo without asking is not a remix — it is a breach of trust, and it can embarrass or harm them. The same goes for posting: your face, your photo, your choice.

Formats also fade. A prompt that works brilliantly this month may stop working as the tools change, or as everyone copies it so much that the feeds tire of it. When a format stops working, do not force it — take the parts you liked, the style words and the structure, and build something new with them. The skill you learned from the format outlasts the format itself.

Questions people ask

What is the younger-self Polaroid prompt?

It is a prompt that asks the image tool to draw you as an adult gently hugging yourself as a child, in the style of a vintage Polaroid photo with faded colours, soft flash and a white border. Pick the format in the picker above to see the full prompt and copy it.

How do I make a desk figurine picture?

Upload a clear photo of yourself, then use the figurine prompt from the picker: a collectible desk figurine of you in a smart outfit, standing next to its toy box on a wooden desk, shot like a studio product photo. Add one personal detail, such as your usual outfit or favourite colour, to make it yours.

What is the 90s yearbook prompt?

It is a prompt for a 90s high school yearbook portrait: feathered hair, a soft studio backdrop, warm film colours and a gentle smile. Use a clear front-facing photo and name the decade plainly in the prompt, or the tool may give you a modern portrait instead.

Do these prompts work in Gemini too?

Yes, all six formats work with Gemini and ChatGPT, because both tools read plain descriptions the same way. The pictures will look a little different in each tool, so if one format disappoints you in one tool, try the same prompt in the other.

Why do some viral prompts not work for me?

Viral prompts are usually shared after many tries, so the posted picture may be the best of a dozen attempts. Your photo, your wording and the tool's current version all change the result, so try the prompt a few times, change one word at a time, and start from a clear, well-lit photo.

Can I post AI pictures of other people?

No, not without asking them first. An AI picture of another person is still a picture of them, and posting it without permission can embarrass or harm them. Use your own photos, or photos you have clear permission to edit and share.
