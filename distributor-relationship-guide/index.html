Distributor Relationship Guide

A distributor buys your product and sells it on to shops, businesses or customers in their area, acting as the bridge between you and the market. This guide explains how that relationship works, what a fair agreement covers, and how to keep it strong over time.

This page gives general business information only. It is not legal advice. Distribution agreements are legal documents with real consequences, so have any agreement checked by a qualified professional before you sign it. This page lists no distributors and arranges no deals.

What this is and who needs it

A distributor is a business that buys products from a maker and sells them on to retailers, other businesses or end customers in a particular area. The distributor holds stock, handles local sales, and often looks after delivery and after-sales support. In return, they buy at a price that leaves them a margin — the difference between what they pay you and what they charge their customers. For a small maker, a good distributor can open a whole region that would be too costly to reach alone.

This guide is for makers and small brands thinking about working with distributors, and for businesses that already have distributors and want the relationship to work better. It is also useful for anyone who has been offered a distribution deal and wants to understand what is being asked before signing anything. The ideas here apply to physical products of most kinds, from food and household goods to tools and clothing.

Distributors differ from other partners in one important way: they take ownership of the stock. Unlike an agent, who sells on your behalf and takes a commission, a distributor buys the goods and takes the risk of selling them. That changes the relationship. The distributor has their own money tied up in your product, so their success and yours are linked — but they also have their own priorities, their own customers, and often other brands competing for their attention.

A distribution relationship is a partnership, not a sale. The first order is only the beginning. What decides whether it works is what happens after: how orders are handled, how problems are solved, how prices are set, and how both sides communicate. The makers who do best with distributors treat them as long-term partners and invest time in the relationship, rather than treating each order as a one-off deal.

How it works, step by step

Most distribution relationships start with a conversation, not a contract. A distributor may approach you, or you may approach them, and the early talks cover the basics: what you make, where they sell, how much they could move, and what price they would need. If both sides are interested, the next step is usually a trial: a small first order, so each side can test the other before committing to more. A trial protects everyone and is a normal, sensible way to begin.

If the trial goes well, the relationship is put into a written agreement. This is the document that sets the terms: which area the distributor covers, whether they are the only distributor in that area, how prices are set, how orders are placed and paid for, and how long the agreement lasts. An agreement protects both sides, because it turns vague promises into clear terms that either side can point to later. Never rely on a handshake for a relationship that involves stock, money and your brand name.

  1. Talk first: share what you make, your prices, and what you expect; listen to what the distributor needs.
  2. Start with a trial order so both sides can test the working relationship before committing.
  3. Put the terms in a written agreement and have it checked by a qualified professional.
  4. Set up the practical details: how orders are placed, how delivery works, and how payment is made.
  5. Agree how you will stay in touch: regular check-ins, sales updates, and a clear way to raise problems.
  6. Review the relationship on a set schedule — for example every few months — and adjust the terms if both sides agree.

Once the relationship is running, the day-to-day work is mostly about orders and communication. The distributor places orders, you fulfil them on time, and both sides keep each other informed. The distributor tells you what is selling, what customers are asking for, and what competitors are doing. You tell the distributor about new products, price changes and stock levels. This flow of information is one of the most valuable parts of the relationship, and it only works when both sides make time for it.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Working with a distributor costs the maker a share of the price. The distributor buys below your retail price and sells at a price that covers their costs and leaves a profit. How large that gap is varies by product and by market, and it is one of the main things to agree before you start. A margin that is too small leaves the distributor with no reason to push your product; a margin that is too large leaves you with too little to run your business. The right balance is one where both sides can make a fair living from the product.

There are also practical costs on your side: the time to manage the relationship, the cost of shipping stock to the distributor, and the cost of any support you provide, such as product training, printed materials or samples. These costs are real and should be counted when you work out whether distribution is worth it. A distributor who sells a lot but needs constant support may cost more than one who sells a little and needs none.

Before you start, you need a few things in place. You need a product that is ready to sell: consistent quality, clear packaging, and any safety or labelling requirements for the market already met. You need to know your costs and your lowest workable price, so you can negotiate from facts rather than guesses. And you need a simple way to track orders, stock and payments, because a distribution relationship generates paperwork from the first order onward.

Time matters too. Finding the right distributor takes time, the trial takes time, and building real sales takes longer than most makers expect. A distributor with a good network may still need months to place your product with their customers. Plan for a slow start, and judge the relationship over a fair period rather than on the first few weeks.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is choosing a distributor for the wrong reason — usually because they are the first to show interest, or because they promise the biggest orders. Big promises are easy to make and hard to keep. A distributor who sells a little, reliably, and treats your product well is worth more than one who promises the world and delivers nothing. Check what the distributor already sells, who their customers are, and whether your product fits their range. A good fit matters more than a big promise.

The second mistake is signing an agreement without understanding it. Distribution agreements cover territory, exclusivity, pricing, payment terms, minimum orders and how the agreement can be ended. Each of these can cause real harm if it is wrong: an exclusive territory given away too cheaply, a minimum order that is never met, or an ending clause that leaves you stuck. Read every clause, ask what each one means in practice, and have the agreement checked by a qualified professional before you sign.

The third mistake is going quiet after the first order. Some makers treat distribution as a tap they turn on and then forget. Distributors need support: product information, answers to questions, news about new lines, and a real person to talk to when something goes wrong. A distributor who feels ignored will quietly give their attention to a brand that stays in touch. Regular, short check-ins do more for the relationship than occasional grand gestures.

  • Never grant exclusivity over a large area to a distributor you have not tested with a trial first.
  • Never sign an agreement you have not read in full and had checked by a professional.
  • Never set prices without knowing your own costs and your lowest workable price.
  • Never promise stock you cannot deliver — a missed delivery damages trust faster than a slow start.
  • Never let communication go quiet for months; a short regular check-in keeps the relationship alive.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist when starting a new distribution relationship, and again when reviewing an existing one. It covers the steps that matter most, in the order they usually happen.

  • Check the distributor's background: what they sell now, who their customers are, and how long they have been trading.
  • Ask how they would sell your product and what support they expect from you.
  • Agree the territory in plain words: which area, and whether they are the only distributor there.
  • Agree pricing and payment terms, including when payment is due and how it is made.
  • Agree minimum orders if any, and what happens if they are not met.
  • Agree how orders are placed, how delivery works, and who pays for shipping.
  • Put everything in a written agreement and have it checked by a qualified professional.
  • Start with a trial order before committing to a long agreement.
  • Set a regular check-in schedule and keep a simple record of orders, stock and payments.
  • Agree how either side can end the agreement, and what happens to unsold stock if it ends.
  • Review the relationship on a set schedule and adjust the terms if both sides agree.

If a distributor resists any step on this list — especially the written agreement or the trial — treat that as information. A serious distributor expects these steps and welcomes them, because they protect both sides. Resistance usually means the distributor wants the benefits of the relationship without the commitments that make it work.

Tips from real practice

Treat your distributor's customers as your customers too. When a distributor reports a complaint or a request, take it seriously and respond quickly. The distributor is your eyes and ears in their market, and the feedback they bring is often the most useful market research you will ever get. Makers who listen to that feedback improve their products faster than makers who ignore it.

Share your plans early. If a new product is coming, if a price will change, or if stock will be short for a while, tell your distributors before it happens. Surprises damage trust, and a distributor who hears about a change from a customer will not forget it. Early news lets them plan, and planning keeps the relationship smooth.

Visit when you can. A short visit to a distributor's warehouse or shop teaches you more about your product's place in the market than months of messages. You will see how the product is displayed, hear what shop staff say about it, and meet the people who sell it every day. If a visit is not possible, a video call with the sales team is the next best thing.

Finally, keep the relationship honest on both sides. If sales are slow, say so and work out why together. If the distributor is not performing, raise it early and plainly rather than letting it grow into a grievance. Most distribution problems are fixable when they are raised early and discussed openly. The relationships that fail are usually the ones where problems were left to fester in silence.

Questions people ask

What does a distributor do?

A distributor buys products from a maker and sells them on to shops, businesses or customers in a particular area. They hold stock, handle local sales, and often look after delivery and after-sales support. In return they buy at a price that leaves them a margin on each sale.

How is a distributor different from a wholesaler?

The two are similar and the words are sometimes used loosely, but a distributor usually has a closer, longer-term relationship with the maker, often covering a set territory and promoting the brand. A wholesaler more often buys and resells with less brand involvement. What matters most is the actual agreement, not the title.

What should a distribution agreement cover?

It should cover the territory, whether the distributor is exclusive there, pricing and payment terms, minimum orders, how orders and delivery work, how long the agreement lasts, and how either side can end it. Have the agreement checked by a qualified professional before you sign it.

How are distributor prices set?

The maker sets a wholesale price that leaves the distributor enough margin to cover their costs and make a profit, while leaving the maker enough to run the business. The right price is one both sides can live with. It should be agreed in writing before the first order.

How do I support my distributors?

Stay in touch with regular check-ins, answer questions quickly, share news about new products and price changes early, and provide the product information and materials they need to sell well. Treat their feedback about customers as valuable market information.

What if a distributor stops selling my product?

Raise it early and plainly: ask what has changed and whether the problem can be fixed. Check what the agreement says about minimum orders and ending the relationship. If the relationship cannot be repaired, follow the ending terms in the agreement and plan how unsold stock will be handled.
