This checklist helps you plan an accessible event — it is not a legal certificate and it does not replace advice from an access professional. Rules and standards differ by place, so check the local requirements for your venue and ask your guests what they need.

How to use the checklist

Accessibility fails in the details. Nobody plans an event that locks people out — it happens because one small thing was forgotten: the ramp was at the back door, the hearing loop was never switched on, the joining instructions came only as a scanned image. This checklist exists so that nothing important is left to memory.

Work through the five areas in order: venue, transport, hearing, sight, and information. Tick each item off as you confirm it, not as you hope for it — an item counts as done only when someone has checked it in person. Your ticks are saved in this browser only, so you can close the page and come back later without losing your place. Nothing is uploaded anywhere, and nobody else can see your list.

Use the checklist early, not the week before the event. Some items take time to arrange — booking a captioner, printing large-print programmes, fixing a step-free route — and the earlier you start, the cheaper every fix becomes. Share the finished list with your venue and your team, so everyone is working from the same page. And when the event is over, keep the list: it is the starting point for the next one.

The venue

The venue is where most access problems start, and most of them are visible on a single careful walk-through. Arrive as a guest would arrive: from the street, through the entrance, to the main room, to the toilet, and back out again. Every step, every narrow doorway, and every heavy door is a problem for someone, and you will only find them by walking the route yourself.

A step-free entrance is the first test, and it must be the entrance everyone uses — a ramp at the back door, past the bins, is not access, it is an afterthought. Inside, the aisles must be wide enough for a wheelchair to pass without moving chairs, and cables must never cross a walkway. Check the toilets on the same floor as the event: an accessible toilet two floors away is no help to anyone.

Think about rest as well as movement. Guests who tire easily need chairs with backs and armrests near the front, and a quiet room where they can sit down away from the noise. Clear signs matter more than you think — a guest who cannot find the room cannot enjoy the event. Walk the route with fresh eyes, or better still with someone who uses a wheelchair, and fix what you find before the invitations go out.

Transport

An event nobody can reach is not an event. Transport is the part organisers forget most often, because they know how they get there and assume everyone else will manage. Say plainly how to reach the venue: which bus or train stop is nearest, whether the route from the stop is step-free, and where the drop-off point is. Check the pavement route yourself — a dropped kerb on the map is not always a dropped kerb on the street.

Keep accessible parking spaces marked and free, and say how many there are in the joining instructions, so guests can plan. If you run a shuttle, make sure it can carry a wheelchair, and say so. Share taxi details before the event, not on the day, when a guest is already standing in the rain. And name one person as the transport contact on the day, with a phone number that is answered — a guest who is lost needs a human, not a web page.

Transport planning is also about timing. Guests who rely on accessible transport often need longer journeys, so start times matter: a nine o'clock start can be impossible for someone whose accessible bus runs once an hour. Where you can, choose a start time that gives everyone a fair chance of arriving on time.

Hearing and sight

Hearing and sight needs are easy to meet when you plan for them, and impossible to fix on the day when you do not. The most common failure is the simplest: a hearing loop that was fitted years ago and never tested. Test it before the first session, with someone who uses it, and test it again after any change to the sound system. A loop that hums or cuts out is worse than no loop at all, because guests trust it and then hear nothing.

Speakers must use the microphone at all times, even in a small room, even when they think their voice carries. A guest who lip-reads needs to see the speaker's face, so keep the stage lit and ask speakers not to turn away to read their slides. Live captions help far more people than you expect — not only guests who are deaf, but guests whose first language is not yours, and guests who simply miss a word. Book the captioner early; good ones are busy.

For sight, the basics are large print, high contrast, and good light. Offer large-print programmes without being asked, and make sure signs use large lettering with strong contrast against their background. Check slides for readable font sizes — if you cannot read a slide from the back row, neither can your guests. Welcome guide dogs, offer water for them, and brief staff to offer an arm to a guest who asks, rather than grabbing one. Small courtesies, done well, are what guests remember.

Information

Information is the quietest access barrier, and the easiest to remove. Joining instructions sent only as a scanned image cannot be read by a screen reader, and a PDF with no real text is a locked door. Send instructions in plain text as well as PDF, and check that your event web page works with a screen reader before you publish it. If you have never tested it, test it now — the fixes are usually small.

Ask for access needs at registration, and ask early enough to act on the answers. A single open question — is there anything we can do to help you take part — covers more ground than a long list of tick boxes, because guests know their own needs better than any form does. Name an access contact on every invitation, and make sure that person answers. A guest who writes in and hears nothing will simply not come.

On the day, explain the emergency exits in words at the start, not just on a slide — a guest who cannot see the slide still needs to know the way out. Offer the feedback form in more than one format, so the guests who had the hardest time getting there can still tell you about it. Information given well is access given well.

The standards in plain words

Access standards exist in most countries, and they differ from place to place — there is no single worldwide rulebook for events. What the standards share is a simple idea: a venue open to the public must be usable by the public, all of it, not just the able-bodied part. That idea covers step-free access, accessible toilets, hearing support, clear signs, and information in usable formats. The details — measurements, counts, deadlines — are local, and this page does not pretend to list them.

Your job as an organiser is not to memorise the law. It is to ask the venue what standard it meets, to check the answer with your own eyes, and to fill the gaps yourself where the venue falls short. A venue that meets the letter of the standard can still fail your guests in practice — a ramp that meets the slope rule but ends at a locked door is a good example. Walk the route, test the equipment, and ask your guests what they need. The standard is a floor, not a ceiling.

If your event is large or public, consider asking an access professional to review your plan. One hour of expert advice before the event is cheaper than one complaint afterwards, and it often catches the two or three things you would never have thought of. Keep a short written record of what you checked and what you fixed — it protects your guests, and it protects you.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is the ramp at the back door. Step-free access that routes guests past the bins and through the kitchen is not access, and guests notice. The main entrance must work for everyone, or it works for no one.

The second is the hearing loop that was never tested. A loop fitted and forgotten will fail exactly when it matters. Test it before the event, with a guest who uses it, and test it again after any change to the sound.

The third is forgetting the information. Joining instructions as a scanned image, a programme only in small print, a feedback form only online — each one locks someone out, and each one costs nothing to fix.

The fourth is planning access at the last minute. Captioners, large-print runs, and step-free routes all need time. Start with this checklist when you book the venue, not the week before the event.

The fifth is never asking the guests. You cannot guess what people need, and you do not have to. Ask at registration, answer what comes back, and keep asking every year.

Questions people ask

What is event accessibility?

Event accessibility means planning an event so that everyone can join in: step-free access, hearing and sight support, clear information, and transport that works. It is not a special extra — it is part of running a good event.

What should I check at the venue?

Check the entrance, the route to the main room, the toilets, the seating, and the signs — all on the same floor as the event, all checked in person. A ramp at the back door and a toilet two floors away do not count as access.

How do I help hearing-impaired guests?

Fit and test a hearing loop, make every speaker use the microphone, and book live captions for the main sessions. Keep background music low during breaks, and offer written copies of key talks on request.

What about transport?

Say plainly how to reach the venue, which stop is nearest, and whether the route is step-free. Keep accessible parking free, share taxi details before the event, and name one person guests can call for help on the day.

What information should be accessible?

Joining instructions, programmes, signs, slides, and feedback forms all need to be usable: plain text as well as PDF, large print on request, and web pages that work with screen readers. Information given well is access given well.

Where do I start?

Start with this checklist when you book the venue, and walk the route as a guest would. Ask for access needs at registration, test the equipment before the day, and keep the finished list for next year.
