AI Video Generator for Long Videos

By the Funtasha team · Published 20 November 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026

Most free AI video generators make clips of 5 to 15 seconds, so a long video is never made in one click. The real workflow is a script split into scenes, one short clip per scene, stitched together with narration in CapCut — and the planner on this page builds that scene list for you.

This page builds a plan, not a video — the clips themselves are made in a video tool such as Flow or Kling, and stitched in CapCut. Free tiers cap clip length and daily credits, so plan around the limits, not against them.

Why no free tool makes a 10-minute video in one click

Every free AI video generator today makes short clips: most cap out at 5 to 15 seconds per generation. That is not a flaw in one tool — it is how the technology works right now. Each second of video costs real computing power, and free tiers hand out just enough of it for short bursts: Flow's 50 free daily credits buy five 8-second clips, PixVerse's starter credits cover a handful of short generations, and Runway's one-time trial is 125 credits.

So when a website promises a 10-minute AI video in one click, read it carefully. What it usually means is a slideshow with a voiceover, or a tool that stitches short clips together for you — which is a real workflow, but not one-click generation. The honest version of that workflow is exactly what this page teaches: plan the scenes, generate the clips, stitch them together. It takes longer than one click, and the result is far better.

There is a second reason long videos resist one-click generation: stories need decisions. A 10-minute video has a beginning, a middle and an end, a change of pace, a point. No generator knows your point. Splitting a video into scenes is not a workaround for a weak tool — it is how every video, AI or not, has always been made.

The stitch workflow, step by step

Long videos are made by stitching clips plus narration. Here is the whole workflow, end to end, using only free tools.

  1. Write the script first — short sentences, one idea per line. A 60-second video is roughly 130 to 150 words of narration, split into scenes.
  2. Split the script into scenes, one idea per scene. Six scenes make a 60-second video; each scene becomes one generated clip.
  3. Write one prompt per scene: the visual, the camera move, the light. The planner on this page does this for you.
  4. Generate the clips in your video tool — Flow, Kling, PixVerse or Runway — a few a day on free credits.
  5. Record the narration in your own voice, or use a free text-to-speech option, one line per scene.
  6. Stitch everything in CapCut: clips in order, narration underneath, captions on top, 9:16 for vertical feeds.

Nothing in that list costs money, and nothing in it requires a camera. What it requires is the plan — which is why the planner sits at the top of this page instead of at the bottom.

Script structure: hook, three beats, close

Every video that holds attention follows the same skeleton: a hook, a few beats, and a close. The hook is the first two seconds — the one line or image that makes a viewer stay. The beats are the middle: one idea each, in an order that builds. The close is the ending: the takeaway, the question, or the next step.

For a 60-second video, that skeleton is six scenes: scene 1 is the hook, scenes 2 to 5 are the beats, scene 6 is the close. Each scene gets one sentence of narration and one visual. If a scene needs two sentences, it is two scenes — split it, and the video breathes better.

Write the hook last and put it first. Draft the beats, find the strongest idea, then turn that idea into the opening line. Hooks written first tend to promise things the video never delivers; hooks written last promise exactly what follows.

Narration options: your voice, free TTS, or typed captions

A stitched video needs a voice to hold it together, and you have three honest options. Your own voice is the best one: it costs nothing, it sounds human because it is, and viewers trust it. Record on your phone in a quiet room, one line per scene, and do not worry about perfection — a natural voice with a pause beats a polished voice that sounds like a machine.

Free text-to-speech is the second option, and it suits faceless channels and anyone who dislikes their recorded voice. Keep sentences short and write one idea per line — long sentences trip up synthetic voices and human ones alike. Listen to the result before you publish: if a line sounds wrong spoken aloud, rewrite it, because the voice is only reading what you wrote.

Typed captions are the third option, and they work alone for feeds where most people watch with the sound off. Short caption lines, one idea per screen, timed to the scene changes. Many creators use captions plus narration together — the voice for listeners, the text for scrollers — and CapCut adds both for free.

Assembly in CapCut (free)

CapCut is where the stitched video becomes a video. It is free, it runs in the browser and on your phone, and it covers every step of assembly: trimming clips, ordering scenes, adding narration, captions, music, and the 9:16 vertical frame that TikTok, Reels and Shorts expect.

Work in order. Drop the clips in first and trim each one to its scene — cut the wobbly first second of a generated clip, keep the clean middle. Then lay the narration underneath, one line per scene, and nudge the clips so each line lands on its picture. Captions come next: CapCut generates them from the narration, and you fix the words it mishears. Music and effects go last, quiet enough that the voice stays in front.

Export in 9:16 for vertical feeds, and keep a copy of the project. The next video in a series reuses the same structure — same hook shape, same caption style, same ending — so the second video takes half the time of the first.

Worked example: a 60-second video planned as 6 clips

Here is the whole workflow on one topic — how to water houseplants — planned as six clips of about 10 seconds each. Use it as a template: swap the topic, keep the shape.

  • Scene 1, hook: a drooping plant in sunlight, slow push-in. Narration: Your plant is not dying — you are watering it wrong.
  • Scene 2: hands lifting a pot, checking the soil with a finger. Narration: First, check the soil. If the top inch is dry, it is time to water.
  • Scene 3: water pouring slowly at the base of the plant. Narration: Water the soil, not the leaves — slow, until it drains out the bottom.
  • Scene 4: the same plant a week later, upright and green. Narration: Wait until the soil dries again before the next drink.
  • Scene 5: a row of plants on a windowsill, morning light. Narration: Most houseplants want a drink once a week — no more.
  • Scene 6, close: hands holding a small watering can. Narration: Dry soil, slow water, wait a week. That is the whole secret.

Six prompts, six clips, six narration lines — and a video a stranger would happily watch to the end. The planner at the top of the page builds the same shape for any topic you type in.

Questions people ask

Can AI make a 10-minute video in one click?

No. Free AI video generators make clips of 5 to 15 seconds each, so a long video is always built by stitching clips together with narration. The workflow on this page — script, scenes, clips, stitch — is how long AI videos are really made.

How many clips do I need for a 60-second video?

About six 10-second clips, or eight 8-second clips from Flow. Plan one scene per clip, with one narration line each, and the video will hold together without feeling rushed.

Which free tool is best for long videos?

Flow's 50 free daily credits cover five 8-second clips a day, which is the steadiest free supply. Kling and PixVerse add more clips on their free tiers, and CapCut stitches everything together for free.

Do I need to show my face?

No. Faceless videos work well for facts, stories and tutorials — narration plus AI visuals is enough. The planner builds scenes that work with or without a presenter on screen.

What happened to Sora?

Sora is gone. OpenAI announced the shutdown on 24 March 2026, the Sora app and site shut down on 26 April 2026, and the API followed on 24 September 2026. No successor was named, so long videos now depend on the stitch workflow on this page.

Does the planner on this page make the video for me?

No. The planner builds the scene-by-scene plan — the visuals, the prompts and the narration lines. You paste the prompts into a video tool, generate the clips, and stitch them in CapCut.
