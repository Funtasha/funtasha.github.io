The 10 questions worth asking

Good survey questions ask about something specific the listener can remember. The ten questions in the builder are written that way. The first asks what made the listener press play, which tells you what your titles and topics promise. The second asks which part held their attention, which tells you what to do more of. The third asks about the moment they almost stopped listening, which is the most useful question of all, because it points at the exact place the episode lost them.

The rest cover what the listener took away, how long episodes should be, which topics deserve more time and which deserve less, how the listener would describe the show to a friend, and what would make them recommend it. The last question is an open one, because the most useful feedback is often something you did not think to ask about. You do not need all ten every time. Five good questions, answered by twenty listeners, teach you more than thirty questions answered by nobody.

  • What made you press play on this episode?
  • Which part held your attention most?
  • Was there a moment you almost stopped listening?
  • What did you learn or take away?
  • How long should our episodes be?
  • Which topics do you want more of, and which less of?
  • How would you describe this show to a friend?
  • What would make you recommend this show?

Asking without leading the listener

A leading question pushes the listener towards the answer you want to hear, and the answer it gets is worthless. Asking did you love this episode is not a question, it is a request for praise, and most listeners will give it rather than be rude. Asking what did you think of this episode leaves the listener free to say what they really thought, including the parts they did not like. The honest answer is the one that helps you make a better show.

Three habits keep your questions honest. First, ask about what happened, not about how good it was: which part did you skip is better than was it great. Second, offer no answer in the question: avoid words like amazing, boring or too long inside the question itself. Third, make it safe to be critical by saying so at the top of the survey, in plain words: honest answers help the show, and no answer will offend anyone. Listeners who believe that will tell you the truth.

  • Ask what happened, not how good it was.
  • Keep praise and blame out of the question itself.
  • Say at the top that honest answers help and will not offend.
  • Ask one thing per question, never two.

Reading the answers honestly

When the answers come back, read them all before you decide what they mean. The first few answers are often the most enthusiastic, because the keenest listeners reply first, and the quieter ones take longer. If you stop after five answers, you will hear mostly praise. Wait until the replies slow down, then read them in one sitting and look for patterns: the same point made by three different listeners is a finding, and a single strong opinion is only one person's view.

Count what can be counted, and read what cannot. If you asked about episode length, count how many want shorter, longer or the same. If you asked open questions, group the answers by theme: sound quality, topics, pacing, guests, length. Write down the three themes that come up most, and one quote for each, so you can act on them later. Ignore the answers that praise everything and the answers that attack everything, and pay attention to the middle: the listeners who liked the show and still found something to fix.

  • Read every answer before deciding what they mean.
  • Wait for the replies to slow down; early answers skew positive.
  • Count the countable answers, and group the open ones by theme.
  • Three listeners making the same point is a finding, not a coincidence.

What to do with what you hear

Feedback only helps if it changes something, so pick one change from each survey and make it. If listeners say the introductions are too long, shorten the next one and see what happens. If they want more of a topic, plan two episodes on it. One change at a time matters, because if you change five things at once, you will never know which one worked. Tell the listeners what you changed and why, in the next episode, so they know their answers were read.

Not every suggestion should be followed. A suggestion that fits your show and your listeners is worth trying; a suggestion that would turn your show into a different show is not. The test is simple: would this change make the show better for the listeners it already has? If the answer is yes, try it for a few episodes and ask again. If the answer is no, thank the listener and carry on. Your show does not have to please everyone, but it should listen to everyone.

  • Pick one change from each survey and make it.
  • Tell listeners what changed and why, in the next episode.
  • Try a change for a few episodes before judging it.
  • Follow suggestions that fit your show, and thank the rest.

When to ask again

Ask for feedback when something has changed, or when enough time has passed for the answers to be different. After a new format, a new host, or a run of episodes on a new topic, a short survey tells you whether the change worked. A regular check, two or three times a year, catches slow drifts that you would not notice from inside the show. Asking every week teaches you nothing new and trains listeners to ignore the request.

Keep each survey short. Five to eight questions is the range most listeners will finish, and a survey that takes more than a few minutes will be abandoned. Ask at a moment when listeners are already thinking about the show, such as the end of an episode or the day after a release, and give them a reason to answer: tell them what you will do with the answers, and when they will hear about it. A survey with a purpose gets answers; a survey with no purpose gets ignored.

  • Ask after a change, and two or three times a year as a routine.
  • Keep it to five to eight questions, and a few minutes at most.
  • Ask when listeners are already thinking about the show.
  • Say what you will do with the answers, and when they will hear about it.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is asking did you like it and nothing else. That question can only be answered yes or no, and neither answer tells you what to change. A survey needs questions about specific moments, specific topics and specific lengths, because those are the answers you can act on. The second mistake is the opposite: thirty questions nobody finishes. A long survey is abandoned halfway, and the answers you get come only from the most patient listeners, which is not a fair sample of your audience.

The third mistake is asking leading questions and then believing the praise they produce. The fourth is collecting answers and never acting on them, which teaches listeners that their time was wasted and makes the next survey harder. And the fifth is taking one angry answer as the voice of the whole audience. One listener can be wrong, and one listener can be right; the difference is whether other listeners say the same thing. Read the whole set, look for patterns, and act on the patterns.

  • Never ask only did you like it: ask about specific moments and topics.
  • Keep the survey short, or only the most patient listeners will finish it.
  • Do not ask leading questions and then believe the praise.
  • Act on the answers, and tell listeners what changed.
  • One answer is one opinion; a pattern across answers is a finding.
