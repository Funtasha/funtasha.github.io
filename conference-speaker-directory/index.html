How to use this guide

This guide explains how to find the right speaker for a conference, check that they are as good as they look, invite them properly, and handle the money side honestly. It is written for organizers — first-timers and experienced ones — who want a speaker the audience will remember for the right reasons. It carries no list of speakers, because a list would go out of date and could never cover every topic; instead it teaches you where to look and what to check, so you can find the right person for your event.

Work through the guide in order if you are starting from nothing: where to look, how to check, how to invite, and what to pay. If you already have a shortlist, jump to the checking section, which is where most bad bookings are caught. The common mistakes at the end are worth reading whatever stage you are at, because they are the errors organizers repeat most often.

One principle runs through the whole guide: a good speaker is not the most famous name you can afford, but the person whose talk fits your audience, your topic and your event. Fame fills seats on the first day; a talk that fits is what people talk about afterwards. Keep that test in mind at every step, and the rest of the guide falls into place.

Where to look for speakers

The best place to start is your own network and your own experience. Think of talks you have enjoyed, speakers colleagues have recommended, and people whose work your audience already follows. A recommendation from someone who has seen the speaker live is worth more than any profile page, because it answers the one question a profile cannot: what were they actually like on stage?

Beyond personal recommendations, the usual places to look are speaker bureaus and agencies, conference programmes from events like yours, professional associations in your field, universities, and published authors and researchers on your topic. Video platforms carry recordings of many talks, which let you judge a speaker before you ever contact them. Industry podcasts and panel discussions are another good source — a guest who is clear and engaging in conversation is often clear and engaging on stage.

When you search, search by topic and by audience, not just by name. A speaker who is brilliant for a technical audience may lose a general one, and a speaker who energizes a room of beginners may bore a room of experts. Look for people who have spoken to audiences like yours, on topics like yours, and who have done it more than once. A single good talk can be luck; a pattern of good talks is evidence.

Be careful with lists that rank speakers by fee or by fame. A high fee proves a speaker is in demand, not that they fit your event, and a famous name can draw a crowd that leaves disappointed if the talk does not match the audience. Use lists as a starting point for names, then do the checking this guide describes before you invite anyone.

Checking a speaker: talks, videos, references

Checking a speaker takes an hour and saves a conference. Start with video: watch at least one full talk, not just a highlight reel, and watch it the way your audience will — for clarity, pace, structure and energy. A good talk has a clear point, builds towards it, and leaves the audience with something to do or think about. A weak talk wanders, repeats itself, or leans on slides the speaker reads aloud.

  • Watch a full talk, not a showreel. Showreels show the best minutes; a full talk shows the whole speaker.
  • Check the topic fit. Has this person spoken on your topic, to an audience like yours, recently?
  • Ask for references from other organizers, and actually contact them. Ask what the audience said afterwards.
  • Read the speaker's recent work — a book, articles or posts — to check the ideas are current and sound.
  • Check reliability: do they answer messages promptly, meet deadlines, and confirm details in writing?
  • Ask about the talk itself: what is the title, the outline, the length, and what the audience will take away?

References deserve a little more attention, because organizers often skip them and regret it. Ask the speaker for two or three organizers they have worked with, then ask those organizers three questions: how was the talk, how was the speaker to work with, and would you book them again? The third question is the one that matters most — an organizer who would book a speaker again is giving you the strongest recommendation there is.

Watch out for warning signs during checking: no video of a real talk anywhere, a speaker who will not share an outline, references who are vague or unreachable, or a speaker who seems more interested in the fee than in the audience. Any one of these is a reason to slow down and check harder; two of them together are a reason to look elsewhere.

The invitation: what to say

A good invitation is short, specific and honest. It says what the event is, who the audience is, what you would like the speaker to talk about, when and where it happens, and what you are offering. Speakers receive many invitations, and the ones they accept are usually the ones that show the organizer has done their homework and knows what they want.

Start with why this speaker, for this event. Mention a talk you saw, a book you read, or work you admire — briefly and honestly. Then describe the event: the audience size and kind, the date and place, the length of the talk, and the topic or question you would like them to address. Be clear about whether you want a keynote, a session talk, or a panel contribution, because each one is a different job.

Say what you are offering in the first message: the fee or honorarium, travel and accommodation, and anything else — meals, a ticket for a guest, a recording of the talk. Speakers need this information to decide, and holding it back wastes everyone's time. If your budget is small, say so honestly and say what you can offer instead, such as a large audience in their field, a recording they can use, or expenses covered in full.

Give the speaker a clear next step and a date: a short call to discuss the talk, a deadline for their answer, and a single contact person. Once they accept, confirm everything in writing — date, time, topic, length, fee, expenses, and what happens if either side must cancel. A written confirmation protects both sides and prevents the misunderstandings that cause most speaker problems.

Fees and expenses: the honest picture

Speaker fees vary enormously, and this guide states no fee as fact, because a fee depends on the speaker, the event, the country and the year. What matters is understanding what a fee covers and how to talk about it honestly. A fee usually covers the talk itself, the preparation behind it, and the speaker's time on the day — not just the hour on stage, but the travel, the waiting and the conversations afterwards.

Expenses are separate from the fee in most arrangements: travel to the event, accommodation, and meals. Agree in advance who pays for what, and put it in writing. Common arrangements include the organizer booking and paying for travel and a hotel directly, or the speaker booking and claiming the cost back against receipts. Agree a limit for each part, and say how claims are made and when they are paid.

Some speakers speak without a fee — academics sharing research, practitioners building a reputation, or people who care about the cause behind the event. If you invite someone in that position, be honest about the budget, cover their expenses in full, and treat their time with the same respect you would give a paid speaker. A free talk is a gift, not a discount, and the speaker deserves the same care with scheduling, promotion and thanks.

Whatever the arrangement, get it in writing before you announce the speaker. The written agreement should cover the fee, the expenses, the talk details, the cancellation terms for both sides, and whether the talk will be recorded or streamed. Money agreed in writing is money without arguments; money agreed by memory is the commonest source of speaker disputes.

When things go wrong: cancellations and no-shows

Speakers cancel sometimes, for good reasons and bad ones, and the organizer who has a plan copes while the organizer who has not panics. The first line of defence is the written agreement: it should say what happens if either side cancels, how much notice is expected, and what is paid in each case. Agree these terms when everyone is happy, not when one side is already cancelling.

The second line of defence is a backup plan. Know, before the event, what you would do if a speaker cancelled the week before, or the day before, or on the morning. A backup speaker, a longer panel discussion, a workshop session, or a recorded talk can fill a gap — but only if you have thought about it in advance. Keep a shortlist of people who could step in, and keep their contact details where you can reach them quickly.

If a speaker does cancel, act fast and stay gracious. Tell the audience honestly, without blaming the speaker in public, and announce the replacement or the change of plan as soon as you can. Audiences forgive a change of speaker far more easily than they forgive confusion or silence. After the event, review what happened: was the cancellation foreseeable, and would a different agreement or a different choice of speaker have prevented it?

No-shows — a speaker who simply does not arrive — are rare but devastating. Reduce the risk by confirming the speaker the week before and the day before, by having a single contact person who knows the speaker's travel plans, and by keeping the speaker's phone number with the stage manager. If it happens, the backup plan is all you have, which is why it is worth making one.

Common mistakes

The mistakes organizers make with speakers are few, and they repeat at every level of event, from small meetups to large conferences. Learn them once and you will avoid them for years.

  • Booking by fame alone — a famous name who does not fit the audience leaves a disappointed room.
  • No video of a real talk — booking a speaker you have never seen speak is a gamble, not a decision.
  • Skipping references — a ten-minute call to a past organizer can save a ruined session.
  • A vague brief — a speaker who does not know what you want will give the talk they always give, not the talk you need.
  • Agreeing money by memory — fees and expenses agreed in conversation and never written down cause most disputes.
  • No backup plan — a cancellation without a plan becomes a crisis; with a plan it is an inconvenience.
  • Forgetting the speaker on the day — no welcome, no briefing, no check on sound and slides, and a speaker left to find the stage alone.

The thread running through all of these is preparation. The organizer who checks the speaker, briefs them well, confirms the money in writing, and plans for the worst case is the organizer whose speakers shine. The speaker does the talking, but the organizer creates the conditions for a great talk — and that work starts long before the speaker reaches the stage.

Questions people ask

Where do I find conference speakers?

Start with your own network and with talks you have enjoyed, then look at speaker bureaus, conference programmes from events like yours, professional associations, universities, and authors and researchers on your topic. Video platforms carry recordings of many talks, which let you judge a speaker before you contact them. Search by topic and audience, not just by name — a speaker who fits your audience matters more than a famous one who does not.

What should I check before inviting?

Watch at least one full talk, not just a highlight reel, and check the topic fit, the audience fit, and the speaker's reliability. Ask for references from other organizers and actually contact them — the most useful question is whether they would book the speaker again. Read the speaker's recent work to check the ideas are current. Watch out for no video of a real talk, no outline, or vague references.

How much do speakers cost?

Fees vary enormously by speaker, event and country, so there is no single figure. A fee usually covers the talk, the preparation and the speaker's time on the day, while travel and accommodation are usually agreed separately. Agree the fee, the expenses, the cancellation terms and the recording rights in writing before you announce the speaker. Some speakers speak without a fee — if so, cover their expenses in full and treat their time with respect.

How do I invite a speaker?

Write a short, specific invitation: why this speaker for this event, what the event is, who the audience is, what you would like them to talk about, and when and where it happens. Say what you are offering — fee, travel and accommodation — in the first message, and give a clear next step and a date. Once they accept, confirm everything in writing: date, time, topic, length, fee, expenses and cancellation terms.

What if a speaker cancels?

Act fast and stay gracious: tell the audience honestly, without blaming the speaker in public, and announce the replacement or the change of plan as soon as you can. Your written agreement should already cover cancellation terms for both sides, and you should have a backup plan — a backup speaker, a longer panel, or a workshop session. Review afterwards whether the cancellation was foreseeable and what would have prevented it.

Should I pay travel costs?

Usually yes — travel and accommodation are normally paid by the organizer, either booked directly or claimed back against receipts, in addition to any fee. Agree in advance who pays for what, set a limit for each part, and put it in writing. If your budget cannot cover travel, say so honestly in the first message rather than after the speaker has accepted, so the speaker can decide with full information.
