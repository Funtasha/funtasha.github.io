    The sponsor tiers explained

    Sponsor tiers are packages: a set of benefits offered at a set level of support. Most events use three or four tiers, often named after metals or simple words — Gold, Silver, Bronze, or Lead, Supporting, Community. The names matter less than the clarity: each tier should offer a distinct set of benefits, and a sponsor should be able to see at a glance what each level buys.

    A typical top tier includes the most visible benefits: the logo on the main stage and the event page, a speaking slot or a hosted session, a large exhibition space, and the most complimentary tickets. A middle tier includes the logo in the programme and on signage, a smaller exhibition space, and fewer tickets. A lower tier includes the logo in the programme and a ticket or two. The exact benefits are yours to decide — what matters is that each tier is genuinely different, and that the top tier is worth its price.

    Keep the tiers few and the benefits deliverable. Five tiers with overlapping benefits confuse sponsors and create arguments about who got what. Three clear tiers, each with benefits you can actually deliver, are better than five ambitious ones. And be honest about what each tier costs you to deliver — a speaking slot costs nothing, but exhibition space, tickets and printed logos all have real costs, and a tier that costs more to deliver than it brings in is a loss, not a sponsorship.

    What sponsors get

    Sponsors pay for three things: visibility, access and association. Visibility is the logo, the mentions, the signage — being seen by your audience. Access is the chance to meet that audience: exhibition space, a speaking slot, a hosted session, or a list of attendees who agreed to be contacted. Association is the link between the sponsor's name and your event's reputation — a good event makes its sponsors look good, and a bad one does the opposite.

    The benefits that matter most to a sponsor depend on the sponsor. A company launching a product wants a speaking slot and a demonstration space. A company hiring wants to meet attendees. A local business wants its name in front of the community. Ask each sponsor what they want before you offer a tier — the sponsor who gets what they wanted is the sponsor who comes back next year, and the sponsor who gets a generic package is the sponsor who does not.

    Whatever you promise, write it down and deliver it. The tracker has a field for promised items precisely because promises are easy to make and easy to forget: the logo on the stage, the mention in the opening, the introduction to a speaker. Record each promise when you make it, check it off when you deliver it, and review the list the week before the event. A sponsor who was promised a logo and never saw it will not sponsor you twice.

    The follow-up that keeps sponsors

    Most sponsorships are lost not at the pitch but in the silence afterwards. A sponsor who hears nothing between signing and the event starts to wonder whether the event is real, whether their logo will appear, and whether anyone is in charge. A short message every few weeks — a programme update, a ticket milestone, a question about their stand — costs minutes and keeps the relationship warm.

    The follow-up that matters most is the one after the event. Within a week, send each sponsor a short report: how many people attended, what the audience said, photographs of their logo and their stand, and the numbers that show the event worked. Thank them by name, and tell them what you would like to do differently next year. A sponsor who receives a good report is a sponsor who takes your call next year; a sponsor who hears nothing assumes the event did not matter.

    Keep notes on every sponsor: who you spoke to, what they wanted, what you promised, and how it went. The tracker stores the facts; your notes store the relationship. When you approach the same sponsor next year, those notes are the difference between a cold pitch and a warm conversation — and warm conversations close faster than cold ones.

    Tracking money honestly

    Sponsorship money is easy to lose track of, because it arrives in pieces: a pledge in one month, a payment in another, a part-payment now and the rest after the event. The tracker separates the two numbers that matter — the amount pledged and the amount received — and shows the outstanding balance, which is the difference. Check the outstanding balance weekly in the run-up to the event, and chase late payments early and politely.

    Enter amounts in your own currency, and enter them as the numbers you actually agreed — not rounded, not guessed. A pledge is a promise, not money in the bank, and the tracker treats it that way: pledged and received are separate, and only the received total is money you can spend. Never commit event spending against pledged money that has not arrived, because a pledge that falls through leaves a hole no last-minute saving can fill.

    Keep a paper trail for every sponsor: the agreement, the invoice, the receipt, and the record of what was delivered. The tracker is your working list, not your accounts — your invoices and receipts are the real record, and they are what you need if a payment is disputed or a sponsor asks what they paid for. A tidy record is also the basis for next year's pricing: when you know what each tier actually brought in, you can price the next event honestly.

    Common mistakes

    The mistakes organizers make with sponsors are few, and they all come from the same root: treating sponsorship as money that arrives by itself, rather than a relationship that needs work. Learn the list once and you will keep more sponsors than you lose.

    • Promising the logo and forgetting it — a promise made in a meeting and never recorded is a promise broken by accident.
    • No written agreement — terms agreed by memory, then disputed when the event arrives.
    • Too many tiers — five overlapping packages that confuse sponsors and create arguments about who got what.
    • Chasing new sponsors while ignoring old ones — a returning sponsor costs less effort than a new one and is worth more.
    • Spending pledged money before it arrives — a pledge is a promise, not cash, and promises sometimes fall through.
    • Silence after the event — no report, no thanks, no photographs, and a sponsor who never hears from you again.
    • Overpromising the audience — numbers the event cannot deliver, discovered by the sponsor on the day.

    Notice that every mistake on this list is preventable with a list, a note or a message. Sponsorship is not a talent; it is a habit of writing things down, following up, and delivering what you promised. The tracker on this page handles the writing down. The rest is up to you.

    After the event: the thank-you and the report

    The week after the event is when next year's sponsorship is won or lost. Send each sponsor a thank-you message within a few days, while the event is still fresh, and follow it with a short report within a week. The report does not need to be long: how many people attended, what the audience said, photographs of the sponsor's logo and stand, and the numbers that show the event worked.

    Be honest in the report. If attendance was lower than expected, say so and say why — sponsors respect honesty far more than they respect inflated numbers, and a sponsor who discovers the truth later will not trust you again. If something went wrong with a sponsor's benefits, own it, apologise, and say what you will do differently. An honest report builds a relationship; a polished one builds suspicion.

    Then ask for the conversation, not the commitment. A short call to ask how the sponsor found the event, what they would change, and whether they would consider next year is worth more than a sales pitch sent cold. Listen more than you talk: the sponsor's answers tell you how to price and shape next year's tiers. And record the outcome in the tracker, so next year's organizer — even if it is you, a year later — starts from knowledge, not from memory.

Keep the tiers few and the benefits deliverable.
