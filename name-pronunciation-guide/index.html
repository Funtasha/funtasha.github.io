Name Pronunciation Guide

This guide shows you how to say 66 common names from around the world, with plain-words sound breakdowns and listen buttons. Search a name, read it in parts, and practise it slowly before you say it to someone.

These breakdowns are guides, not perfect recordings. Sounds differ by region and family, and the listen button uses your device's own voice. When you are unsure, ask the person — they will be glad you asked.

What this guide is for

Saying someone's name right is one of the simplest ways to show respect. It takes only a few seconds of effort, and it tells the other person that they matter to you. Yet many of us quietly avoid names we are unsure about, or we shorten them without asking, or we guess and hope for the best. This guide exists to remove that guesswork. It covers 66 common names from South Asia, the Arab world, Africa, Europe, East Asia and Latin America, with a plain-words breakdown for each one. Every entry tells you the syllables, which part carries the stress, and which sounds to watch. You do not need any training in phonetics to use it. If you can read the breakdown aloud slowly, you can say the name.

The guide is built for everyday moments. You might be a teacher meeting a new class, a manager welcoming a new colleague, a nurse calling a patient, or a friend who keeps meeting the same person and still stumbles over their name. In all of those moments, the fix is the same: slow down, break the name into parts, and practise it a few times before you need it. The listen button on each entry speaks the name with your device's own voice, so you can hear the shape of it before you try it yourself. Used together, the written breakdown and the spoken version give you two ways into the same sound.

How to use the name directory

Start at the search box near the top of the page. Type the name you want, or just the first few letters, and the list narrows as you type. If you are browsing rather than searching, use the region filter to look through one group at a time. Each entry shows the name, the region it is commonly linked with, and the breakdown. Read the breakdown first, then tap the listen button and hear it once. Then say it yourself, slowly, one part at a time. The list shows fifteen names at first; tap Show more names to see the rest.

On a phone, everything works by touch. The search box, the filter, the listen buttons and the practise button are all large enough to tap without zooming. On a desktop, you can type in the search box and use the keyboard as well. Nothing on this page needs an account, and nothing is uploaded anywhere. The list of names lives in the page itself, and the listen button uses the speech voice already built into your device or browser.

If your device has no speech voice, the listen button will tell you so plainly instead of failing silently. That happens on some browsers and some older phones. In that case, the written breakdown still gives you everything you need: the syllables, the stressed part, and the sounds to watch. Chrome on Android usually has a voice ready, so that is a good place to try if your usual browser stays quiet.

How to break a name into parts

Every name in this guide is written as a string of syllables with the stressed syllable in capital letters. Take the name Siobhan, written as shi-VAWN. That tells you three things at once: the name has two parts, the second part carries the stress, and the first part sounds like shi while the second sounds like vawn. You do not need to know Irish spelling rules to read that. The capital letters do the work for you.

The best way to practise is in three passes. First, read the breakdown silently and notice where the stress falls. Second, say each part on its own, slowly, with a small pause between parts. Third, join the parts together at normal speed and say the whole name three times. If a part feels awkward in your mouth, slow down again and say just that part five times. Most names become comfortable after only a few minutes of this, because the difficulty is usually in one sound, not in the whole name.

Pay special attention to the Sounds to watch line. It points out the one or two things that English speakers most often get wrong for that name: a rolled r, a soft h, a vowel that is longer than it looks, or a letter combination that does not sound the way it is spelled. Those small notes are where the real help lives. A name is rarely hard all the way through. It is usually one sound that needs a little attention, and once that sound is right, the rest of the name falls into place.

How to ask someone how to say their name

No guide can replace asking the person themselves. Names vary by family, by region and by personal habit, and the person who owns the name is the only true expert on it. Asking is not rude. It is the opposite of rude. It shows that you care enough to get it right. The simplest way to ask is also the most honest: Could you say your name for me slowly, so I can learn it properly? Most people will be pleased to help, and many will repeat it twice without being asked.

When they say it, listen for the stress first, because stress is what makes a name sound right or wrong to its owner. Then repeat it back and ask if you got it close. If they correct you, thank them and try again. Do not apologise over and over, and do not make a joke about how hard the name is. A short thank you and a second try is enough. If you know you will forget, write the breakdown down in your own words the moment you can, the same way this guide writes it, with the stressed part in capitals.

There are two things worth avoiding. The first is shortening a name without being invited to do so. A nickname is a gift the person gives you, not something you take because the full name feels long. The second is avoiding the name altogether and hoping nobody notices. People do notice, and it feels worse than a clumsy attempt. A careful try, followed by a correction, is always better than silence.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is reading the spelling instead of the sound. English spelling is a poor guide to how names sound, and it is a worse guide still for names from other languages. Siobhan does not sound like see-oh-bhan, Aoife does not sound like ay-oy-fee, and Joaquin does not sound like joe-a-quin. The breakdowns in this guide are written from the sound, not from the letters, and that is why they work. Trust the breakdown over the spelling every time.

The second mistake is rushing. When we are nervous about a name, we tend to say it fast, as if speed will hide the uncertainty. It does the opposite: it squeezes the syllables together and drops the stress. Slow is not embarrassing. Slow is how you learn any new sound. Say the name slowly three times, then at normal speed. If you are introducing someone, a slow and careful name sounds respectful, not hesitant.

The third mistake is giving up after one bad attempt. If you say a name and it comes out wrong, the fix is a second try, not a retreat. Say it again, more slowly, and check with the person. Nobody expects a perfect first attempt. They expect effort, and effort is easy to see. The fourth mistake is practising only in your head. Silent reading does not train your mouth. You have to say the sounds out loud, even quietly, for the name to become easy.

Worked examples: three names practised slowly

Take Siobhan first. The breakdown is shi-VAWN, stress on the second part. Say shi on its own, the way you would say she, but shorter. Then say vawn, rhyming with dawn. Now join them: shi-VAWN, shi-VAWN, shi-VAWN. The trap is the spelling: the b is silent in the way the name is spoken, and the io sounds like a short i. Once you know that, the name is only two easy sounds.

Next, Xiaoming. The breakdown is SHYOW-ming, stress on the first part. Say shyow, starting with sh and gliding into ow, the way you say how. Then say ming, rhyming with sing. Join them: SHYOW-ming. The trap is the X, which in this name sounds like sh, and the ao, which glides rather than splitting into two vowels. Say it slowly five times and the glide becomes natural.

Finally, Joaquin. The breakdown is wah-KEEN, stress on the second part. Say wah, like the start of water. Then say keen, like the word keen. Join them: wah-KEEN. The trap is the J, which sounds like a soft h, and the qu, which sounds like a plain k. The name has only two parts, and both are sounds you already use every day.

What this page cannot do

This guide cannot give you a perfect recording of any name. The breakdowns are written guides to the most common way each name is said, and the listen button uses a general device voice, not a native speaker of the language the name comes from. Real pronunciation varies. A name may sound slightly different in one family than in another, and the same spelling may be said differently in different countries. That is normal, and it is why the page keeps telling you to ask the person when you can.

The guide also cannot cover every name. Sixty-six names is a useful start, and it covers many of the names people meet most often, but the world has far more names than any one page can hold. If the name you need is not here, the method still works: ask the person to say it slowly, write down the parts in your own words with the stressed part in capitals, and practise it out loud. That method is the real skill this page teaches. The directory is the practice ground, and the method is what you take with you when you meet a name you have never seen before.

Questions people ask

Why are some names hard to pronounce?

Names are hard when their spelling does not match the sounds of English, or when they use a sound your mouth is not used to making. A rolled r, a soft throat sound, or a vowel that is longer than it looks can all trip you up. Breaking the name into parts and practising one part at a time fixes almost all of it.

How do I ask someone how to say their name?

Ask plainly and kindly: could you say your name for me slowly, so I can learn it properly? Listen for the stress first, repeat it back, and ask if you got it close. Thank them for the correction and try again. Most people are glad to help.

What if I say a name wrong?

Say it again, more slowly, and check with the person. A second try shows respect, and nobody expects a perfect first attempt. Do not apologise over and over or make a joke about the name. A short thank you and another try is enough.

Should I shorten a name I cannot say?

No, not unless the person invites you to. A nickname is something the person offers, not something you take because the full name feels long. Practise the full name instead. It usually becomes easy after a few minutes of slow practice.

How do I practise a new name?

Read the breakdown, say each part on its own slowly, then join the parts at normal speed and say the whole name three times. If one part feels awkward, say just that part five times. Practise out loud, not only in your head, so your mouth learns the sounds.

Do names sound the same in every country?

No. The same name can sound different in different countries and families, and spelling is only a rough guide. The breakdowns here show the most common way each name is said. When you are unsure, ask the person — they are the expert on their own name.
