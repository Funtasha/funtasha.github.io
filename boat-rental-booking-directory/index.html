How boat rental works

Renting a boat is like renting a car, with water underneath. You choose a boat, agree the dates, show that you are allowed to drive it, pay a deposit, and take the boat for the agreed time. The company hands the boat over with fuel, safety gear and a briefing, and you bring it back at the agreed time, in the state you took it. Some rentals include a skipper, a professional who drives the boat for you, and some are bareboat, which means you drive it yourself. A skippered rental costs more and suits anyone without experience; a bareboat rental suits people who know what they are doing.

What the price includes varies. Most rentals include the boat, the safety gear, and a full tank of fuel to start with, with fuel used charged at the end. Some include bedding, cooking gear and water toys, and some charge extra for them. Some include insurance with an excess, which is the amount you pay first if something is damaged, and some offer a lower excess for an extra charge. Before you compare prices, find out what each price includes, because a low price that excludes the essentials is not low at all.

The licence question

Whether you need a licence to rent a boat depends on where you are, what kind of boat it is, and how powerful its engine is. In some places, small boats with small engines need no licence at all. In others, every motor boat needs one. In some places, a licence from your own country is accepted, and in others it is not. The rules also differ between inland waters and the sea, and between a boat you drive yourself and a boat with a skipper. There is no single answer that works everywhere, and anyone who gives you one is guessing.

Check with the rental company and with the official authority for the waters you will sail, before you book. Ask the company what licence or experience they require, and ask what proof they want to see. If you need a licence and do not have one, ask whether the company offers a short course, or whether a skippered rental is the better choice for this trip. Never set off without the licence the place requires: an unlicensed rental can mean no insurance, a fine, and a ruined trip.

Checking the boat before you leave

Before you leave the dock, walk around the boat with the handover person and check it together. Look at the hull for scratches and dents, and point out every mark you find, so you are not blamed for damage that was already there. Take photos of the marks, with the date showing, because a photo is the proof that ends an argument. Check the engine starts, the steering works, and the lights work. Check the safety gear: lifejackets for everyone, in sizes that fit, a working radio, flares, and a first aid kit.

Ask how everything works before you need it in a hurry: how to start and stop the engine, how to use the anchor, where the fuel tank is and how to read its level, and how to call for help on the radio. Ask where you may go and where you may not: most rentals have limits, such as staying within a marked area or keeping a distance from the shore. And ask what to do if something breaks: who to call, and what number to use. A ten-minute check at the dock saves hours of trouble on the water.

  • Take photos of the marks, with the date showing.
  • Check the engine, steering, lights and safety gear.
  • Ask how the engine, anchor, fuel gauge and radio work.
  • Ask where you may go, and who to call if something breaks.

Insurance and the deposit

Most rentals include insurance, and most insurance has an excess. The excess is the amount you pay first if the boat is damaged, and the insurer pays the rest. The excess can be large, and some companies offer a lower excess for an extra daily charge. Read the insurance terms before you sign: what is covered, what is not, and what the excess is. Damage caused by breaking the rules, such as sailing outside the allowed area or sailing at night when the agreement forbids it, is usually not covered at all.

The deposit is separate from the insurance. It is money held by the company, usually as a hold on a card, and returned when the boat comes back in the state it left in. The deposit covers small damage, missing gear and extra fuel, and it is returned in full if none of those apply. Ask how much the deposit is, how it is held, and when it is returned, because holds can take days to clear. And keep the handover photos: they are what proves the boat came back as it left.

  • Damage from breaking the rules is usually not covered.
  • The deposit is held and returned when the boat comes back as it left.
  • Ask how the deposit is held, and when it is returned.

Returning the boat

Bring the boat back at the agreed time, to the agreed place, and in the state you took it. Fill the fuel tank if the agreement says so, or expect the fuel used to be charged. Tidy the boat: remove your belongings, clear the rubbish, and leave the boat as you found it. A boat returned late can be charged for extra time, and a boat returned dirty can be charged for cleaning, so leave time for both.

Do the return check together, as you did at handover. Walk around the boat with the company, point out any new marks, and be honest about anything that happened: a scrape, a broken fitting, a lost fender. Honesty at the return is cheaper than a dispute later, and most companies are fair about small, honest damage. Get the return in writing: a note that the boat came back, when, and in what state, and a note of when the deposit will be returned. Keep that note until the deposit is back.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is no licence check. Renters assume the rules are the same as at home, and find out on the water that they are not. The second mistake is no damage photos before leaving, which turns an old scratch into your scratch when the boat comes back. The third is not reading the insurance terms, and finding out that the excess is larger than expected, or that the damage is not covered because a rule was broken.

The fourth mistake is sailing outside the allowed area, which can void the insurance and leave you paying for any damage in full. The fifth is returning late without warning, which can be charged as an extra day. And the sixth is not asking how things work at handover, and then struggling with the anchor, the engine or the radio when it matters. Check the licence, take the photos, read the terms, and ask every question before you leave the dock.

  • Check the licence before you book, not on the water.
  • Take damage photos before leaving, with the date showing.
  • Read the insurance terms, including the excess.
  • Stay inside the allowed area, or the insurance may not cover you.
  • Return on time, and warn the company if you will be late.
  • Ask how everything works at handover, not when you need it.

Questions people ask

How do I rent a boat?

Choose a boat, agree the dates, show that you are allowed to drive it, and pay a deposit. The company hands the boat over with fuel, safety gear and a briefing, and you bring it back at the agreed time in the state you took it. Some rentals include a skipper who drives for you.

Do I need a licence?

It depends on the place, the boat and the engine. Some places need no licence for small boats, and others need one for every motor boat. Check with the rental company and the official authority for the waters you will sail, before you book.

What should I check on the boat?

Walk around it with the handover person, note every existing mark, and take photos with the date showing. Check the engine, steering, lights and safety gear. Ask how the engine, anchor, fuel gauge and radio work, and where you may and may not go.

How much does rental cost?

Rental prices vary widely by place, boat size, season and length of hire, so no single figure is useful. Find out what each price includes, because fuel, bedding, extras and insurance are sometimes included and sometimes charged separately.

What about insurance?

Most rentals include insurance with an excess, which is the amount you pay first if the boat is damaged. Read the terms before you sign: what is covered, what is not, and what the excess is. Damage from breaking the rules is usually not covered at all.

What if the boat is damaged?

Tell the company at once, and be honest about what happened. The deposit covers small damage and missing gear, and the insurance covers the rest up to its terms. The handover photos prove which marks were already there, which is why taking them matters.
