Digital Craft Pattern Marketplace

A digital craft pattern is a set of instructions you buy, download, and make yourself — and the marketplaces that sell them connect makers with designers all over the world. This guide explains how to buy wisely and how to sell fairly.

This guide explains how pattern marketplaces work — it lists no patterns or sellers, and it is not legal advice. Licence terms differ by marketplace and by seller, so read the licence on every pattern you buy or sell.

How to use this guide

A digital craft pattern is a strange and wonderful thing: a set of instructions, sold as a file, that becomes a jumper, a quilt, a toy, or a table. The marketplaces that sell patterns connect designers with makers all over the world, and they have changed how crafts are learned and shared. This guide explains how they work, how to buy wisely, and how to sell fairly.

Work through the sections in order. First, how marketplaces work — who does what, and who gets paid. Then buying patterns: what to check before you pay. Then selling your own: pricing, photographs, and fees. Then licences and copyright, the two subjects that cause most of the arguments. Finish with the common mistakes, and you will use these marketplaces with confidence.

One honest line: this guide lists no patterns or sellers, and it is not legal advice. Marketplaces change their rules, sellers come and go, and licence terms differ by listing. What does not change is how to read a listing, how to price honestly, and how to respect the designer's work — and that is what this page teaches.

How marketplaces work

A pattern marketplace is a shop window shared by many designers. Each designer opens a shop, lists digital patterns for sale, and the marketplace handles the payment, the download, and a share of the promotion. The buyer pays, downloads the files, and makes the item at home. The marketplace takes a fee from each sale, and the designer keeps the rest.

The pattern itself is usually a PDF file: instructions, diagrams, measurements, and photographs of the finished item. Some patterns come with video links or extra files. What you buy is the right to use the instructions — not the design itself, and not the right to share the file or claim the design as your own. That distinction matters, and it is where most disputes begin.

Marketplaces differ in what they charge, how they promote new designers, and how they handle complaints. Some take a small fee per sale; some take a monthly shop fee as well. Some promote new shops; some bury them. Before you sell, compare two or three marketplaces on fees, audience, and rules — and before you buy, check the seller's reviews and the listing's licence, not just the price.

Buying patterns

Buying a pattern well starts with the listing. Read it fully before you pay: what the pattern makes, what skill level it needs, what materials it uses, and what format the files are in. Check the finished measurements, not just the photograph — a jumper that looks generous on the model may be smaller than you expect. Check whether the pattern includes all sizes or only one, and whether the instructions are written or charted, or both.

Check the licence. Most patterns are licensed for personal use: you may make the item for yourself or as a gift, but not sell the finished items. Some allow small-scale selling of finished items, with credit to the designer. The licence is part of the listing, and it varies by seller — read it, because it is the agreement you are accepting.

Check the reviews, and read them honestly. A pattern with many reviews and a high rating is usually safe; a pattern with no reviews may still be good, but it is a gamble. Look for reviews that mention errors, unclear instructions, or missing sizes — those are the complaints that matter. And download your files straight away, and keep a copy somewhere safe. Marketplaces close, shops disappear, and a pattern you cannot download again is a pattern you have lost.

Selling your own patterns

Selling patterns starts with one good pattern, well tested. Write the instructions as if your reader has never made the item before, because they have not. Test the pattern with at least two makers who are not you, and fix everything they trip over. A pattern with errors will earn bad reviews faster than any amount of promotion can repair.

Photographs sell patterns. Show the finished item clearly, in good light, from more than one angle, and show it being used — a jumper on a person, a bag holding things, a toy in a child's hands. Write an honest description: what the pattern makes, what skill it needs, what materials it uses, and what the buyer receives. Say what the licence allows, plainly, in the listing itself.

Price by the value of the finished knowledge, not only by the hours you spent writing it. A pattern that saves a maker ten hours of figuring out is worth more than the hours it took to write. Look at what similar patterns sell for, and remember that the marketplace takes a fee from every sale — price so that the fee leaves you with something worth having. And start with one pattern, not ten: learn how the marketplace works, gather reviews, and then add more.

Licences explained

A licence is the agreement between the designer and the buyer, and it says what the buyer may do with the pattern. The most common licence is personal use: the buyer may make the item for themselves or as a gift, but may not sell the finished items, share the file, or teach from it. Some designers allow small-scale selling of finished items, often with a limit on numbers and a requirement to credit the designer. Some allow teaching, for a fee. The listing states the licence, and the listing is the agreement.

As a buyer, read the licence before you pay, and keep to it. If you want to sell the finished items, look for a licence that allows it — and if none does, ask the designer. Many designers will agree a commercial licence for a fair price, and asking is always better than assuming. As a seller, write your licence plainly in the listing, in words a buyer can understand. A licence nobody reads is a licence nobody follows.

Licences differ by marketplace as well as by seller. Some marketplaces set a default licence; some let the seller write their own. Check both: the marketplace's rules and the listing's terms. And remember that a licence is a contract — breaking it is not a small thing, even when nobody notices.

Copyright points

The designer owns the copyright in the pattern: the words, the diagrams, and the design as expressed in the instructions. Buying a pattern does not give you the copyright, and it does not give you the right to share the file, resell the pattern, publish it, or claim it as your own work. That is true whether or not the pattern carries a copyright notice.

Making the item is different from copying the pattern. When you buy a pattern, you buy the right to make the item it describes, within the licence. What you may do with the finished item — keep it, give it, sell it — depends on the licence, as the last section explained. What you may not do is copy the pattern itself, in whole or in part, and pass it on.

If you sell patterns, protect your work. Keep records of what you created and when: dated drafts, photographs, and the first listing. Register your work where your country allows it, and watermark your photographs so they cannot be reused without your name. If someone copies your pattern, keep records and seek advice — this page is not legal advice, but a dated record is the first thing any adviser will ask for.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is buying a pattern with no licence check. A pattern bought for personal use cannot be used to stock a shop. Read the licence before you pay, and ask the designer if you want more.

The second is pricing by hours alone. A pattern's value is in what it teaches, not only in the hours it took to write. Price by value, check what similar patterns sell for, and remember the marketplace fee.

The third is selling an untested pattern. A pattern nobody has tested will have errors, and errors earn bad reviews that never go away. Test with two makers who are not you, and fix what they find.

The fourth is weak photographs. Buyers judge a pattern by its photographs, and a blurry photograph of a good design will lose to a clear photograph of an average one. Show the finished item well, from more than one angle.

The fifth is ignoring the licence as a seller. A licence nobody can find is a licence nobody follows. Write it plainly in the listing, in words a buyer can understand, and answer questions about it promptly.

Questions people ask

How do pattern marketplaces work?

Designers list digital patterns for sale, buyers pay and download the files, and the marketplace takes a fee from each sale. The pattern is a set of instructions — you buy the right to use it, not the design itself.

How do I buy a pattern?

Search by craft and skill level, read the listing carefully, and check the licence, the format, and the reviews before you pay. Download the files straight away, and keep a copy somewhere safe.

Can I sell my own patterns?

Yes — most marketplaces let anyone open a shop, and many designers start with a single pattern. You will need clear photographs, honest descriptions, and a licence that says what buyers may do with your work.

How do I price a pattern?

Price by the value of the finished knowledge, not only by the hours you spent writing it. Look at what similar patterns sell for, and remember that the marketplace takes a fee from every sale.

What licence do I get?

It depends on the listing: most patterns are licensed for personal use, and some allow small-scale selling of finished items. Read the licence on the pattern you buy — it varies by seller, and the listing is the agreement.

What about copyright?

The designer owns the copyright in the pattern, and buying it does not give you the right to share, resell, or claim it as your own. If you sell patterns, register your work where your country allows it, and keep records of what you created and when.
