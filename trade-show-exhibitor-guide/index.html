Trade Show Exhibitor Guide

A trade show is an event where businesses show their products to buyers, and exhibiting puts your goods in front of the people who order them. This guide explains how to pick the right show, plan a booth, talk to buyers, and turn the contacts you make into orders.

This page lists no shows and takes no bookings. Costs are explained as ranges and vary widely by show, city and booth size, so always get a written quote from the show organiser before you commit.

What this is and who needs it

A trade show is an event where businesses in one industry gather to show their products to buyers, distributors and other businesses. Exhibiting means taking a booth at the show, displaying your goods, and meeting the people who might order them. It is one of the few ways to put a physical product in front of many buyers in a few days, and for many businesses it is the most important selling event of the year.

This guide is for businesses thinking about exhibiting for the first time, and for businesses that have exhibited before and want better results. It suits makers, wholesalers and brands whose buyers attend shows, and it is honest about when exhibiting is the wrong choice. If your buyers do not go to shows, or if one show would use up your whole marketing budget, there are better ways to spend the money.

The guide explains how to pick the right show, what exhibiting costs, how to plan a booth that attracts buyers, how to talk to visitors, and how to follow up afterwards so the contacts become orders. It lists no shows and takes no bookings, because the right show depends on your industry and your buyers.

How it works, step by step

Exhibiting follows a clear order, and the work starts months before the show opens. The businesses that do well are the ones that prepare early, and the ones that struggle are usually the ones that booked late and planned in a rush.

  1. Pick the show. Look for a show where your buyers already go, check the visitor list and the size, and ask other exhibitors what they thought of it. Visit as a guest first if you can.
  2. Book early. Space is cheaper and better placed when booked early, and early booking gives you time to plan the booth, the samples and the travel.
  3. Plan the booth. Decide what to show, how to display it, and what the booth should say in the first few seconds a visitor sees it. Keep it simple: a few strong products beat a crowded table.
  4. Prepare the materials. Samples, a price list, business cards, and a simple way to record who visited and what they wanted. Order printed material early, because printers are busy before a show.
  5. Brief the staff. Everyone on the booth should know the products, the prices, and what to do when a serious buyer appears. Agree who greets visitors, who answers technical questions, and who records the leads.
  6. Work the booth. Stand where visitors can see you, greet them, listen to what they need, and take notes on every serious conversation.
  7. Follow up within days. Send the information you promised, answer the questions they asked, and suggest the next step while the show is still fresh in their minds.

Each step builds on the one before it. A good show is planned, not improvised, and the follow-up matters as much as the booth itself.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

The cost of exhibiting varies widely, and this guide quotes no figure as fact. The largest part is usually the space hire, which depends on the show, the city and the size of the booth. On top of that come the booth itself, which can be a simple shell scheme supplied by the organiser or a custom stand you build, travel and accommodation for your staff, the samples and printed material, and the cost of getting everything to the show and back. A small booth at a local show costs far less than a large stand at an international one, and the only reliable figure is a written quote from the organiser that lists every charge.

Time is the other cost, and it is larger than most first-time exhibitors expect. Planning starts months before the show, the show itself takes several days including setting up and taking down, and the follow-up takes a week or more afterwards. Budget the staff time honestly, because a booth staffed by tired people who arrived late is a booth that loses buyers.

Before you start, gather what you need: the show dates and the organiser's terms, a budget that covers the space and all the extras, the samples and materials you will show, and the people who will staff the booth. Book travel and accommodation early, because prices rise as the show approaches, and confirm the rules for setting up, including what time you can arrive and what you may bring.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

First-time exhibitors make the same mistakes, and most of them are avoidable with a little planning. The cost of a mistake at a trade show is high, because the show only lasts a few days and there is no second chance.

  • Picking the wrong show. A large show full of the wrong people is worse than a small show full of your buyers. Check the visitor list and ask other exhibitors before you book.
  • A cluttered booth. Too many products, too much text and no clear message make visitors walk past. Show a few strong products, keep the booth tidy, and make the message readable from a few steps away.
  • Staff on their phones. Visitors do not approach a booth where the staff are looking at their phones or chatting to each other. Stand, smile, and greet people as they pass.
  • No lead capture. A conversation without a note is a lead lost. Record the name, the business, what they wanted, and the next step, for every serious visitor.
  • No follow-up. Most exhibitors follow up late or not at all, and the buyers they met buy from someone who did. Follow up within a few days, while the show is still fresh.
  • Underestimating the cost. The space is only the start. Travel, accommodation, booth building, samples and printed material add up, and a budget that covers only the space will run out halfway.

The pattern behind every mistake is the same: the show was treated as a few days of standing at a booth, instead of a campaign that starts months before and ends weeks after.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist for every show, from the first booking to the last follow-up, and keep it with the show file.

  • Choose the show: check the visitor list, the exhibitor list, and the cost, and ask other exhibitors.
  • Book the space early, and get a written quote listing every charge.
  • Set one clear goal for the show: orders, new buyers, a product launch, or market research.
  • Plan the booth: what to show, how to display it, and the message a visitor reads first.
  • Order samples, printed material and business cards early.
  • Book travel and accommodation for the staff, and confirm the setup times.
  • Brief the staff: products, prices, who greets, who records leads.
  • Pack a booth kit: samples, price list, cards, a lead form, chargers, and tools to fix a display.
  • On the day, keep the booth tidy, greet visitors, and record every serious lead.
  • After the show, follow up within a few days, starting with the hottest leads.
  • Review the show: count the leads and the orders, and compare them with the cost.
  • Decide whether to exhibit again next year, and book early if the answer is yes.

The checklist takes an hour to work through at the start and a few minutes a day during the show. It is the difference between a show you control and a show that happens to you.

Tips from real practice

Set one goal for the show, and let it shape every decision. A show for orders needs price lists and order forms. A show for new buyers needs a strong display and a good lead form. A show for a product launch needs the new product front and centre. One goal keeps the booth focused, and a booth with one clear purpose attracts the right visitors.

Brief your staff every morning of the show. Five minutes on what happened yesterday, what to watch for today, and who does what, keeps the booth sharp for the whole show. Tired staff who have not been briefed drift, and a drifting booth loses buyers. Rotate the staff so nobody stands all day, and keep water and snacks behind the booth.

Finally, measure the result. Count the leads, count the orders, and compare them with the total cost of the show. The numbers tell you whether to exhibit again, whether to take a bigger booth or a smaller one, and which show deserves your budget next year. A show without measurement is a guess, and a guess is a poor basis for next year's budget.

Questions people ask

Should my business exhibit at a trade show?

Exhibiting suits businesses that sell to other businesses and need to meet buyers face to face, show samples, or launch a product. It is a poor fit if your buyers do not attend shows, or if the cost of one show would use up your whole marketing budget. Visit a show as a guest first, and exhibit only if the buyers there are the buyers you need.

How do I pick the right trade show?

Look for a show where your buyers already go, and check the visitor list, the exhibitor list and the size of the show before you book. Ask other exhibitors what they thought of it, and compare the cost against the number and quality of visitors. A small show full of your buyers beats a large show full of the wrong people.

How much does exhibiting usually cost?

The cost varies widely: the space hire is the largest part, and booth building, travel, accommodation, samples and printed material add to it. A small shell-scheme booth at a local show costs far less than a large custom stand at an international show. Always get a written quote from the organiser that lists every charge, and budget for the extras as well as the space.

What should I bring to a trade show?

Bring samples of your best products, a price list, business cards, and a simple way to record who visited your booth and what they wanted. Bring enough stock for the whole show, plus the tools to fix a broken display and the chargers for your devices. Pack a small kit for yourself as well: water, comfortable shoes and a phone charger.

How do I talk to buyers at a booth?

Stand where visitors can see you, greet them with a question about what they are looking for, and listen before you start selling. Show the product, explain what makes it different in a few sentences, and ask what they would need to place an order. Take notes on every serious conversation, and agree the next step before they walk away.

What should I do after the show ends?

Follow up within a few days, while buyers still remember your booth: send the information you promised, answer the questions they asked, and suggest the next step. Sort your leads into hot, warm and cold, and work through them in that order. Finally, review the show: count the leads and the orders, compare them with the cost, and decide whether to exhibit again next year.
