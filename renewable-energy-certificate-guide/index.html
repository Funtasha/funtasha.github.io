Renewable Energy Certificate Guide

A renewable energy certificate is a document that proves one unit of electricity came from a renewable source, such as wind, solar or water power. This guide explains how certificates are created, who buys them, and what they can and cannot prove about green power.

This page is general information only, not investment advice. It sells no certificates and shows no live market data, and certificate prices and rules vary by scheme and by country, so always check the official scheme documents before acting.

What this is and who needs it

A renewable energy certificate is a record that proves one unit of electricity was generated from a renewable source, such as wind, solar, water or biomass. One certificate usually stands for one megawatt-hour of renewable electricity, which is one thousand kilowatt-hours. The certificate is separate from the electricity itself: the power goes into the grid and is used like any other power, while the certificate travels on its own as proof of where that power came from.

This guide is for anyone who has seen the words green power or renewable energy on a bill or a company report and wondered what stands behind them. That includes business owners who want to report green power honestly, households choosing a green tariff, and anyone curious about how the market for green electricity works. You do not need a background in energy to follow it.

The guide explains how a certificate is created, how it moves from a generator to a buyer, and how it is retired so it cannot be claimed twice. It also explains the common misunderstandings, because certificates are easy to get wrong, and a wrong claim about green power is worse than no claim at all.

How it works, step by step

A certificate begins at a renewable power station, such as a wind farm or a solar park. The generator produces electricity, and a meter records exactly how much was produced and when. That metered amount is the basis for everything that follows: no power produced means no certificate issued, and the amount on the certificate always matches the amount the meter recorded.

  1. The generator produces renewable electricity, and the output is metered and recorded.
  2. The registry issues certificates to match the metered output, usually one certificate per megawatt-hour. Each certificate carries details of the generator, the energy source and the time of production.
  3. The certificates are sold, either with the electricity or separately, to suppliers, businesses or traders. The price depends on the scheme, the source and the market at the time.
  4. The buyer holds the certificates until it wants to make a green power claim, for a tariff or a report.
  5. To make the claim, the buyer retires the certificate in the registry. Retirement is permanent: a retired certificate cannot be sold or claimed again, which stops the same unit of green power being counted twice.

The registry is the heart of the system. It is the official record that tracks every certificate from issue to retirement, and it is what makes the claim trustworthy. Without a registry, a certificate would be only a piece of paper.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

The cost of a certificate is not fixed: it varies by scheme, by energy source, by country and by the market at the time, so this guide quotes no price as fact. Households rarely buy certificates directly at all. A household usually buys green power through a green tariff from its supplier, and the supplier holds or retires certificates to match the tariff. The price the household pays is the tariff price, and the certificate cost is inside it.

Understanding the system takes an afternoon of reading, not a course. The time goes into the details that matter for your own situation: which scheme your supplier uses, what the tariff actually promises, and whether the certificates are retired on your behalf. A business that wants to report green power should allow more time, because the reporting rules and the paperwork take longer.

Before you start, gather three things: the name of the scheme, the name of the registry, and the terms of the tariff or contract you are looking at. With those three, you can check whether the certificates exist, whether they are retired, and what the claim really covers. Without them, you are taking someone else's word for it.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

Certificates are simple in principle and easy to misunderstand in practice. The mistakes below are the ones that cause the most trouble, and each one is avoidable.

  • Thinking the exact electricity in your socket is green. Electricity from every source mixes in the grid, so no socket receives only green power. A certificate proves that a matching amount of green power was generated somewhere, not that your own electrons came from a wind farm.
  • Counting the same unit twice. If a certificate is sold and also claimed by the generator, the same megawatt-hour is counted twice. Retirement in the registry is what prevents this, so always check that the certificate was retired.
  • Confusing a certificate with a carbon credit. A certificate proves renewable electricity was generated. A carbon credit represents a reduction in greenhouse gas emissions. They are different tools, and one cannot replace the other.
  • Assuming every green tariff is equal. Some tariffs are backed by retired certificates from named generators, and others are backed by vague promises. Read the terms and ask which scheme the certificates come from.
  • Treating a certificate as an investment. Certificates are a way to prove green power, not a way to make money. Their prices move, the rules change, and buying them to speculate is a risk this guide does not recommend.

The test for any green power claim is simple: which scheme, which registry, and was the certificate retired. If those three questions have clear answers, the claim can be checked. If they do not, the claim is only words.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist before you buy a green tariff or make a green power claim. Work through it in order, and keep the answers with your records.

  • Find the name of the scheme the certificates come from, and confirm it is a real, official scheme.
  • Find the name of the registry, and check that certificates are tracked and retired there.
  • Ask the supplier whether the certificates are retired on your behalf, and how you can verify it.
  • Read the tariff terms and note exactly what is promised: green power for all of your usage, or only part of it.
  • Check the energy sources named: wind, solar, water, biomass, or a mix.
  • Ask what happens if the supplier changes scheme or source during the contract.
  • Keep a copy of the terms, the scheme name and the registry name together.
  • If you report green power for a business, note the reporting rules that apply to you before you make the claim.
  • Compare two or three tariffs on the same points, so the comparison is fair.
  • If any answer is vague or missing, ask again in writing before you commit.

The checklist takes under an hour and saves far more time later. A green power claim that can be checked is worth far more than one that cannot, whether it is for your home or your business.

Tips from real practice

Always ask for the scheme and the registry by name. A supplier that names both is usually a supplier that can prove its claim, and a supplier that will not name them is a supplier to question. The names take a minute to ask for and a minute to check, and they are the difference between a real green tariff and a green-sounding one.

Remember that the certificate is only one part of a green power system. The other parts are the renewable generators themselves, the grid that carries the power, and the demand that makes building more generators worthwhile. Certificates help by paying generators extra income, but they do not build wind farms on their own, and a claim that suggests otherwise is overstating the case.

Finally, keep the paperwork. Green power claims are checked more often than they used to be, by customers, by auditors and by regulators, and the proof is the scheme name, the registry record and the retirement. A claim with paperwork behind it stands up. A claim without it does not.

Schemes change over time, and a certificate that counted last year may follow different rules this year. Check the scheme documents once a year, and ask your supplier about any change that affects your tariff. A claim that was true last year is only still true if the scheme and the retirement behind it have not changed.

Questions people ask

What is a renewable energy certificate?

A renewable energy certificate is a record that proves one unit of electricity was generated from a renewable source, such as wind, solar, water or biomass. One certificate usually stands for one megawatt-hour of renewable electricity. The certificate is separate from the electricity itself, so it can be sold and tracked on its own.

How does a certificate prove power is green?

When a renewable generator produces power, the amount is metered and a matching number of certificates is issued in an official registry. Each certificate carries details of the generator and the time of production. When a buyer wants to claim that green power, the certificate is retired in the registry, which stops it being sold or claimed again.

Who buys renewable certificates?

The main buyers are electricity suppliers who offer green tariffs, and businesses that want to report that the power they use is renewable. Some large buyers purchase directly, while smaller ones buy through a supplier or a broker. Households rarely buy certificates directly; they usually buy a green tariff from a supplier.

Can a household buy green certificates?

In most places a household buys green power through a green tariff from its supplier, rather than buying certificates directly. The supplier holds or retires certificates to match the tariff. Ask the supplier which scheme the certificates come from and whether they are retired on your behalf.

What is the difference between a certificate and a carbon credit?

A renewable certificate proves that a unit of electricity came from a renewable source. A carbon credit represents a reduction in greenhouse gas emissions, usually one tonne, from a project such as planting trees or capturing methane. They are different tools for different claims, and one cannot replace the other.

Do certificates really help the climate?

Certificates help by paying renewable generators extra income beyond the electricity price, which supports more renewable projects. Critics point out that a certificate does not by itself build a new wind farm, and that the same renewable power might have been generated anyway. The honest view is that certificates are one part of a green power system, not a complete climate solution on their own.
