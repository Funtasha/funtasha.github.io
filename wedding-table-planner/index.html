Wedding Table Planner

Enter your guest names, the number of tables, and the seats per table, and this planner seats everyone evenly in seconds. You can shuffle the tables, move any guest by hand, and print the finished list — all in your browser, with nothing uploaded.

How the seating works

Enter your guest names one per line, say how many tables you have and how many seats fit at each one, then press Assign seats. The planner deals guests out evenly across the tables, one at a time in turn, so the tables end up as equal as possible — no table carries far more guests than another. If you have more guests than seats, the planner says so plainly and asks you to add a table or more seats per table, instead of squeezing anyone in.

Shuffle and manual moves

Not happy with the first arrangement? Shuffle deals the same guests out again in a new random order, keeping the tables even. For finer control, pick any guest and move them to another table with a free seat — useful when you want families together, or when two guests would get on better apart. Every change updates the table list straight away, and you can shuffle and move as often as you like until the plan feels right.

This planner only arranges names into tables — it cannot know who gets on well together, so check the plan yourself and move anyone who needs a different seat.

Planning the day

Once the tables look right, print the list and keep a copy with your seating plan — it makes the day much smoother when helpers can check who sits where. Leave a couple of spare seats if you can, in case a guest brings a plus-one you did not expect. Everything on this page runs in your browser: your guest list is never uploaded anywhere, and nothing is saved when you close the page, so print or copy the list before you leave.

How to group your guests

The first step in seating is not tables, it is groups. Before you assign anyone a seat, sort your guests into the groups they already belong to: families, circles of friends, work colleagues, and neighbours. Most guests are happiest sitting with people they already know, so these natural groups are the building blocks of your seating plan. A table of people who know each other will look after itself, while a table of strangers needs careful thought to work.

Once you have your groups, think about size. A table that seats eight can hold one family of six plus a couple, or two groups of four friends. Groups that are bigger than a table need to be split, and groups that are smaller need to be joined with someone compatible. The planner helps here: it deals guests out evenly, and then you can move people by hand until the groups sit together. A little shuffling at this stage saves a lot of awkwardness on the day.

Finally, think about the mix. A table of all one age group or all one family can feel closed off, while a table with a little variety, a couple of generations, a few different circles, often has the liveliest conversation. The best tables are the ones where everyone has at least one person they know well and one person they might enjoy meeting. Aim for that, and the room will take care of itself.

Seating etiquette that keeps everyone comfortable

A few courtesies make the seating plan work for everyone. Seat older guests and guests who may need a rest near the edge of the room, close to the door and away from the speakers, so they can slip out easily and hear the conversation. Seat children with their parents or with a family table, not on a separate table of their own, unless the children are old enough to enjoy it and the parents agree. And always seat couples and close partners together: splitting them is the one seating mistake guests remember.

The top table has its own customs, and they vary from family to family. Traditionally the couple sits with their closest family, but many couples now choose a table of their best friends, or sit with both sets of parents. There is no single right answer: the top table should hold the people the couple most wants beside them. Whatever you choose, tell the people involved in advance, so nobody is surprised on the day.

One more courtesy: never seat a guest alone among strangers. Every guest should have at least one familiar face at their table, and ideally someone they can talk to easily. If you have a guest who knows nobody else at the wedding, a colleague, a plus-one, seat them with the friendliest table you have, and tell the couple to introduce them early. A small kindness like that is what turns a good wedding into a warm one.

Tricky seating and how to handle it

Every wedding has a seating puzzle or two. Former partners, family members who do not get on, and guests with strong opinions about where they belong can all make the plan harder. The golden rule is distance with dignity: seat people who need space from each other at different tables, far enough apart that they need not interact, but never make the separation obvious. Nobody should feel banished, and nobody should feel on display.

Plus-ones are another common puzzle. If you can, leave a couple of spare seats in the plan so that an unexpected plus-one has somewhere to sit without a last-minute reshuffle. When you know about a plus-one in advance, seat the couple together at a table where at least one of them knows someone. A plus-one who knows nobody is a guest who will have a long, quiet evening, and a little planning prevents that.

When a seating problem has no perfect answer, choose the option that protects the most people. A guest who is slightly inconvenienced is better than a guest who is upset, and a table that is slightly uneven is better than a table where two people spend the evening avoiding each other. The planner lets you try as many arrangements as you like: shuffle, move, shuffle again, so take your time, and when the plan feels as kind as it can be, print it and stop worrying.

Questions people ask

How do I enter my guest list?

Type one guest name per line in the box, then enter your number of tables and seats per table, and press Assign seats.

How are guests shared between tables?

Guests are dealt out evenly, one at a time in turn, so the tables end up as equal as possible and no table carries far more guests than another.

What if I have more guests than seats?

The planner tells you plainly how many guests and seats you have, and asks you to add a table or more seats per table before it assigns anyone.

Can I move a guest to a different table?

Yes — pick the guest and the table you want, then press Move guest. A guest can only move to a table with a free seat.

Is my guest list uploaded anywhere?

No — everything runs in your browser, your guest list is never sent anywhere, and nothing is saved when you close the page, so print the list before you leave.

When should I finalise the seating plan?

Once the guest list is confirmed and you know the number of tables and seats. Build a first version early, then adjust it as replies come in, and print the final list a day or two before the wedding.
