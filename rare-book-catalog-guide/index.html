Rare Book Catalog Guide

A good catalog turns a pile of old books into a collection you can actually manage, insure and enjoy. This guide explains what makes a book rare, how to record edition and condition, how to store books safely, and gives you a catalog template you can copy and use.

What this is and who needs it

A rare book is a book that is scarce and wanted: few copies survive, and collectors, libraries or readers are looking for them. Age alone does not make a book rare. Millions of books were printed in the nineteenth century, and most of them are common and cheap today. What makes a book rare is a mix of scarcity, demand, edition, condition and association — a first edition of an important work, a small print run, a copy signed by the author, or a book that survives in far fewer copies than were printed.

This guide is for anyone with old or unusual books who wants to keep them in order. You may have inherited a shelf of family books and want to know what you have. You may be starting a collection and want to record each book properly from the beginning. You may need a list for insurance, or for deciding what to sell, or simply to stop buying the same book twice. A catalog serves all of those purposes, and it also deepens your enjoyment: a collection you can search and browse on paper is a collection you understand.

A catalog is a working record, not a display. It lists what each book is, which edition and printing it is, what condition it is in, where it came from and what you know about its value. It does not need to be fancy: a notebook, a spreadsheet or a simple list will do, as long as every book is recorded the same way. This guide gives you a template and shows you how to fill it in, so that your catalog is useful to you, to an insurer and to a dealer.

How to catalog a rare book, step by step

Catalog one book at a time, with the book in your hands and a clean, flat surface to work on. Rushing through a shelf leads to mistakes that are hard to find later, so set aside an hour, take the books down in small batches, and record each one before you move on.

  1. Record the basics. Write down the author, the full title, the publisher, the place of publication and the year, exactly as they appear on the title page. Copy the wording carefully: a changed word or a missing subtitle can be the difference between one edition and another.
  2. Identify the edition and printing. Turn to the copyright page, usually on the back of the title page. Look for a statement such as first edition, a number line, or a printing history. Number lines vary by publisher and by country, so check the pattern for that publisher rather than guessing. A bibliography for the author or the subject will tell you the points of issue — the small details that separate one printing from another.
  3. Describe the binding and format. Note whether the book is in hard covers or paper, what the binding is made of, and its size. Note the number of pages, and whether there are illustrations, maps or plates. Count the plates: a missing plate is a real fault and it affects value.
  4. Grade the condition honestly. Use the standard words booksellers use: fine, very good, good, fair and poor, and add notes for anything specific — a torn page, a loose hinge, foxing, a stain, a previous owner's name on the endpaper. Condition is the factor collectors argue about most, so be precise and be honest.
  5. Note the dust jacket. For twentieth-century books the dust jacket often matters as much as the book itself. Record whether the jacket is present, and its condition: chips, tears, fading, price-clipped corners. A first edition without its jacket is a different book, in value terms, from the same edition with a fine jacket.
  6. Record provenance and value notes. Write down where the book came from, what you paid for it, and any valuation you have had, with the date and the name of the valuer. Keep the receipt. Never write a price in the book itself, and never quote a value as fact: values change, and only a qualified dealer can value a book properly.

When every book has a record, your catalog can answer real questions: what do I own, what is it worth insuring, what am I missing, and what should I sell. That is the point of the work, and it is why a catalog kept up to date is worth more than a catalog written once and forgotten.

The example entries in the right-hand column are there to show the shape of an entry, not to describe a real copy. Your own entries should be plain and factual: what the book is, what state it is in, and where your information came from. Leave a field blank rather than guessing, and come back to it when you have checked. A catalog full of guesses is worse than a catalog with gaps, because a wrong edition statement can mislead a buyer, an insurer and you.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Cataloging costs very little in money and a fair amount in time. You need a clean table, good light, a pencil for notes, and a notebook or a spreadsheet. A magnifying glass helps for reading small print on the copyright page, and a soft cloth is useful for dusting a book before you handle it. Do not use water, polish or cleaning products on old books, and never write in a book to mark it: use a slip of acid-free paper tucked inside the cover if you need a bookmark or a note.

Allow about ten minutes per book when you are starting out, and more for books that need checking against a bibliography. A shelf of fifty books is an afternoon's work, not a weekend's, but a large collection will take several sessions. Work in batches, and put each book back on the shelf as you finish it, so that a half-finished catalog never leaves your books in piles on the floor.

If you want a value for insurance or for a sale, budget for a professional opinion. A qualified rare book dealer or an auction house with a book specialist can value a collection, and a written valuation usually carries a fee. Bring the catalog with you: a dealer who can see a clear, honest record will work faster and give you a better answer than one faced with a pile of unlisted books.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

Old books are forgiving of careful handling and unforgiving of carelessness. The mistakes below are the ones that damage books and catalogs most often.

  • Shelving too tightly or too loosely. Books packed so tight that you have to force one out will tear spines and hinges. Books left leaning at an angle will warp. Shelve books upright, snug enough to stand straight, with room to slide one out easily.
  • Pulling books by the headcap. Tugging a book from the shelf by the top of its spine breaks the headcap, the most fragile part of the binding. Push the books on either side back slightly and take the book out by its middle.
  • Using sticky tape or glue. Tape stains, yellows and cannot be removed without damage. Never tape a torn page or a loose spine. A torn page should be left alone, or shown to a conservator if the book matters.
  • Writing in books. Notes, prices and underlining in a rare book reduce its value and annoy the next owner. Keep your notes in the catalog, not on the endpapers.
  • Storing books in damp or sunny places. Damp breeds mould and foxing; sunlight fades cloth and leather. Attics and basements are the worst rooms in a house for books. A cool, dry, shaded room with steady conditions is what books need.
  • Guessing at editions. A book that says first edition on the copyright page is not always a first edition, and a book with no statement may still be one. Check the number line, check a bibliography, and ask a dealer when it matters.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist each time you catalog a batch of books, and once a year when you check the collection.

  • Clear a clean table and wash and dry your hands before handling books.
  • Work in small batches, and return each book to its shelf when done.
  • Record author, title, publisher, place and year from the title page.
  • Check the copyright page for edition, printing and number line.
  • Count the pages and plates, and note any missing.
  • Grade the condition honestly and note every fault you find.
  • Record the dust jacket: present or absent, and its condition.
  • Note where the book came from and what you paid for it.
  • Check the shelves: upright, snug, out of direct sun, dry and cool.
  • Look for insects, mould or damp, and act at once if you find any.
  • Keep a backup copy of your catalog away from the books themselves.

A yearly check takes less than an hour for a small collection, and it catches problems while they are still small: a damp patch, a leaning shelf, a jacket starting to tear. Books that are checked stay in good condition; books that are forgotten do not.

Tips from real practice

Collect what you love, and learn it deeply. A collector who knows one author, one period or one subject well will spot a bargain and avoid a fake far more reliably than a collector who buys a little of everything. Read the bibliographies, visit the rare book rooms of libraries, and handle as many books as you can: the feel of a real first edition is something you learn with your hands.

Buy the best copy you can afford, and be patient. A fine copy of a book you want is worth waiting for, and it will always be easier to sell than a poor copy bought in a hurry. When you buy, ask the dealer questions: a good dealer will tell you the faults as well as the virtues, and will welcome a buyer who asks. Keep every receipt and every dealer's description with your catalog.

Finally, share the books. A collection that is never opened is a store, not a library. Read your books with clean hands and a careful touch, lend them only to people you trust, and consider leaving a note in your catalog about which books matter most to you and why. The next owner will be glad of it.

What this page cannot do

This page teaches you how to catalog and care for rare books. It lists no books for sale, it names no dealers, and it cannot value your books or tell you whether a particular copy is a first edition. Values change with the market, and only a qualified rare book dealer who has seen the book can give you a real valuation.

For a value, take the book, or clear photographs of the title page, copyright page, binding and any faults, to a qualified dealer or an auction house with a book specialist. Keep the written valuation with your catalog, and have it updated from time to time if the collection is insured.

Questions people ask

What makes a book rare?

Scarcity plus demand. A book is rare when few copies survive and collectors or libraries want them: a first edition of an important work, a small print run, a copy signed by the author, or a book that survives in far fewer copies than were printed. Age alone does not make a book rare, and many old books are common and cheap.

How do I tell a first edition?

Check the copyright page, usually on the back of the title page, for a first edition statement, a number line or a printing history. The rules vary by publisher and by country, so check the pattern for that publisher and, when it matters, check a bibliography for the author. A dealer can confirm a doubtful case.

How should rare books be stored?

Upright on a shelf, snug enough to stand straight, in a cool, dry room out of direct sunlight, away from damp, heat and dust. Avoid attics and basements. Never pull a book from the shelf by the top of its spine, and never use sticky tape, glue or rubber bands on a book.

What details go in a book catalog?

Author, full title, publisher, place and year, edition and printing, binding and format, condition with specific faults, dust jacket, provenance, and notes on plates, maps or inscriptions. Add a value note with the valuation date and the valuer's name, but never write a price inside the book itself.

Where can I get a rare book valued?

From a qualified rare book dealer or an auction house with a book specialist. Bring the book, or clear photographs of the title page, copyright page, binding and any faults, together with your catalog record. A written valuation usually carries a fee, so ask what it covers before you agree.

Should I insure a rare book collection?

If the collection has real value, yes. Talk to your insurer, keep the catalog and any written valuations with the policy, and have the valuations updated from time to time. Photograph the most valuable books and keep a copy of the catalog away from the books themselves.
