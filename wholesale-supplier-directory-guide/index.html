Wholesale Supplier Guide

A wholesale supplier sells goods in bulk at a lower price per unit, so a shop can resell them at a profit. This guide explains where to look for suppliers, how to check that one is real, and how to place a first order without risking your money.

This page lists no suppliers and names no companies. Always verify a supplier yourself, start with a small order, and never pay the full amount in advance to a supplier you have not checked.

What this is and who needs it

A wholesale supplier sells goods in bulk, at a lower price per unit than a shop charges its customers, so the shop can resell them at a profit. The supplier may be a manufacturer selling directly, a distributor who buys from manufacturers, or an importer who brings goods in from abroad. What they have in common is volume: they sell in quantities, not one at a time, and the price per unit falls as the quantity rises.

This guide is for shop owners, market traders and online resellers who need stock they can rely on. It is also for people starting a shop for the first time, who need to know where suppliers are found and how to tell a real one from a fake. The guide names no companies and lists no suppliers, because the right supplier depends on what you sell, where you are, and how much you buy.

The guide explains where to look for suppliers, how to check that one is real, what to ask before ordering, how to pay safely, and the warning signs that mean you should walk away. A good supplier is a long-term partner, and finding one is worth doing carefully.

How it works, step by step

Finding a supplier follows a clear order, and rushing any step costs money later. Start by deciding exactly what you want to sell, in what quantities, and how often you will reorder. A clear product list makes every conversation with a supplier shorter and more useful, because you know what you are asking for and what you will pay.

  1. Search in several places. Trade directories, trade shows, industry associations and referrals from other shop owners are the usual starting points. Many suppliers have their own websites and take enquiries online.
  2. Shortlist two or three suppliers, and compare them on the same points: price, minimum order, delivery time, and payment terms.
  3. Check each one. Confirm a real address and a working phone number, search for reviews from other buyers, and ask how long the business has been trading.
  4. Request samples. A sample shows the real quality, which no photo or description can prove. Pay for the sample if you must; it is the cheapest insurance you will buy.
  5. Negotiate the terms. Agree the price per unit at your quantity, the delivery time, the payment terms and the returns policy, and get them in writing.
  6. Place a small first order. Order the smallest quantity allowed, check the quality and the delivery, and only then order more.

Through every step, keep notes on each supplier: what was promised, what was delivered, and how quickly they answered. The notes become the record you judge them by.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

The cost of wholesale stock is not only the price per unit. There is the minimum order quantity, which sets how much you must buy at once, the shipping cost, which can be a large part of a small order, and the cost of samples. Some suppliers charge for samples and take the cost off the first order, and some do not. Ask about every cost before you order, and work out the total cost per unit including shipping, because that is the number your selling price must cover.

Time matters too. A first order usually takes days to weeks from enquiry to delivery, depending on the supplier, the stock and the distance. Samples add time at the start but save far more time later, because they catch quality problems before a full order. Plan your first order well before you need the stock, and do not promise customers a date until the supplier has confirmed delivery.

Before you start, gather what a supplier will ask for: your business details, a clear list of the products you want, the quantities, and where the goods should be delivered. You also need storage space for the stock when it arrives, and a budget that covers the order, the shipping and a margin for anything going wrong. A first order that uses all of your money leaves nothing for the next one.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

New buyers make the same mistakes with suppliers, and the mistakes are expensive because they involve stock that cannot be sold or money that cannot be recovered.

  • Ordering big the first time. A large first order from an untested supplier risks the whole budget on quality you have not seen. Start small, test the goods and the supplier, and order more once the first order has gone smoothly.
  • Skipping samples. Photos and descriptions do not show quality, size or colour accurately. A sample is the only way to know what you are buying, and skipping it is the most common cause of unsellable stock.
  • No written terms. An agreement made only in conversation is hard to prove when something goes wrong. Get the price, the quantity, the delivery time, the payment terms and the returns policy in writing before you pay.
  • Paying in full in advance. A new supplier who demands full payment before shipping, by a method with no buyer protection, is a risk. A deposit with the balance on delivery, or payment through a trusted platform, is safer.
  • Ignoring delivery times. A supplier who delivers late leaves your shelves empty and your customers waiting. Ask about delivery time before you order, and check it against the first delivery.
  • Relying on one supplier. A single supplier who fails, closes or raises prices leaves you with no stock and no alternative. Keep a second supplier for your most important lines, even if you order from them rarely.

Every one of these mistakes is a shortcut taken to save time or money. The shortcut costs more than the careful route, every time.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist for every new supplier, and keep it with the supplier's file until the relationship is proven.

  • Write down exactly what you want to buy, in what quantities, and how often.
  • Find two or three suppliers from different sources, and compare them on the same points.
  • Check each supplier: real address, working phone number, website with a history, reviews from other buyers.
  • Ask for a sample, and check the quality, size and colour against what was promised.
  • Ask about the minimum order quantity, the price at different quantities, and the shipping cost.
  • Ask about delivery time, payment terms and the returns policy, and get the answers in writing.
  • Place a small first order, and check the goods carefully when they arrive.
  • Note the delivery time, the quality and the communication, and decide whether to order again.
  • Keep a record of every order, every payment and every conversation.
  • Keep a second supplier in mind for your most important lines.

The checklist takes an hour spread over a few days, and it is the difference between a supplier you trust and a supplier you hope about. Trust comes from evidence, and the checklist gathers the evidence.

Tips from real practice

Build a relationship with your supplier, because a supplier who knows you will warn you about price rises, hold stock for you, and answer the phone when something goes wrong. Pay on time, order regularly, and say when you are happy with the goods. A good relationship is worth more than a small discount from a stranger.

Track quality over time, not only on the first order. A supplier who starts well and drifts is common, and the drift is caught by checking each delivery against the sample you kept. Keep the first sample as the standard, and compare every delivery with it. If the quality falls, say so at once, in writing, while the evidence is fresh.

Finally, review your suppliers once a year. Prices change, new suppliers appear, and your own volumes grow, which may earn you better terms. A short annual review, comparing your current suppliers with one or two new ones, keeps your costs honest and your stock reliable.

When a supplier does well, say so, and when they do not, say that too, quickly and in writing. Suppliers who hear only complaints stop listening, and suppliers who hear nothing assume all is well. Honest feedback, both ways, is what turns a supplier into a partner.

Questions people ask

Where can I find wholesale suppliers?

Common places to look are trade directories, trade shows, industry associations, and referrals from other shop owners. Many suppliers also have their own websites and take enquiries online. Start with two or three sources and compare, rather than taking the first supplier you find.

How do I know if a supplier is real?

Check that the business has a real address, a working phone number and a website with a history, and search for reviews from other buyers. Ask for a sample before any large order, and be careful of suppliers who only accept payment methods with no buyer protection. A genuine supplier answers detailed questions about products, delivery and returns.

What should I ask before my first order?

Ask about the minimum order quantity, the price per unit at different quantities, delivery time, shipping cost, payment terms and the returns policy. Ask whether samples are available and how quality is checked. Get the answers in writing, so there is no argument later about what was agreed.

How much should I order the first time?

Order the smallest quantity the supplier allows, enough to test the product and the supplier without risking too much money. A small first order shows whether the quality is right, the delivery is on time, and the supplier communicates well. You can order more once the first order has gone smoothly.

How do I pay a supplier safely?

Use a payment method that leaves a record and offers some protection, and get a written invoice before you pay. Avoid paying the full amount in advance to a new supplier; a deposit with the balance on delivery, or payment through a trusted platform, is safer. Never send money by a method you cannot trace to a supplier you have not verified.

What are warning signs of a fake supplier?

Warning signs include prices far below every other supplier, pressure to pay at once, no real address or phone number, and refusal to send a sample. A supplier who only communicates through one chat app, changes bank details suddenly, or avoids written terms is also a risk. If several warning signs appear together, walk away and find another supplier.
