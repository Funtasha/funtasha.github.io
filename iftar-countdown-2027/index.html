How the iftar countdown works

The timer on this page counts down to the iftar time you enter. Pick your city from the list, set the time from your local prayer timetable, and press Start countdown. The page then shows the hours, minutes and seconds left until you break your fast, ticking live.

If the time you set has already passed today, the countdown rolls over to tomorrow automatically, and says so in the small line under the timer. Nothing breaks and nothing needs resetting: just check the time again tomorrow, because iftar moves a little each day as the sunset shifts.

Why iftar times are different everywhere

Iftar begins at sunset, and sunset is a local event. Two cities in the same country can break their fast minutes apart, and cities in different time zones can be hours apart. That is why a single fixed time can never be right for everyone, and why this page asks you to set your own.

The time also moves through the month. In February and March, when Ramadan falls in 2027, the days are lengthening in the northern hemisphere, so sunset comes a little later each evening. A timetable from the first week of Ramadan will not match the last week, so check the time for the day you are fasting, not the week you started.

Height matters too. A family on a high floor or a hilltop may see the sun a minute or two after a family at street level in the same city, and mosques allow for this in their timetables. It is one more reason to follow your local timetable rather than a time you saw on a page for another place.

Setting your time from your local timetable

Your local mosque or Islamic centre publishes a Ramadan timetable every year, usually as a printed sheet, a poster, or a page on its website. Find your city on it, read across to today's date, and enter that time here. If your mosque gives a range or a note about a safety margin, follow the mosque, not this page.

If you are travelling, remember that the timetable changes with you. A time that was right at home may be wrong in another city, even one a short drive away. When in doubt, ask at the nearest mosque, or look up the sunset time for the place where you actually are.

Breaking the fast gently

After a long day without food or water, the kindest thing you can do for your body is to break the fast slowly. A few dates and a glass of water are the traditional start, and they are a good one: a little natural sweetness, a little fluid, and time for your stomach to wake up before the main meal.

Give yourself ten or fifteen unhurried minutes before eating properly. Many families pray first and eat afterwards, which gives the body exactly that pause. Eating slowly also helps you notice when you are full, which is easy to miss when you are very hungry.

Keep the main meal balanced rather than heavy. Soup, bread, vegetables and a little protein sit better than a large fried feast, and you will feel more comfortable for the evening prayers afterwards. Save the richest dishes for the weekend, when there is time to enjoy them slowly.

A calm half hour before iftar

The half hour before iftar can be the busiest part of the day, and also the most peaceful. The table is being laid, the food is nearly ready, and everyone is waiting for the same moment. If you can, finish the cooking a little early, so the last minutes are for sitting together rather than rushing.

Some families use this time for a short prayer or a few quiet minutes of reflection. Others read together, or let the children help set out the dates and water. Whatever you do, try to keep the last ten minutes calm: the fast ends better in peace than in a scramble.

Iftar with family and guests

Iftar is at its best when it is shared. Invite a neighbour, a friend, or a relative who would otherwise eat alone, and keep the meal simple enough that hosting stays a pleasure rather than a burden. A pot of soup, some bread, and a warm welcome are more than enough.

If guests bring food, accept it graciously and put it on the table. If children are fasting for part of the day, let them break their fast with the family and praise their effort. The point of the gathering is togetherness, and a relaxed table does that better than a lavish one.

After iftar: the evening ahead

Once you have broken the fast, the evening opens up. Drink water steadily through the night rather than all at once, and keep a bottle nearby so you remember. A light walk after the meal helps many people feel comfortable, and it is a good time to visit friends or family.

Many people pray taraweeh in the evening during Ramadan, at the mosque or at home. Others read, study, or prepare suhoor for the morning. However you spend the night, leave enough time for sleep: tomorrow's fast begins early, and a rested body fasts far more easily than a tired one.

Questions people ask

Is 6:30 in the evening the real iftar time for my city?

No. The 6:30 time is only an example so the timer has something to show when the page first loads. Iftar times are different in every city and change a little each day, so you must set the time from your local prayer timetable.

Where do I find my real iftar time?

Your local mosque or Islamic centre publishes a Ramadan timetable each year, on paper or on its website. Find your city and today's date on it, and enter that time here. If you cannot find a timetable, ask at the nearest mosque.

Why does iftar time change every day?

Iftar begins at sunset, and the sunset time shifts a little each day as the seasons change. In February and March 2027 the days are lengthening in the northern hemisphere, so sunset comes slightly later each evening through Ramadan.

What happens if the time has already passed today?

The countdown rolls over to tomorrow automatically and tells you so in the line under the timer. You do not need to press anything. Just check tomorrow's time, because it may be a minute or two different from today's.

Does the countdown work on my phone?

Yes. The countdown runs entirely in your browser with no sign-up and no download, and it works on phones, tablets, and computers. Keep the page open in the kitchen and the seconds will keep ticking.

What do people usually break the fast with?

Dates and water are the traditional way to break the fast in many families, followed by a prayer and then the main meal. The habit is a gentle one: a little sweetness, a little water, and a pause before eating properly.
