What a landing page is — and what this builder does

A landing page is a single web page built around one offer and one action. A visitor arrives, reads one clear promise, and is asked to do one thing: sign up, buy, book, or download. That focus is the whole point. A page that tries to say everything ends up saying nothing, and a visitor who is unsure what to do usually does nothing at all.

This builder turns five short answers into a full landing-page outline. You type in your product, your audience, the main benefit, your offer, and the words for your button. The builder gives you three headline options, the sections of the page in order, and the button text, all written from your own answers. You can copy the whole outline and use it as the plan for a real page.

The outline is a starting point, not a finished page. Good copy comes from knowing your buyers, and no tool can do that part for you. What the builder does is save you the blank page: the structure, the order, and a first draft of the words, so you can spend your time improving them instead of staring at an empty screen.

The 5 parts every landing page needs

Every strong landing page has the same five parts, in the same order. First, a headline that states the main benefit in plain words. A visitor decides in seconds whether to keep reading, and the headline does that work. If it is vague, clever, or full of jargon, the visitor leaves.

Second, a short subhead that adds one detail: who it is for, or how it works, in one sentence. Third, the problem, stated in the visitor's own words — the frustration or wish that brought them here. When a visitor sees their own problem on the page, they trust that the page understands them.

Fourth, the offer and the proof. The offer is what they get and what it costs them (money, time, or an email address). The proof is why they should believe it: a testimonial, a result, or a guarantee. Fifth, the call to action — one button, one clear instruction, repeated at the end. Five parts, one offer, one button. That is the whole formula.

The order matters as much as the parts. A visitor who has not yet understood the benefit will not care about the offer, and a visitor who has not seen the proof will not trust the button. Each part earns the next one: the headline earns attention, the problem earns trust, the offer earns interest, the proof earns belief, and the button collects the result. Skip a part and the chain breaks — usually at the button, where it hurts most.

How to write a headline that works

A good headline says the benefit, not the product. "An online English course" is a product. "Speak English with confidence" is a benefit. The visitor does not want a course; they want the confidence. Put the benefit first and let the product support it.

Use the visitor's own words. If your buyers say "I want to speak without freezing up", your headline should sound like that — not like a textbook. Short beats long: one line a visitor can read at a glance. And be specific where you can. "Speak English with confidence in three months of daily practice" is stronger than "Speak English with confidence" alone, because it answers the silent question: how long will this take?

Write three headlines and pick the clearest, not the cleverest. Clever headlines make writers smile and visitors confused. If a stranger cannot tell what you offer from the headline alone, rewrite it until they can. The builder gives you three options so you can compare them side by side and choose the one that sounds most like your buyer.

Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

The most common mistake is three different offers on one page. A visitor sees "buy the course, join the newsletter, book a call" and does nothing, because three choices feel like work. Pick one offer and one button. Everything else can live on another page.

The second mistake is a weak headline — usually a product name, a slogan, or a vague promise like "Welcome to our website" or "Quality you can trust". A headline must say what changes for the visitor. If your headline could belong to any business in any industry, it is too weak. Rewrite it with the benefit in it.

The third mistake is a hidden button. The button should be easy to find at the top of the page and repeated after each main section. A visitor who is ready to act should never have to hunt for the way to act. One clear button, in the same words, in several places — that is not pushy, it is helpful.

A fourth mistake is writing about yourself instead of the visitor. "We are a leading provider of innovative solutions" says nothing about what changes for the person reading. Count how many sentences start with "we" and how many start with "you". If "we" wins, rewrite the page from the visitor's side: their problem, their benefit, their next step. The page is not about your business; it is about their decision.

What this builder cannot do — and what to do next

This builder cannot put a page on the internet, take payments, or collect email addresses. It makes the plan and the words; the live page is a separate step. That is honest, and it is also normal: every website starts as a plan before it becomes a page.

The next step is to take the copied outline to a website builder — the kind that lets you drag blocks onto a page without writing code — or to a developer, if you want something custom. Hand them the outline and say: "Build this page, in this order, with these words." Because the outline is already written, the build is quick and the result matches your plan.

Finally, test the page with real people. Show it to three people who match your audience and ask them what the page offers and what they would click. If they hesitate, the headline or the button needs work. A landing page is never finished — it is improved, one honest test at a time.

Questions people ask

What is a landing page?

A landing page is a single web page built around one offer and one action. A visitor arrives from an advert, a link, or a search, reads one clear promise, and is asked to do one thing — sign up, buy, book, or download. Unlike a full website, it has one job, which is why it usually converts better than a general page.

How is it different from a homepage?

A homepage introduces a whole business: many products, many links, many choices. A landing page removes all of that and focuses on one offer for one audience. A homepage is a front door; a landing page is a single room with one clear purpose. That focus is what makes landing pages work for adverts and campaigns.

What should the headline say?

The headline should say the main benefit in the visitor's own words — what changes for them, not what your product is called. Short beats long, and specific beats vague. If a stranger cannot tell what you offer from the headline alone, rewrite it until they can.

How many offers should a page have?

One. A page with three offers gives the visitor three decisions, and most visitors choose none of them. Pick the single offer that matters most for this page and give it one button. Other offers belong on other pages, where they can have the same focus.

Where should the button go?

At the top of the page, where a visitor sees it without scrolling, and again after each main section — after the offer, after the proof, and at the very end. Use the same words on every button so the visitor always knows what happens next. A visitor who is ready should never have to hunt for it.

How do I know if my page works?

Count two numbers: how many people visit, and how many take the action. The share who act is your conversion rate, and a small improvement there is worth more than more visitors. This page does not track anything for you — you measure with your website's own statistics and by asking real visitors what they understood.
