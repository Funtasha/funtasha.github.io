The 2027 tournament at a glance

The Cricket World Cup 2027 runs from Saturday 2 October to Sunday 21 November 2027, hosted by South Africa, Zimbabwe, and Namibia. Fourteen teams play fifty-seven matches across twelve venues, in the fifty-over one-day format that has defined the World Cup since it began.

It is the first Cricket World Cup in Africa since 2003, and the first ever for Namibia, which hosts matches in Windhoek. South Africa hosts eight venues, Zimbabwe three, and Namibia one, so the tournament stretches across the whole southern part of the continent, from Cape Town in the south to Victoria Falls in the north.

Australia arrive as defending champions, after beating India in the 2023 final in Ahmedabad, and they have won the tournament six times, more than any other team. India, England, New Zealand, Pakistan, and South Africa will all believe they can stop them, and the new format means more teams stay in contention for longer than ever before.

The hosts and venues

South Africa hosts eight venues: Johannesburg, Tshwane and Centurion, Cape Town, Durban, Gqeberha, Bloemfontein, Paarl, and KuGompo City. Each ground has its own character, from the high bounce of the Wanderers in Johannesburg to the sea breeze at Newlands in Cape Town, and the two semi-finals are split between Cape Town on 17 November and Centurion on 18 November.

Zimbabwe hosts three venues: Harare, Bulawayo, and Victoria Falls, where the Mosi-oa-Tunya ground sits near one of the great natural wonders of the world. Namibia hosts one venue, the Namibia Cricket Ground in Windhoek, and also hosts the opening Super Series, the round robin between the three lowest-ranked qualifiers that decides the last place in the group stage.

Twelve venues across three countries means long journeys for the teams and wonderful variety for the fans. A match in the dry heat of Bloemfontein feels very different from a match by the sea in Durban, and the teams that adapt quickest to each ground usually go furthest in the tournament.

The format, step by step

The 2027 format has four stages, and it is worth learning them before the tournament starts. First comes the Super Series: the three lowest-ranked qualifiers play a round robin in Namibia, and the winner advances to the group stage. Then comes the group stage itself: twelve teams in two groups of six, with every team in a group playing every other team once.

Then comes the Super 7: the top three teams in each group, plus the best fourth-placed team, play a single round robin of twenty-one matches. The top four teams in the Super 7 reach the semi-finals, which are played in Cape Town on 17 November and Centurion on 18 November, and the two winners meet in the final on 21 November 2027.

Add it up and the numbers work neatly: three matches in the Super Series, thirty in the group stage, twenty-one in the Super 7, two semi-finals, and one final, making fifty-seven matches in total. The format rewards consistency, because a team must finish near the top of its group and then survive the Super 7 to reach the last four.

The two groups

Group A is made up of India, Australia, Pakistan, Afghanistan, Zimbabwe, and Qualifier A, the winner of the Super Series. It is a group full of history: India and Pakistan is the biggest rivalry in the sport, Australia are the defending champions, and Afghanistan have become one of the most exciting teams in world cricket.

Group B is made up of New Zealand, South Africa, Sri Lanka, England, Bangladesh, and Qualifier B. South Africa play at home, which is always an advantage, New Zealand have reached the last two finals before 2023, and England won the tournament in 2019. Bangladesh and Sri Lanka both have the players to upset anyone on their day.

Every team plays five group matches, and the top three in each group go through to the Super 7, joined by the best fourth-placed team across the two groups. That last place keeps the group stage alive until the final round: a team in fourth place is never out of it until the last ball is bowled.

Past finals to remember

The quiz asks about past finals, so here are the four most recent ones, which are worth knowing. In 2023, Australia beat India in Ahmedabad, winning their sixth title in front of a huge home crowd that had expected an Indian win. In 2019, England beat New Zealand at Lord's in one of the most dramatic finals ever played, decided on a boundary count after the scores were tied.

In 2015, Australia beat New Zealand in Melbourne, winning their fifth title at home. In 2011, India beat Sri Lanka in Mumbai, winning their second title twenty-eight years after their first. Together, these four finals show how the tournament has changed: Australia's dominance, England's rise, New Zealand's near misses, and India's home triumphs.

Knowing the past finals helps you enjoy the new tournament more, because every match carries a little history with it. When India meet Australia in Group A, it is a repeat of the 2023 final; when England meet New Zealand in Group B, it is a repeat of the 2019 final. The stories write themselves.

How to enjoy the tournament

Fifty-seven matches over seven weeks is a lot of cricket, so pick your matches rather than trying to watch them all. The group games between the big rivals are the ones to circle first, then the Super 7 matches that decide the semi-final places, then the two semi-finals and the final. A simple wall chart, with the groups and the Super 7 table, helps you follow the story as it unfolds.

Watch with other people when you can. Cricket is a social game, and a World Cup is better shared: a family watch party, a gathering of friends, or even a group chat where everyone shares their reactions. Quizzes, predictors, fantasy teams, and bingo cards all add to the fun, and this site has a tool for each of them.

Finally, enjoy the new format. The Super Series gives smaller teams a stage, the Super 7 keeps the middle of the tournament competitive, and the best fourth-placed team rule means no group match is ever meaningless. By the time the final arrives on 21 November, you will have watched a tournament full of surprises.
