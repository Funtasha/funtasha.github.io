How to use this guide

This guide is for anyone who runs training at work and has to answer the question every manager asks: did it work? It explains the four numbers that matter — completion, scores, time, and confidence — how to read each one honestly, the five reports worth making, and what to do when the numbers point in different directions. Read it in order the first time, then use the reports section as a checklist.

One thing to be clear about from the start: this page shows no live analytics data, because it has none to show. It is a guide to reading numbers, not a dashboard. Every number in the worked examples is labelled as an example, and your real numbers live in your own training system. What this guide gives you is the method: which numbers to look at, what each one means, and which questions to ask of them.

The numbers explained: completion, scores, time, confidence

Completion is the number every training report starts with: how many people finished the course. It is easy to measure and easy to misunderstand. A completion rate of ninety-five in a hundred sounds like success, but completion only proves that people reached the end — it says nothing about what they learned on the way. Treat completion as a starting point, never as a verdict.

Scores are the second number: how well people did on the checks inside the training. A score tells you what stuck, which is what completion cannot. But a score is only as honest as the questions behind it — easy questions produce high scores that mean nothing, and a single score hides which questions were missed. Always read scores question by question, not as one number for the whole course.

Time is the third number: how long people spent. Time tells you whether the training fits the working day — a course that takes three hours will be abandoned by people who have thirty minutes — and whether people rushed. A learner who finished a one-hour course in ten minutes did not learn it in ten minutes; they skipped it. Confidence is the fourth number, and the one most reports leave out: do learners feel able to do the thing? Ask it directly after training, and treat a low answer as a training need.

The 5 reports worth making

Most training systems can produce dozens of reports, and most of them are never read. Five are worth making, and they cover almost everything a manager needs to know. The first is the completion report: who finished, who did not, and who started and stopped. The stop point matters — if most people stop at the same place, that place is the problem, not the people.

The second is the score report, question by question: which questions were missed, and by how many people. The third is the time report: how long the training took, and whether people rushed or lingered. The fourth is the confidence report: how confident learners feel, before and after training. The fifth is the follow-up report: three months later, are people still using what they learned? That last report is the hardest to make and the most honest of the five.

  • Completion report: who finished, who stopped, and where they stopped
  • Score report: which questions were missed, and by how many people
  • Time report: how long the training took, and who rushed it
  • Confidence report: how confident learners feel, before and after
  • Follow-up report: three months later, is anyone still using it?

Reading the numbers honestly

Reading numbers honestly starts with asking what each number cannot tell you. Completion cannot tell you about learning. Scores cannot tell you about skill — a learner can pass a quiz about a task and still fail at the task itself. Time cannot tell you about effort, because a slow learner may be a careful one. Confidence cannot tell you about competence, because confidence and ability do not always move together.

The second habit is to compare like with like. A completion rate of eighty in a hundred means one thing for a voluntary course and another for a compulsory one. Scores from new starters and scores from experienced staff should be read separately — mixing them hides both stories. And a single number for the whole company hides every team, every site, and every group that needs help: break the numbers down before you judge them.

The third habit is to look for the story behind the number. A sudden drop in completion may mean the course broke, the link expired, or a busy season arrived — not that the training got worse. Ask the learners before you blame the training, and ask the trainers before you blame the learners. Numbers start conversations; they do not end them.

When completion is high but scores are low

The most confusing pattern in learning numbers is also the most common: almost everyone finishes the course, and almost nobody passes the checks. The first thing to do is believe both numbers, because both are probably telling the truth — people finished, and people did not learn. The question is why.

There are three usual reasons. The first is that the course is too easy to finish and too hard to learn from: clicking through slides is not learning, and a course that asks nothing of the learner until the final test will produce finishers who fail. The second is that the checks test something the course did not teach — the questions and the content do not match. The third is that the learners had no reason to try: the course was compulsory, the score did not matter to them, and finishing was the only goal anyone set.

The fix depends on the reason. If the course asks nothing until the end, add checks through the course, not just at the finish. If the checks and the content do not match, rewrite one of them until they do. If learners had no reason to try, give them one: explain why the skill matters, show what it is for, and make the check feel like practice, not punishment. And in every case, ask the learners — they usually know exactly what went wrong.

What to do next: turning numbers into changes

Numbers that change nothing are numbers wasted. The point of reading them is to decide what to do next, and the decisions are usually small: fix the section where people stop, rewrite the question everyone misses, shorten the course that takes too long, or add practice where confidence is low. Small changes, made from real numbers, improve training faster than any redesign.

Make one change at a time, and measure it. If you change the course, the questions, and the timing all at once, you will never know which change worked. Change the weakest spot first, run the training again, and compare the new numbers with the old ones. Improvement you can measure is improvement you can defend — and it is the only kind a manager should be asked to pay for.

Finally, share the numbers with the learners. People who can see their own results — what they got right, what they missed, how the group did — learn more from the assessment than people who only see a pass or a fail. Transparency turns a score into a lesson, and a lesson into a reason to try again.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is celebrating completion while nobody learned. A full completion rate is a good headline and a bad measure: it proves people reached the end, and nothing else. A training team that reports completion alone is a team that has stopped asking whether the training works.

The second mistake is one number for the whole company. A single completion rate hides the team that finished everything and the team that finished nothing, the new starters who struggled and the experienced staff who breezed through. Break the numbers down by team, by site, and by experience, or do not report them at all.

The third mistake is buying expensive software before knowing what to measure. Software reports whatever you ask it to, and if you do not know which numbers matter, it will report the wrong ones beautifully. Decide the five reports first — completion, scores, time, confidence, follow-up — and only then ask whether your current system can produce them. Most can.

Questions people ask

What is learning analytics?

Learning analytics is the measurement of training: completion, scores, time spent, and learner confidence, read together to answer one question — did the training work? It is not the numbers themselves that matter, but what they tell you to change. This page teaches you how to read them; it shows no live data of its own.

Which numbers matter most?

Four numbers matter most: completion (who finished), scores (what stuck), time (how long it took, and who rushed), and confidence (whether learners feel able to do the thing). Completion alone proves nothing about learning, so read all four together — and always read scores question by question, not as one number.

What does completion really mean?

Completion means people reached the end of the course — nothing more. It does not mean they learned anything, understood anything, or can do anything new. A high completion rate with low scores usually means the course was easy to finish and hard to learn from. Treat completion as a starting point, never as a verdict.

How do I read the scores?

Read scores question by question: which questions were missed, and by how many people? A question everyone misses means the training did not cover it, or the question is badly written. Compare like with like — new starters and experienced staff separately — and never judge a whole company on a single number.

What if scores are low?

Believe both numbers first: people finished, and people did not learn. Then find the reason — the course may ask nothing until the final test, the checks may test something the course did not teach, or learners may have had no reason to try. Fix the cause, change one thing at a time, and ask the learners: they usually know what went wrong.

Do I need expensive software?

Usually not. Decide what to measure first — completion, scores, time, confidence, and a follow-up report — and then check whether your current training system can produce those five reports. Most can. Expensive software reports whatever you ask it to, and if you do not know which numbers matter, it will report the wrong ones beautifully.
