Drawing Practice Challenge

This challenge gives you one drawing prompt a day for thirty days, starting with easy shapes and ending with full scenes. Each week has its own theme and its own tips, and every prompt takes about fifteen minutes.

There is no right answer in this challenge and no score at the end. If a prompt feels hard, draw it badly on purpose and move on: the habit matters more than any single drawing.

How the challenge works

The challenge is simple: one drawing prompt a day, for thirty days. Each prompt takes about fifteen minutes, and each week has its own theme, so the difficulty grows gently instead of jumping. Week one is lines and shapes, the alphabet of drawing. Week two is everyday objects, drawn from life. Week three is living things, which move and bend and refuse to sit still. Week four is scenes and stories, where everything you practised comes together in one picture.

You need nothing special to start: a pencil and any paper will do. Draw in the order given if you like structure, or pick any day that catches your eye if you like freedom. The order is designed so each prompt builds on the ones before it, but the challenge works either way. There is no score, no test, and no prize for finishing, except the habit itself and a sketchbook full of proof that you showed up thirty times.

Drawing is mostly looking. Before each prompt, spend a minute just looking at your subject: its outline, its shadows, where it is wide and where it is narrow. Beginners often draw what they think a thing looks like instead of what is in front of them, and the cure is to look again, more slowly, and let the hand follow the eye. The prompts are built to teach that habit, because every one of them starts with something you can see.

Week 1: Lines and shapes (days 1 to 7)

The first week is about loosening up and learning to trust your hand. Draw slowly, look more than you draw, and let the lines be wobbly: wobbly lines drawn with attention beat straight lines drawn without it. If a prompt feels too easy, draw it bigger, or draw it twice, once fast and once slow, and compare the two.

  1. Day 1: Draw ten straight lines, then ten curved lines, and pick your smoothest one.
  2. Day 2: Draw a circle, a square and a triangle, then turn each one into a face.
  3. Day 3: Draw a cup from the side using only straight lines.
  4. Day 4: Draw a leaf, then draw the same leaf three times smaller.
  5. Day 5: Draw a staircase going up, then a staircase going down.
  6. Day 6: Draw a hand holding a pencil, using only outlines.
  7. Day 7: Draw your own shoe from above, slowly, one line at a time.

Week 2: Everyday objects (days 8 to 14)

The second week moves from shapes to things. Draw from life, not from memory: put the object in front of you, look at it properly, and draw what you see rather than what you think it looks like. A mug is not a rectangle with a handle; it has a rim, a curve, a shadow, and a weight. The prompts this week are all things you already own, so there is nothing to buy and nothing to search for.

  1. Day 8: Draw the mug you drink from, including the handle.
  2. Day 9: Draw a bunch of keys lying on a table.
  3. Day 10: Draw a pair of glasses, open, from the front.
  4. Day 11: Draw a book standing open, pages and all.
  5. Day 12: Draw a chair from the side, with all four legs on the ground.
  6. Day 13: Draw a lamp, and shade the side away from the light.
  7. Day 14: Draw your breakfast plate before you eat it.

Week 3: Living things (days 15 to 21)

Living things are harder than objects, because they have gesture: the curve of a back, the tilt of a head, the stretch of a branch. This week is about capturing that gesture in a few lines before adding detail. Start each drawing with the biggest shape first, a sweeping line for the spine or the trunk, and add the smaller parts after. If the animal moves, and it will, draw the pose you remember rather than chasing the live one.

  1. Day 15: Draw a cat sitting, in five lines or fewer.
  2. Day 16: Draw a tree with roots, trunk and branches.
  3. Day 17: Draw a bird in flight, wings wide.
  4. Day 18: Draw a fish, then add water, bubbles and a stone.
  5. Day 19: Draw a flower with six petals and a bent stem.
  6. Day 20: Draw a dog lying down, asleep.
  7. Day 21: Draw a person walking, caught mid-step.

Week 4: Scenes and stories (days 22 to 30)

The last week puts it all together. A scene is a drawing with a near and a far, a foreground and a background, and something happening in it. Start with the horizon or the biggest shape, place the main subject, then add the details that make the scene yours. These prompts take a little longer than the early ones, and that is fine: by now your hand is warmed up and your eye is faster. Day 30 looks back at the whole challenge, so save it for last if you can.

  1. Day 22: Draw the view from your window, near and far.
  2. Day 23: Draw a street with three buildings and one tree.
  3. Day 24: Draw a market stall with fruit piled high.
  4. Day 25: Draw a beach with a boat, a bucket and footprints.
  5. Day 26: Draw a bedroom at night, with the moon in the window.
  6. Day 27: Draw a picnic on a hill, with a blanket and a basket.
  7. Day 28: Draw a rainy day, with umbrellas and puddles.
  8. Day 29: Draw your favourite place in the world, from memory.
  9. Day 30: Draw yourself drawing, with your tools around you.

Tips for finishing all thirty days

Pick a time and keep it. Fifteen minutes after breakfast, or before bed, or on the bus: the time matters less than the repetition. Keep your supplies where you can see them, because a pencil in a drawer is a prompt postponed. Draw small when you are busy: a tiny drawing still counts, and a small page is less frightening than a big blank one. If a prompt feels hard, draw it badly on purpose and move on. A bad drawing finished beats a perfect drawing never started.

Missed a day is not a failed challenge. Just do the next prompt the next day, or do two prompts if you feel like it. Sharing helps some people: showing a friend, or posting a photo, adds a gentle pressure to keep going. Keeping the drawings private helps others. Either way, date each drawing, and look back at day one when you reach day thirty. The difference will surprise you, because progress in drawing is slow day to day and obvious month to month.

What to draw with

A pencil and paper are enough for the whole challenge. A soft pencil, the kind marked B or 2B, gives darker lines with less pressure than a hard HB, and a rubber is useful but not required: many drawers improve faster when they leave the mistakes in and draw the correction beside them. If you want colour, coloured pencils or a few felt pens are plenty. Charcoal is fun for the week-four scenes, but it smudges, so keep finished pages apart or slip paper between them.

Do not buy expensive kit to start. A sketchbook helps, because it keeps the thirty drawings together in order, but loose sheets in a folder work just as well. The challenge is about looking and drawing, not about supplies, and the best tool is the one already in your hand.

Date every drawing, even the ones you dislike. Dates turn a pile of paper into a record, and the record is what shows your progress when the daily change feels too small to notice. When you reach day thirty, spread the drawings out in order and look at the first and the last side by side. The line is steadier, the shapes are surer, and the scenes have a confidence the early shapes did not. That is what thirty days of looking and drawing buys, and it costs nothing but fifteen minutes a day.

Questions people ask

How long does each prompt take?

About fifteen minutes each. The early prompts are quicker and the week-four scenes take a little longer, but none of them needs more than half an hour. If you enjoy a prompt, take longer: the time is a guide, not a limit.

What if I miss a day?

Just continue with the next prompt the next day. The challenge is a habit, not a test, and a missed day does not cancel the days you already did. You can also do two prompts in one day if you want to catch up.

Do I need to draw well to start?

No. The challenge is built for beginners, and the first week starts with lines and shapes on purpose. Drawing well is the result of the challenge, not the requirement for starting it.

Can I use colour?

Yes. Start in pencil so you can focus on shapes, and add colour whenever you like. Coloured pencils, pens, or paint all work, and the prompts do not depend on any particular medium.

Should I share my drawings?

That is your choice. Sharing helps some people stay going, because a friend watching adds gentle pressure. Keeping the drawings private helps others, because there is no audience to worry about. Either way, date each drawing so you can see your progress.

What do I do after day 30?

Repeat your favourite prompts, set your own prompts for another thirty days, or start a sketchbook habit of one drawing a day. The challenge ends, but the habit does not have to: thirty days is enough to prove you can keep going.
