Document Preservation Guide

Old letters, certificates and photographs fade and crumble when they are stored badly, but a few simple habits can keep them safe for decades. This guide explains what damages paper, how to store and handle fragile pages, how to scan them safely, and what to leave to a professional conservator.

What this is and who needs it

Old documents are fragile in ways that are easy to miss. A letter kept in a damp drawer grows mould. A certificate left in sunlight fades until the writing is gone. A photograph stored in a plastic bag sticks to the plastic. Paper looks solid, but it is made of fibres that weaken with light, damp, heat and time, and once the damage is done it cannot be undone. Preservation is the set of simple habits that slow that damage down.

This guide is for families with old papers at home and for small archives, clubs, schools and local societies that keep records with no professional staff. You may have a box of family letters, a bundle of birth and marriage certificates, old photographs, school records, or the minutes of a club that has met for fifty years. You do not need a laboratory or a large budget to look after them: you need to understand what damages paper, and to store and handle it with care.

Preservation is not the same as repair. Preservation means keeping documents safe so they survive: good storage, careful handling, and a digital copy so the original is handled less. Repair means fixing damage that has already happened, and that is work for a professional conservator. This guide covers preservation only, and it tells you clearly where preservation ends and a conservator begins.

What damages paper, and how to stop it

Paper is damaged by a small number of enemies, and all of them are under your control. Understand them, and most of preservation follows.

  • Light. Sunlight and strong artificial light fade ink, yellow paper and weaken fibres. Keep documents out of direct sun, store them in closed boxes or folders, and never display an original in a sunny window. If you want to display a document, display a copy.
  • Damp. Damp breeds mould, cockles paper, and makes pages stick together. It also brings insects. Keep documents in a dry room, off the floor, away from outside walls and pipes, and never in a basement, a garage or a bathroom.
  • Heat and change. Heat dries paper and makes it brittle, and swings of temperature and humidity do more damage than a steady reading that is slightly off. A cool, steady room is better than a room that is hot by day and cold by night.
  • Acids. Ordinary cardboard, cheap folders and newsprint are acidic, and acid eats paper over time. Store documents in acid-free folders and boxes, and keep old newspaper cuttings in separate sleeves so their acid does not spread to the papers beside them.
  • Bad fastenings. Paper clips rust, staples rust and tear, rubber bands perish and stick, and sticky tape stains and cannot be removed. Remove old fastenings carefully, and never add new ones.
  • Handling. Hands carry oils and dirt, and a page folded, bent or dragged across a table is a page damaged. Handle paper with clean, dry hands, support fragile pages from below, and work on a clean, flat surface with no food or drink nearby.

None of this needs special equipment. A dry room, a closed box, clean hands and a little patience will keep most family papers safe for a lifetime. The expensive damage is almost always caused by a simple mistake: the box in the attic, the tape on the tear, the original framed in the sun.

How to store old documents, step by step

Start with the most fragile or most loved items, and work in small batches. A single afternoon will put a family box in good order.

  1. Sort before you store. Spread the papers out and sort them into groups: letters, certificates, photographs, newspaper cuttings, and anything else. Set aside anything that is mouldy, wet, stuck together or badly torn, and do not try to fix it yourself.
  2. Remove harmful fastenings. Take out old paper clips, staples and rubber bands with care. If a staple is rusted into the paper, leave it and ask a conservator: forcing it out tears the page.
  3. Put each item in an acid-free folder or sleeve. One folder per item, or one folder per small group of related items. Label the folder in pencil on the outside, never in pen on the document itself.
  4. Store folders flat or upright in an acid-free box. Flat is best for large or fragile sheets. Upright is fine for letters and small papers, packed snugly enough to stand straight without bending. Do not overfill a box.
  5. Choose the right room. A cool, dry, steady room inside the house, away from sunlight, radiators, outside walls and pipes. A bedroom cupboard or a spare-room shelf is usually far better than an attic, a basement or a garage.
  6. Keep a simple list. Write down what is in each box and folder, and keep the list with the boxes. A list means you can find a document without opening every folder, and it means someone else can find things when you are not there.

Photographs need the same care, with one extra rule: keep them away from ordinary paper and cardboard, which can stick to photographic surfaces, and handle them by the edges. Negatives and slides go in their own sleeves, stored cool and dark. If you are unsure about a photograph, treat it gently and ask an archive or a conservator.

How to scan a fragile paper safely

Scanning, or digitizing, is the best protection a document can have: once a good copy exists, the original can rest in its box and be handled rarely. It also protects against loss: a fire, a flood or a burglary cannot take a copy that lives somewhere else.

Use a flatbed scanner for most papers, or a camera on a steady stand for large, fragile or bound items that should not be pressed flat. Work with clean, dry hands, support the page from below, and close the scanner lid gently — never press down hard on a fragile page. Scan at a resolution that gives a readable copy: 300 dots per inch is a common minimum for an archive copy, and higher is better for small handwriting or fine detail. Save the scan in a widely used format, give the file a clear name that matches your list, and keep copies in two places: on your computer and on a separate drive or a cloud store.

Scan once and scan well, so the original is handled as little as possible. Check each scan on screen before you put the original away: a blurred or cropped scan means handling the page again. And remember that a scan is a copy, not a replacement: keep the original stored safely, because a scan cannot capture the paper, the ink or the feel of the thing itself.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Preservation costs little. Acid-free folders and boxes are the main expense, and they are sold by archive suppliers and good stationers; buy the sizes that fit your papers rather than the cheapest pack. A flatbed scanner or a phone camera is enough for most digitizing, and a pencil, a clean table and a notebook cover the rest. Avoid anything sold as a quick fix for old paper: sprays, laminating pouches and cleaning products usually do more harm than good.

Time depends on how much you have. A single box of family papers can be sorted, sleeved and listed in an afternoon. A club archive or a large family collection will take several sessions, and it is better to do a little at a time and do it well than to rush through everything in a weekend. Start with the items that matter most to you, or the ones that are most at risk, and work outward from there.

If you need a conservator, ask for one through your national archive, a local museum or a professional conservation body. Describe the item, say what is wrong with it, and ask what the work will cost before you agree. A good conservator will explain what can and cannot be done, and will never promise to make a damaged document look new.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

Almost every damaged document was damaged by someone trying to help. The mistakes below are the ones conservators see most often.

  • Laminating. Lamination seals a document in plastic and cannot be undone. Heat and adhesives damage the paper, and a laminated document cannot be treated by a conservator later. Use an acid-free sleeve instead, which protects the document and can be removed at any time.
  • Sticky tape. Tape stains, yellows, and leaves a residue that cannot be cleaned off. Never tape a torn page. A tear should be left alone, or shown to a conservator.
  • Storing in plastic bags. Sealed plastic traps damp and encourages mould. Paper needs to breathe: use acid-free folders and boxes, not bags, carrier bags or bin liners.
  • Keeping papers in the attic or the basement. Attics are hot and dry in summer and cold in winter; basements are damp. Both are the worst rooms in a house for paper. A cupboard in a lived-in room is far better.
  • Cleaning with water or chemicals. Water cockles paper, runs ink and spreads mould. Never wash, wipe or spray an old document. Dust it gently with a soft brush if you must, and leave the rest to a professional.
  • Folding and unfolding. Every fold weakens paper along the fold line, and a fold opened and closed many times will break. Store large sheets flat, and unfold a folded letter only when you need to read or scan it.

A preservation checklist you can follow

Work through this checklist when you first put your papers in order, and once a year afterwards.

  • Sort papers into groups and set aside anything mouldy, wet or torn.
  • Remove old paper clips, staples and rubber bands with care.
  • Put each item in an acid-free folder or sleeve, labelled in pencil.
  • Store folders flat or upright in acid-free boxes, not overfilled.
  • Keep boxes off the floor, away from sun, radiators and outside walls.
  • Keep newspaper cuttings in separate sleeves, away from other papers.
  • Handle photographs by the edges and store them in their own sleeves.
  • Scan the most important items and keep copies in two places.
  • Write a simple list of what is in each box, and keep it with the boxes.
  • Check the boxes once a year for damp, mould, insects or damage.
  • Ask a professional conservator before repairing anything valuable.

A yearly check takes less than an hour, and it catches problems while they are still small: a damp patch, a box left in the sun, a folder that has slipped. Papers that are checked stay safe; papers that are forgotten do not.

Tips from real practice

Label everything in pencil, on the folder and not on the document. A pencil note on the outside of a folder can be changed; a pen note on a certificate cannot. If a document needs a number or a name for your list, write it in pencil, lightly, on the back of the document in a corner — and only if you are sure it will not show through or damage the paper.

Do the most-loved items first. The letter from a grandparent, the photograph of a wedding, the certificate that proves a family story — those are the items that matter most, and they are the ones to sleeve, scan and list first. The rest can wait for the next session. Preservation done in small, regular steps beats a grand plan that never starts.

Finally, write down what you know. The names, dates and stories behind old papers live in people's heads, and they are lost faster than the papers themselves. Add a short note to each folder: who wrote this, when, and why it matters. The next person to open the box will be grateful, and the document will mean more for it.

What this page cannot do

This page gives general preservation information for families and small archives. It is not conservation treatment, and it cannot tell you how to repair a damaged document. Mould, water damage, stuck-together pages, torn maps and fragile bindings all need a professional conservator, and a wrong repair can cause damage that cannot be undone.

For valuable, fragile or damaged items, ask a conservator before you do anything. Describe the item, say what is wrong with it, and follow their advice. Preservation is about patience: the safest thing you can do for a damaged document is usually to store it safely and wait for the right help.

Questions people ask

What damages old documents most?

Light, damp, heat, acids in poor storage materials, insects and rough handling. Sunlight fades ink and weakens paper, damp breeds mould, acid folders eat paper over time, and sticky tape, staples and rubber bands all cause damage that cannot be undone. A cool, dry, steady room and acid-free folders prevent most of it.

How should I store old family papers?

In acid-free folders and boxes, in a cool, dry room inside the house, away from sunlight, radiators, outside walls and pipes. Store large sheets flat and small papers upright, packed snugly. Never use attics, basements, garages or plastic bags, and keep newspaper cuttings in separate sleeves.

Can I laminate an old document?

No. Lamination seals a document in plastic and cannot be undone: the heat and adhesives damage the paper, and a laminated document cannot be treated by a conservator later. Use an acid-free sleeve instead, which protects the document and can be removed at any time.

How do I scan a fragile paper safely?

Use a flatbed scanner, or a camera on a steady stand for items that should not be pressed flat. Handle the page with clean, dry hands, support it from below, and close the scanner lid gently. Scan at 300 dots per inch or higher, check the scan on screen, and keep copies in two places.

What temperature is best for paper storage?

Cool and steady: roughly 15 to 20 degrees Celsius, with relative humidity in the 30 to 50 percent range, is the range most guidance points to for paper. Stability matters more than an exact number — swings of temperature and humidity damage paper more than a steady reading a degree or two off.

When should I ask a professional conservator?

For anything valuable, fragile, mouldy, water-damaged or stuck together, and before any repair at all. A conservator can treat damage, advise on storage, and tell you what can and cannot be done. Ask through your national archive, a local museum or a professional conservation body.
