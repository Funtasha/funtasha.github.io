How marinas work

A marina is a harbour built for pleasure boats, with berths where boats are moored, pontoons to walk on, and services on shore. A berth is a marked place in the water, usually between pontoons, sized for a boat of a certain length and width. You pay for the berth by the season or by the year, and the price usually depends on the length of your boat and the time of year. Some marinas offer berths for a single night, for boats passing through, and some offer only long stays.

What the price includes varies. Most marinas include water and electricity at the berth, access to toilets and showers, and use of the pontoons and the car park. Some include winter storage, lifting in and out of the water, and a workshop. Some charge extra for all of those. Before you compare prices, find out what each price includes, because a low price that excludes the services you need is not low at all. And check the rules: most marinas have rules about noise, about work on boats, and about living aboard, and those rules matter more to daily life than the price does.

  • A berth is a marked place in the water, sized for your boat.
  • Price usually depends on boat length and the time of year.
  • Water, electricity, toilets and showers are usually included.
  • Lifting, storage and workshop time are often charged extra.
  • Marina rules on noise, work and living aboard vary, so read them.

What to compare

Compare marinas on the things that affect your sailing, not only on price. Location comes first: a marina close to the waters you sail saves hours on every trip, and a marina far from them costs hours you will never get back. Check how long it takes to reach open water from the berth, and whether the entrance is usable at all states of the tide, because a marina you cannot leave at low water is a marina that sets your sailing times for you.

Then compare the shelter and the pontoons. A marina sheltered from the prevailing wind is calmer in bad weather, and wide, stable pontoons make boarding safer, especially with children or heavy gear. Compare security: gates, lighting, cameras and staff on site, because a boat left for weeks needs to be safe. Compare the shore facilities against what you actually need, and compare the price only after you know what each price includes. A marina that costs more but saves an hour each way, every trip, may be the cheaper choice in the end.

  • Location: how long to open water, and whether the entrance works at all tides.
  • Shelter: protection from the prevailing wind and from wash.
  • Pontoons: wide and stable, with safe access at all tides.
  • Security: gates, lighting, cameras and staff on site.
  • Price: compare only after you know what each price includes.

The facilities that matter most

The facilities that matter most are the ones you use every time you sail. Fresh water and electricity at the berth come first, because without them every trip starts with carrying water and ends with a flat battery. Toilets and showers matter on any stay longer than a day, and a laundry is worth more than it sounds on a long trip. A car park close to the pontoons saves carrying gear a long way, and a slipway or a crane matters when the boat needs to come out of the water.

Beyond the basics, think about what your boat needs. A workshop, or a yard that allows owners to work on their boats, matters if you maintain the boat yourself. A fuel pontoon saves a trip to buy fuel elsewhere. A chandlery, a shop selling boat parts and supplies, saves a drive when something breaks. And a clubhouse or a cafe matters more than people expect, because sailing is a social pastime, and the marina where you know people is the marina you will enjoy. None of these is essential, but together they decide whether a marina is a place to keep a boat or a place to enjoy one.

  • Water and electricity at the berth, every time.
  • Toilets, showers and a laundry for longer stays.
  • A car park close to the pontoons.
  • A slipway or crane for lifting the boat out.
  • Workshop space, a fuel pontoon and a chandlery if you need them.

Questions before you sign

Visit the marina before you sign anything, and ask your questions on the pontoons, not only in the office. Ask how the berth is allocated, and whether the berth you are shown is the one you will get. Ask what happens at low water: does the berth dry out, and does the entrance stay usable? Ask about winter: is the price the same, is the water turned off, and what happens in a storm? Ask about the waiting list, because popular marinas have one, and a place on the list is not a place in the marina.

Ask about the neighbours too. A marina is a community, and the boats beside you matter: ask what kinds of boats use the marina, and whether there are rules about noise and working hours. Ask who to call out of hours if something goes wrong with your boat. And ask what happens if you want to leave early: is the money refunded, and how much notice do you need to give? The answers to these questions tell you more about a marina than any brochure.

  • Is the berth you are shown the one you will get?
  • Does the berth dry out at low water, and does the entrance stay usable?
  • What changes in winter: price, water, storm arrangements?
  • Is there a waiting list, and what does it mean?
  • Who do you call out of hours if something goes wrong?
  • What happens if you leave early: refund, notice, or neither?

The contract points

A marina contract is usually short, and you should read all of it before you sign. It should say which berth is yours, for how long, and what the price covers. It should say when payment is due, and what happens if it is late. It should say what the marina is responsible for, and what you are responsible for: usually the marina keeps the pontoons and the facilities in order, and you keep your boat insured, safe and in a state that does not endanger the boats beside it.

Check the points that cause the most arguments. Insurance: most marinas require you to insure your boat and to show the policy, so check what cover is required. Liability: who pays if your boat damages the pontoon or a neighbour's boat? Termination: how much notice must each side give, and is any money refunded if you leave early? And rules: the contract usually includes the marina rules, which cover noise, work on boats, and living aboard. If a clause is unclear, ask what it means in plain words before you sign it.

  • Which berth, for how long, and what the price covers.
  • Payment dates, and what happens if payment is late.
  • Insurance: what cover is required, and proof to show.
  • Liability: who pays if your boat damages something.
  • Termination: notice periods, and refunds if you leave early.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is picking by price alone. A cheap marina far from the waters you sail costs hours on every trip, and a cheap berth that dries out at low water sets your sailing times for you. The second mistake is no check on winter storage: a marina that suits summer may have no lifting, no storage and no water in winter, and finding that out in autumn is too late. The third mistake is signing without visiting, and discovering that the berth shown in the office is not the berth you get.

The fourth mistake is not reading the rules, and finding out that the work you planned to do on your boat is not allowed, or that living aboard is not allowed. The fifth is not checking the insurance requirement, and finding that your policy does not meet it. And the sixth is not asking about leaving early, and finding that a year's money is gone when your plans change. Visit first, read the contract, ask the awkward questions, and then sign.

  • Never pick by price alone; location and access matter more.
  • Check winter arrangements before you sign, not in autumn.
  • Visit first, and check the berth you will actually get.
  • Read the rules on work, noise and living aboard.
  • Check the insurance requirement against your own policy.
  • Ask what happens if you leave early, before you pay.

Questions people ask

How do I find a marina?

Start with the waters you sail, and look for marinas within easy reach of them. Visit the ones that suit your boat size, walk the pontoons, and ask about berths, prices and waiting lists. A visit tells you more than any listing, so never sign for a berth you have not seen.

What should I compare?

Compare location, shelter, pontoon quality, security and shore facilities, and compare the price only after you know what each price includes. Check how long it takes to reach open water, and whether the entrance works at all states of the tide.

How much does a berth cost?

Berth prices vary widely by place, boat length and season, so no single figure is useful. Ask what the price includes, because water, electricity, lifting and storage are sometimes included and sometimes charged extra. A low price that excludes what you need is not low.

What facilities matter most?

Fresh water and electricity at the berth come first, then toilets, showers and a car park close to the pontoons. A slipway or crane matters when the boat needs to come out. A workshop, a fuel pontoon and a chandlery matter if you maintain the boat yourself.

Should I visit first?

Yes, always: walk the pontoons, look at the berth you would actually get, and ask about low water, winter arrangements and the rules. Talk to other boat owners there if you can. The visit is when you find out what the brochure does not say.

What does the contract say?

It should say which berth is yours, for how long, what the price covers, and when payment is due. It should cover insurance, liability, termination and the marina rules. Read every clause before you sign, and ask what anything unclear means in plain words.
