What is a solar eclipse?

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between the Earth and the Sun and casts its shadow on the Earth. The Moon is much smaller than the Sun, but it is also much closer to us, so from our point of view the two look almost exactly the same size in the sky. When the line-up is perfect, the Moon covers the Sun completely, and day turns to night for a few minutes.

The Moon's shadow has two parts. The dark inner shadow, called the umbra, is the small area where the Sun is completely hidden: that is where totality happens. Around it is a much larger, lighter shadow, called the penumbra, where the Sun is only partly covered. If you stand in the penumbra you see a partial eclipse; only inside the umbra do you see a total one.

Eclipses do not happen every month, even though the Moon orbits the Earth every month, because the Moon's orbit is tilted. Most months the Moon passes above or below the Sun, and the shadows miss the Earth entirely. A solar eclipse needs the Sun, the Moon, and the Earth to line up almost exactly, which is why total eclipses are rare events for any one place.

Total, partial, and annular eclipses

There are three main kinds of solar eclipse, and it helps to know the difference. In a total eclipse, the Moon covers the Sun's disc completely, the sky goes dark, and the corona becomes visible. In a partial eclipse, the Moon covers only part of the Sun, so the light dims but the sky stays bright. In an annular eclipse, the Moon is a little too far from the Earth to cover the Sun fully, so a bright ring of sunlight remains around the dark Moon.

The eclipse of 2 August 2027 is a total eclipse, and it is a generous one: the path of totality is about 258 kilometres wide, and totality lasts about 6 minutes 20 seconds in Luxor, the longest totality on land until 2114. Outside the path, across a much wider area, people will see a partial eclipse instead.

For viewers, the difference matters most for safety. During the partial phases, before and after totality, you must wear certified eclipse glasses at all times. Only during the brief minutes of totality, when the Sun's disc is fully covered, is it safe to look up with the naked eye, and you must put your glasses back on the moment the Sun starts to reappear.

Why the 2027 eclipse is special

Most total eclipses last two or three minutes. The 2027 eclipse lasts about twice as long as that in the best places, which is what makes it famous before it has even happened. The longest totality on land is about 6 minutes 23 seconds, in the Egyptian desert near Luxor, and Luxor itself sees about 6 minutes 20 seconds. No total eclipse visible from land will last longer until 2114.

The length is partly luck and partly geometry. In August, the Earth is near the far end of its orbit around the Sun, so the Sun looks slightly smaller in the sky, and the Moon is near the close end of its orbit, so it looks slightly larger. A bigger Moon covering a smaller Sun means a longer totality, and in 2027 the numbers line up unusually well.

The path helps too. It crosses land for a long stretch, from southern Spain through North Africa and the Middle East, so millions of people can reach it without a ship or a plane to a remote island. Egypt, with the clearest August skies on the route, is where most eclipse travellers are heading, and Luxor is the centre of it all.

The path across the world

The path of totality for 2 August 2027 begins in southern Spain, crosses the Strait of Gibraltar, and then runs across Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, and Libya before reaching Egypt. From Egypt it crosses the Red Sea into Saudi Arabia, continues through Yemen, and ends in north-eastern Somalia. The whole band is about 258 kilometres wide, and everywhere inside it sees totality.

The cities on the path include Cádiz in Spain, Tangier in Morocco, Tunis in Tunisia, Luxor and Sohag in Egypt, Jeddah in Saudi Arabia, and Sana'a in Yemen. Each of them will see several minutes of darkness in the middle of the day, though only Luxor has a published timing in this guide, about 1:02 p.m. to about 1:08 p.m. local time.

Two cities that many people ask about are not on the path. Aswan, in southern Egypt, sees a deep partial eclipse but never totality, and Cairo sees only a partial eclipse. If you are planning a trip, check your city against the path before you book, and remember that a short move inside the band makes all the difference.

Watching safely

The golden rule of eclipse watching is simple: never look at the Sun without proper protection, except during the brief minutes of totality. The glasses you need are ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses, bought from a trusted seller and checked for scratches, holes, or damage before the day. Ordinary sunglasses, no matter how dark, are never safe, and neither are smoked glass, old camera film, or a phone screen.

A pinhole viewer is a safe alternative for the partial phases. Make a small hole in a sheet of card, hold it up to the Sun, and watch the Sun's image projected onto a second sheet below. You see the eclipse clearly without ever looking at the Sun itself, which makes it a good choice for children and for anyone whose glasses are damaged.

Children need supervision throughout. Practise putting the glasses on before looking up and taking them off only after looking away, and agree the rules before the day: glasses on for the partial phases, glasses off only during totality, and glasses straight back on the moment the Sun reappears. A little rehearsal makes the real day calm and safe.

Eclipse facts to share

Eclipses have fascinated people for thousands of years, and they come with wonderful stories. Ancient sky-watchers learned to predict them long before modern astronomy, and a total eclipse was once seen as an omen, a warning, or a wonder, depending on where you lived. Today we know exactly when and where they happen, but the feeling of watching one has not changed at all.

During totality, look for the corona, the Sun's outer atmosphere, which appears as a pale crown around the black disc of the Moon. Just before and just after totality, watch for the diamond ring: the last flash of sunlight through a valley on the Moon's edge, shining like a jewel. The temperature drops noticeably, the wind often changes, and birds go quiet as if night had fallen.
