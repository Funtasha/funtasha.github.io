Audio Licensing Information Directory

An audio licence is permission to use music or sound on stated terms, and using music without one can get a video muted or taken down. This guide explains the licence types, what each one allows, and where to get licensed audio.

This page is general information, not legal advice. Licence terms differ, so always read the licence text itself before you use a track, and keep the licence and the receipt on file for every track you publish.

How to use this guide

This guide explains audio licensing in plain English: what a licence is, what the common licence types let you do, where to get licensed audio, and the mistakes that get videos taken down. It is written for people who make videos, podcasts and presentations and want to use music and sound legally, without reading a law book first. Nothing here is legal advice, and if a deal matters a lot to you, check the licence text itself or ask a professional.

Read the sections in order the first time through. The first sections explain what a licence is and what the main types allow, the middle sections explain where to find licensed audio, and the last sections cover the mistakes that cause takedowns and how to keep your proof in order. This page names no specific store or library, because stores and libraries change, and a list printed here would go out of date. Instead it teaches you how to judge any source of audio by the licence it offers.

The licences explained

A licence is permission to use a piece of music or sound, given by the person who owns the rights to it, on stated terms. Music is owned like any other property: the composer, the performer and the recording owner each hold rights, and using a recording without permission can infringe more than one of them at once. A licence does not make you the owner of the music. It gives you the right to use it in the ways the licence describes, for the time and the places the licence describes, and nothing more.

Most audio you will meet falls into a few broad types. Royalty-free music is music you pay for once, or get under a subscription, and then use under the terms of that licence without paying again each time it plays. The name is misleading: royalty-free does not mean free of charge, and it does not mean free of rules. Creative Commons music is music the creator has shared under a public licence with stated terms, which often include giving credit, and which sometimes exclude commercial use. A sync licence is permission to synchronise music with moving pictures, which is what a video needs. Music in the public domain is music whose rights have expired, so no licence is needed, but the recording itself may still be owned, so check both the music and the recording.

  • Royalty-free: pay once or subscribe, then use under the licence terms.
  • Creative Commons: shared by the creator under public terms, often with credit required.
  • Sync licence: permission to put music together with moving pictures.
  • Public domain: the music's rights have expired, but the recording may still be owned.

What each licence allows

The only reliable way to know what a licence allows is to read the licence itself. Summaries and labels help you find the right licence, but the licence text is the agreement, and when a summary and the text disagree, the text wins. The questions to answer from the text are always the same: where can you use it, for how long, for what purpose, and what must you do in return. A licence that allows a personal video may not allow a video that earns money. A licence that allows one video may not allow a series. A licence that allows online use may not allow broadcast.

Three limits catch people out most often. The first is commercial use: many free licences allow personal projects but not anything that earns money, including a video with adverts on it. The second is the number of projects: some licences cover one project, and a second video needs a second licence. The third is credit: some licences require you to name the composer or the source in a specific way, and forgetting the credit breaks the licence even if everything else is in order. If any of these points is unclear, do not guess. Ask the seller, or choose a different track with clearer terms.

  • Where: online, broadcast, in person, or all of them.
  • How long: one project, one year, or forever.
  • What purpose: personal, commercial, or both.
  • What you must do: pay, give credit, or link back.
  • When in doubt, read the licence text, not the summary.

Where to get licensed audio

Licensed audio comes from a few kinds of source, and the right one depends on what you are making. Stock music libraries sell large collections of tracks under clear licences, with search tools that let you filter by mood, length and instrument. They suit videos and podcasts that need background music quickly. Composers who sell directly from their own websites suit projects that need something specific, and buying direct often means you can ask the composer a question before you buy. Free libraries offer tracks under public licences, which suit personal projects and small budgets, as long as you read the terms of each track.

A fourth route is to commission music: pay a composer to write something for your project. That costs more and takes longer, but the music fits exactly, and the licence is written for your project alone. Whichever route you take, the checks are the same. Read the licence before you buy, keep the receipt and the licence text together, and check that the licence covers your use: your platform, your purpose, and your project count. A cheap track with the wrong licence is more expensive than a dear track with the right one.

  • Stock libraries: large collections, clear licences, quick to search.
  • Composers selling direct: specific music, and a person to ask.
  • Free libraries: public licences, good for personal and small projects.
  • Commissioning: music written for your project, with a licence written for it too.
  • Always read the licence before you buy, and keep the receipt with it.

The mistakes that get videos taken down

Most takedowns come from a small set of mistakes, and all of them are avoidable. The first is using a well-known song without a licence, on the theory that a short clip is safe. There is no safe length: a few seconds of a famous song can be detected and claimed just as fast as the whole song. The second is trusting the words free download. A track that is free to download is not necessarily free to use, and many free downloads carry no licence at all, which means you have no permission to use them anywhere.

The third mistake is using music licensed for one project in a second project, which the licence does not cover. The fourth is forgetting the credit a licence requires, which breaks the licence even when the music itself was properly bought. The fifth is buying from a source that did not own the rights to sell the music in the first place, which leaves you with a receipt and no licence at all. The result of any of these is the same: the video is muted, blocked or taken down, and in serious cases the account is penalised. Check the licence before you publish, not after.

  • No safe length: a few seconds of a famous song can be claimed too.
  • Free to download does not mean free to use.
  • One licence usually covers one project, not every video you make.
  • A missing credit can break a licence that was otherwise in order.
  • Buy only from sources that own the rights they are selling.

Keeping your licence on file

When a platform questions your music, the answer is your file. Keep the licence text, the receipt, the date of purchase, and the name of the track in one place, for every track you use. A simple folder on your computer, one folder per project, with the licence and the receipt inside, is enough. If the licence came as a web page rather than a file, save a copy of the page, because web pages change and a licence that cannot be shown is a licence that cannot be proved.

Write down, in one line per track, what the licence allows: the project it covers, the platforms it covers, and the credit it requires. That line is what you check before you reuse a track in a new video, and it is what you show if anyone asks. Check your files once a year, because licences with a time limit run out, and a track that was licensed last year may not be licensed this year. A little filing now saves a takedown later.

  • Keep the licence text, the receipt, the date and the track name together.
  • One folder per project, with the licence and receipt inside.
  • Save a copy of any licence that only exists as a web page.
  • Write one line per track: project, platforms, credit required.
  • Check your files once a year for licences that have run out.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is thinking free download means free to use. It does not. A download button is not a licence, and a track with no licence attached gives you no permission at all. The second mistake is keeping no licence on file: when a platform asks for proof, a receipt alone is not enough, and a licence you cannot find is as useless as a licence you never had. The third is using one licence for many projects, when the licence covers only one.

The fourth mistake is trusting a summary instead of reading the licence text, and finding out too late that commercial use was excluded. The fifth is using a famous song for a few seconds and assuming it is too short to notice, which it is not. And the sixth is buying music from a source that never owned the rights, which leaves you with nothing to show. Every one of these mistakes is avoided the same way: read the licence before you use the music, keep the proof on file, and when a term is unclear, choose a different track.

  • Free to download is not free to use; only a licence gives permission.
  • Keep the licence on file, not just the receipt.
  • One licence usually covers one project.
  • Read the licence text, not the summary.
  • There is no safe length for an unlicensed song.

Questions people ask

What is an audio licence?

A licence is permission to use a piece of music or sound, given by the person who owns the rights, on stated terms. It does not make you the owner of the music. It gives you the right to use it in the ways, places and times the licence describes, and nothing more.

What does royalty-free mean?

Royalty-free means you pay once, or under a subscription, and then use the music under the licence terms without paying again each time it plays. It does not mean free of charge, and it does not mean free of rules. Always read the licence to see what it allows.

Can I use any song in my video?

No. Most songs need a licence, and there is no safe length: a few seconds of a famous song can be detected and claimed. Use music you have licensed, music in the public domain with a recording you also have rights to, or music you have commissioned.

Where do I get licensed music?

Stock music libraries sell large collections under clear licences. Composers selling direct suit projects that need something specific. Free libraries offer tracks under public licences for personal and small projects, and whichever route you take, read the licence before you buy.

What is a sync licence?

A sync licence is permission to synchronise music with moving pictures, which is what a video needs. It is separate from the right to play the music on its own. If your video includes music, you need the sync right as well as the right to use the recording.

What happens if I use music without a licence?

The video can be muted, blocked or taken down, and in serious cases the account can be penalised. The platform detects the music automatically, so unlicensed use is usually found quickly. Check the licence before you publish, and keep the proof on file.
