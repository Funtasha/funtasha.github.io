Friendship Calculator: Check Your Name Match

Type two names and this calculator gives you a playful friendship score from 0 to 100, with a kind message to match. The score comes from the letters in the names only, so the same pair always gets the same result, and it is for fun — it means nothing about a real friendship.

How the score is worked out

Type two names and the calculator turns the letters into a score. It adds up the character codes of the letters in both names, multiplies the total by 31, and takes the remainder when divided by 101 — so the score always lands between 0 and 100. The two names are sorted before the maths runs, which means the order you type them in makes no difference: Maya and Jon get the same score as Jon and Maya. The same pair of names always gives the same score, every time you try it.

What the score means

The score comes with a kind, playful message band. High scores get warm words about a strong bond, and lower scores get gentle encouragement — every friendship starts somewhere, and a low number is just an invitation to plan a coffee or a long chat. The bands are written to be fun for everyone, never mean, so you can share your result with the other person without worrying.

For fun only: the score comes from the letters in the two names and nothing else. It means nothing about a real friendship, predicts nothing, and should never be taken seriously.

Fun ways to use your result

Try the calculator with your oldest friend, your newest friend, or a family member, and compare the scores over a cup of tea. It makes a light party game too: everyone checks their score with the birthday person, and the highest pair wins a small cheer. Whatever the number says, the real test of a friendship is the time you spend together — this page just gives you a playful excuse to talk about it.

How the calculator works, step by step

The calculator is a small piece of arithmetic dressed up as a game. It starts by taking the two names and keeping only the letters: spaces and other characters are ignored, so a name with two words counts the same as a name with one. Then it adds up the character codes of those letters. Every letter has a number in the code the computer uses, and adding them together gives a total for the pair.

That total is multiplied by 31, and the answer is divided by 101, keeping only the remainder. The remainder is always between 0 and 100, and that is the score. Before any of this happens, the two names are sorted, so the order you type them in makes no difference: Maya and Jon give exactly the same score as Jon and Maya. Because the same letters always give the same total, the same pair of names always gives the same score, every time you try it.

Nothing else goes into the score. It does not know how long you have been friends, how often you talk, or what you have been through together: it only sees letters. That is worth remembering when you read the result. The calculator is a playful way to start a conversation about friendship, not a measure of one. The steps shown under the result let you check the maths yourself, and that is part of the fun.

What the score means, and what it does not

A high score is a happy accident of letters, and a low score is the same. The message bands are written to be kind at every level: high scores get warm words about a strong bond, and lower scores get gentle encouragement and the reminder that every friendship starts somewhere. Nobody should feel bad about a low number, and nobody should feel smug about a high one. The score says nothing about the real friendship between two people.

What a real friendship is made of has nothing to do with names. It is made of time spent together, of showing up when it matters, of honest conversations and shared jokes. A friendship that has survived busy weeks, long silences and bad weather is a strong one whatever the calculator says, and a new friendship with a low score may turn out to be one of the best. The calculator cannot see any of that, and it does not try to.

The best way to read the score is as a starting point for a chat. A high score is an excuse to tell your friend you are glad to know them. A low score is an excuse to plan something together: a coffee, a walk, a long phone call. Either way, the number has done its job if it gets two people talking and spending time together, which is the only thing that ever makes a friendship stronger.

Friendship ideas for every score

Whatever the score says, the next step is the same: do something together. A walk in the park, a coffee at a favourite cafe, or a long chat on the phone costs little and means a lot. Friends who live far apart can keep in touch with regular calls, shared photos, or a film watched at the same time from two different homes. The habit matters more than the occasion: a short call every week does more for a friendship than a grand plan once a year.

Shared activities build the memories that friendships are made of. Cooking a meal together, starting a small project, or learning something new side by side gives two people stories to retell later. Even a disagreement, handled kindly, can strengthen a friendship, because it shows that both people care enough to be honest. The calculator's message bands hint at this: the best friendships are the ones where two people can be themselves around each other.

If the score was low and it stung a little, treat it as a nudge rather than a verdict. Send a message, suggest a plan, or simply tell your friend you were thinking of them. Small gestures add up, and a friendship tended with small gestures grows steadily. The calculator will give the same score next time, the letters do not change, but the friendship behind it can grow in any direction you choose.
