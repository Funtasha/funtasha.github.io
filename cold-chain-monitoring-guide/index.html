Cold Chain Guide

The cold chain is the unbroken series of cold storage and transport steps that keeps food and medicine safe from where it is made to where it is used. This guide explains the temperatures that matter, how breaks happen, and the simple routines that keep the chain unbroken.

Temperature ranges on this page are general reference only, not rules for any specific product. Always follow the label on the product and your local food safety or health rules, and never guess whether food that got warm is safe.

What this is and who needs it

The cold chain is the chain of refrigerated storage and transport that keeps chilled and frozen goods cold from production to the final user. Every link matters: the warehouse where goods are stored, the truck that carries them, the fridge in the shop, and the journey home. If one link warms up, the goods may spoil even if every other link stayed cold, which is why the chain is only as strong as its weakest link.

This guide is for anyone who handles food or medicine that must stay cold: shop owners, market sellers, restaurant staff, pharmacy workers, and small vendors who carry chilled goods to customers. It is also useful for households that buy in bulk or carry frozen shopping home in hot weather. You do not need expensive equipment to follow it, and the most important habits cost nothing at all.

The guide explains the temperatures that matter, how breaks in the chain happen, and the simple monitoring routines that catch a problem early. It shows no live readings and no dashboard, because the readings that matter are the ones from your own thermometer, on your own goods, on the day.

How it works, step by step

The cold chain works as a series of handovers, and each handover is a point where things can go wrong. The goods are chilled at the source, kept cold in storage, loaded into a chilled vehicle, carried to the shop, and stored cold again until they are sold. At every handover, someone should check the temperature and record it, so a break is found at once instead of hours later.

  1. Chill at the source. Food and medicine should be cooled to their safe temperature before they leave the place they were made or packed. Loading warm goods into a cold truck does not chill them in time.
  2. Store cold. Warehouses and fridges should hold the right temperature, with goods arranged so cold air can move around them. An overfilled fridge cannot keep everything cold.
  3. Pre-cool the vehicle. A fridge truck or an insulated box should be cold before the goods go in, not after. Loading cold goods into a warm vehicle wastes the cold they arrived with.
  4. Load fast and check the temperature. Load quickly, keep doors shut as much as possible, and check the temperature at loading with a thermometer.
  5. Carry cold and check on arrival. During transport, keep the cooling running and the doors shut. On arrival, check the temperature again and record it before the goods go into storage.
  6. Store cold at the destination, and keep checking. A fridge that is checked twice a day catches a fault in hours. A fridge that is never checked catches it when the food is already spoiled.

The routine is the same at every scale, from a large warehouse to a market stall with a cool box. What changes is the equipment, not the principle: check the temperature at every handover, and write it down.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

The cost of cold chain equipment varies widely, from a simple thermometer and a paper log, which cost very little, to a data logger that records temperatures automatically, to a refrigerated vehicle, which is a major purchase. Small sellers can start with the simple end: a reliable thermometer, insulated boxes, ice packs, and a log sheet. The expensive equipment matters for large volumes and long journeys, not for a first step.

Time is the smaller cost. Checking a temperature takes a minute, and writing it in a log takes another. The habit that matters is the regularity: a check at loading, a check on arrival, and a check during the day, every day. A power cut needs a plan made in advance, because deciding what to do in the dark, with a warming fridge, is too late.

Before you start, gather four things: a thermometer you trust, a log sheet or notebook, insulated boxes or bags for transport, and the safe temperature for each product you handle, from its label. If you move goods by vehicle, check that the cooling works before you load, and carry a spare thermometer, because a broken thermometer in the middle of a delivery is no help at all.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

Most cold chain breaks come from ordinary habits, not from equipment failure. The mistakes below are the ones that spoil the most food, and each one is easy to fix.

  • Leaving goods on a warm dock. Goods left standing in the sun or on a warm loading bay warm up fast, and a short wait can undo hours of careful chilling. Move cold goods straight from cold storage to cold transport.
  • Overfilling a fridge. A fridge packed tight cannot circulate cold air, so the middle stays warm while the edges freeze. Leave space between items and do not block the vents.
  • Trusting one reading. A single thermometer can be wrong, misplaced or broken. Check with a second thermometer when a reading looks odd, and check the temperature in more than one place in a large fridge or vehicle.
  • No log. Without a written record, nobody knows when the temperature rose, how high it went, or how long it stayed there. A log turns a guess into a fact, and it is the first thing an inspector asks for.
  • Ignoring a broken seal or a damaged door. A fridge door that does not seal, or a vehicle with a damaged cooling unit, warms up slowly and spoils goods quietly. Fix seals and cooling faults at once.
  • Refreezing thawed food. As a general rule, food that has thawed should not be frozen again; it should be cooked and eaten, or thrown away. Follow the label and local food safety rules, and when in doubt, throw it away.

The pattern behind every mistake is the same: the cold chain was assumed, not checked. Checking is what turns an assumption into a fact.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist every day you handle chilled or frozen goods, and keep the completed log with your records.

  • Check the fridge temperature first thing in the morning, and write it down with the time.
  • Check the freezer temperature at the same time, and write it down too.
  • When goods arrive, check their temperature before they go into storage, and record it.
  • Pre-cool insulated boxes and vehicles before loading cold goods.
  • Load quickly, keep doors and lids shut, and do not leave cold goods standing in warm air.
  • On arrival at the destination, check the temperature again and record it.
  • Check fridge and freezer temperatures again before closing, and write them down.
  • Look at the log each week: are the readings steady, or is a fridge drifting warmer?
  • Check door seals and cooling units once a week, and report faults at once.
  • Know your plan for a power cut: how long the fridge stays cold, who to call, and when to move or discard goods.

The checklist takes a few minutes a day and prevents the losses that come from a break found too late. A log with a gap in it is a gap in your protection, so fill it in every day, even on quiet days.

Tips from real practice

Check the temperature at handovers, because that is where the chain breaks. The loading bay, the delivery step and the shop door are the danger points, and a check at each one catches a problem while there is still time to fix it. A check at the end of the day finds the problem after the goods have already spoiled.

Keep the log even when nothing goes wrong. A full log proves the chain held, which matters for customers, for inspectors, and for your own peace of mind. It also shows patterns: a fridge that drifts warmer every afternoon, or a delivery that always arrives warm, is a problem you can fix before it becomes a loss.

Finally, have a plan for the day the power fails or the fridge breaks. Know how long your fridge stays cold with the door shut, know who repairs it, and know when to move goods to a working fridge or throw them away. The plan costs nothing to make and saves everything on the day you need it.

Questions people ask

What is the cold chain?

The cold chain is the chain of refrigerated storage and transport that keeps chilled and frozen goods cold from production to the final user. Every link matters: the warehouse, the truck, the shop fridge and the journey home. If one link warms up, the goods may spoil even if every other link stayed cold.

Why does temperature matter for food and medicine?

Cold slows the growth of the bacteria that spoil food and make people ill, and it keeps the quality of fresh food for longer. Some medicines lose their strength if they get warm, so the cold chain protects them too. The risk grows the longer goods stay warm, which is why both the temperature and the time matter.

What temperature should a fridge truck keep?

As general reference, chilled food is usually kept at or below 5 degrees Celsius, and frozen food at or below minus 18 degrees Celsius. The right temperature depends on the product, so always follow the label and the rules for that product. A fridge truck should be pre-cooled before loading, and the temperature should be checked at loading and at delivery.

How do you know if the cold chain broke?

The clearest sign is a temperature log that shows the goods rose above their safe range, or a thermometer reading on arrival that is too high. Other signs are melted ice, soft frozen food, condensation inside packaging, or a vehicle that arrived with the cooling switched off. When in doubt, treat the goods as suspect and check the rules for that product before selling or using them.

What should I do if food got warm?

First, check how warm it got and for how long, using the thermometer and the log. As a general rule, chilled food that has been warm for more than a short time should not be sold or eaten, and thawed food should not be refrozen. When in doubt, throw it away and follow your local food safety rules; the cost of the food is never worth the risk of making someone ill.

How do small sellers keep things cold?

Small sellers use insulated boxes, ice packs and cool bags for short journeys, and a reliable fridge or freezer at the shop. A simple thermometer and a paper log are enough to start: check the temperature when goods arrive, during the day, and before closing. Pre-chill the boxes, keep lids shut, and plan the route so cold goods spend as little time as possible outside the fridge.
