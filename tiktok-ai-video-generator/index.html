TikTok rewards short vertical clips that hook in the first two seconds. This guide shows you the format rules, the free tool chain, and a hook builder that turns your topic into five opening lines and a clip-by-clip plan. The page writes words and plans, not videos.

Only post clips and photos you have the right to use. Never use someone else's face or voice without permission, and never present an AI clip as a real event.

The format rules: vertical, captioned, fast

TikTok is a vertical app, so your clips must be vertical too. Shoot and generate in 9:16, the tall shape that fills a phone screen, and keep the important action in the centre of the frame, where captions and buttons will not cover it. A sideways clip with black bars looks out of place in the feed, and viewers swipe past it without thinking.

Captions are not optional. Many people watch with the sound off, at work, on a bus, or late at night, and a clip with no words on screen says nothing to them. CapCut adds captions for free, and you can style them big and bold so the message lands even on mute. Put the key words on screen in the first second, not at the end.

Speed is the third rule. A TikTok clip is short by nature: most ideas fit in 15 to 30 seconds, and the strongest clips say one thing and stop. Cut the slow opening, cut the throat-clearing, and start with the moment that matters. If a clip feels long, it usually has two ideas in it — split them into two clips.

The first two seconds: why the hook is everything

On TikTok, every clip is one swipe away from being skipped. The first two seconds decide whether a viewer stays or goes, and there is no second chance. A good hook is not a trick — it is a promise. It tells the viewer, in one line, why this clip is worth their next twenty seconds.

The strongest hooks are specific. A hook that names the change the viewer will see beats a vague one every time: say what happens, not that something happens. A question works too, when it is a real question that the clip answers. A bold claim works when you can prove it in the next ten seconds.

Write five hooks for every idea, then pick the best one. That is what the builder at the top of this page does: type your topic, pick a format, and it gives you five opening lines plus a clip plan. Use the strongest line as your on-screen text in the first frame, and say it out loud as well, so it lands for viewers with the sound on and off.

The free tool chain: effects, motion, finish

No single free tool does the whole job, so think in a chain: one tool for the effect, one for the motion, and one for the finish. PixVerse is the effects pick, with 60 starter credits plus 30 a day and free clips up to 540p, though free exports carry a watermark. Kling is the motion pick, with free daily credits and a watermark on free exports, and paid plans from ~$7–10/mo.

Flow is the clean-clip pick. It gives 50 free credits a day, enough for five 8-second Veo 3.1 Lite clips, and most free clips carry only an invisible SynthID mark, with no visible watermark. That makes Flow the best choice when the clip itself must look clean, for example in a product demo or a before-and-after.

CapCut is the finish. It is free, and it handles captions, the 9:16 cut, effects, and the final export for TikTok, Reels, and Shorts. Generate your clips in Flow, Kling, or PixVerse, stitch them in CapCut, add captions, and post. The chain costs nothing to start, and each tool does the part it is best at.

If you are stuck for ideas, start from a format that already works. Each format below comes with a prompt you can paste into a video tool. Swap in your own subject and make it yours.

One format, one idea, one clip. When a format works, repeat it with a new subject before you try a new format.

From hook to finished clip: the workflow

Start with the hook. Use the builder above to write five opening lines for your topic, then pick the strongest one. That line becomes your on-screen text and your spoken first line. Everything else in the clip exists to deliver on the promise the hook makes.

Next, generate the clips. Most free generators make clips of 5 to 15 seconds, so plan a 30-second video as three or four clips: the hook, the show, the proof, and the close. Generate each clip separately, with a simple prompt and one clear action per clip, and save the best take of each.

Then stitch and finish in CapCut. Put the clips in order, trim the slow frames, add captions and a simple effect, and export in 9:16. Watch the finished clip once on your phone before you post: if the first two seconds do not grab you, change the hook, not the whole clip.

Posting cadence: how often, and what to learn

Pick a pace you can keep for a month. One clip a day beats five clips in one excited afternoon followed by silence, because TikTok rewards steady posting and your viewers learn when to expect you. If one a day is too much, pick three days a week and keep them.

Watch what your clips teach you. When a clip holds viewers past the first two seconds, look at its hook and reuse the pattern. When a clip is skipped, the hook is usually the problem, not the idea. Keep a short note of which hooks worked, and the builder's five lines give you fresh ones to test.

Do not chase every trend. A trend that fits your topic is worth joining; a trend that does not fit will pull in viewers who leave as fast as they came. Your own formats, posted steadily, build an audience that stays.

What AI cannot do for your TikTok

AI video tools are helpers, not a film crew. They make short clips, not finished TikToks: no free tool turns a topic into a polished 30-second video in one click. The planning, the hook, the captions, and the posting are still your job, and they are the parts that decide whether a clip works.

The clips themselves have limits. Faces and hands can warp, text in a scene can come out garbled, and motion can wobble. Short, simple prompts with one action per clip fail less often than long, busy ones. When a clip comes out wrong, change one thing — the camera move, or the subject — and try again, instead of re-running the same prompt.

Old guides may name tools that no longer exist. Sora shut down on 26 April 2026, and the Sora API shut down on 24 September 2026, so it is not an option for your clips. Stick to tools that are live today — Flow, Kling, PixVerse, Pika, and CapCut for the finish — and check the free tier on the day you use it, because limits change.

Questions people ask

Does this page make the TikTok video for me?

No. This page builds hook lines and a clip plan — words, not video. You generate the clips in a video tool such as Flow, Kling, or PixVerse, then stitch them together in CapCut.

What length works best on TikTok?

A total of 15 to 30 seconds works well for most ideas, built from clips of 5 to 15 seconds each. Shorter is fine if the idea is simple; longer needs a stronger reason to keep watching.

Which free tool is best for TikTok effects?

PixVerse is a good pick for effects, with 60 starter credits plus 30 a day and free clips up to 540p, though free exports carry a watermark. Kling is stronger for realistic motion, with free daily credits and a watermark on free exports.

Do I need to show my face?

No. Before-and-after clips, product demos, photo effects, and text-led tips all work without a face on screen. Faceless formats suit people who would rather let the product or the idea do the talking.

What happened to Sora?

Sora shut down on 26 April 2026, and the Sora API shut down on 24 September 2026. OpenAI announced the shutdown on 24 March 2026 and named no successor. Guides that still list Sora as a working tool are out of date.

Can I use these clips commercially?

It depends on the tool you used to generate each clip. Free tiers often limit commercial use, and the rules change, so check the terms of Flow, Kling, or PixVerse on the day you post. Keep your own photos and your own words in the clip, and you keep the parts that are clearly yours.
