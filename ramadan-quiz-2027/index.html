How the quiz works

The quiz has twelve questions, shown one at a time. Read the question, pick the answer you think is right, and you will find out at once whether you were correct. Each answer comes with a short explanation, so even a wrong answer teaches you something.

Your score builds as you go, and at the end you will see a short message about how you did. You can play again straight away: the questions stay the same, so a second try is a good way to see how much you remembered from the first round.

Ramadan basics the quiz covers

The questions cover the basics of the month: which month Ramadan is, when the fast begins and ends, what suhoor and iftar are, and what taraweeh prayer is. They also cover the festivals and charities around the month, such as Eid al-Fitr and zakat al-fitr.

A few questions are about the 2027 dates, which are given in the guide on this page and on the countdown page. If you are unsure of a date, read the sections below first: the answers are all there, and the quiz is more fun when you have had a chance to learn them.

The two date questions are the only ones that change from year to year. Ramadan 2027 is expected to begin on Monday 8 February 2027, and Eid al-Fitr 2027 is expected on Tuesday 9 March 2027. Both dates depend on the sighting of the moon, so the quiz asks for the expected dates, and the confirmed dates may move by a day.

Why quizzes are a lovely way to learn

A quiz turns learning into a game. Instead of reading a long list of facts, you test yourself, get instant feedback, and remember the answers because you had to think about them. That is why teachers have used quizzes for generations, and why they work just as well at home.

The explanations matter as much as the score. Each one gives the reason behind the answer in a sentence or two, so you finish the quiz knowing more than when you started, whatever your score. If a question surprises you, that is a good thing: surprise is one of the best ways to remember.

Playing together as a family

The quiz works well as a family game. Take turns answering, let the children go first, and talk about each explanation before moving on. Older children can explain the answers to younger ones, which helps them learn twice: once when they answer, and once when they teach.

Keep it light. There is no prize for a perfect score, and no shame in a low one. The point is to spend a few minutes together learning about the month, and to give everyone, whatever their age, a chance to shine on the questions they know.

Questions about the month itself

Several questions are about the month of Ramadan itself: its place in the Islamic calendar, how long it lasts, and why its length changes from year to year. Ramadan follows the moon, so it lasts 29 or 30 days, and it begins about eleven days earlier each solar year.

That is why Ramadan moves through the seasons. In 2027 it falls in February and March, when the days are still short in the northern hemisphere. In other years it falls in summer, when the fasts are much longer. The quiz does not ask about other years, but it is a nice thing to know.

Questions about fasting and prayer

The fasting questions cover the rhythm of the day: suhoor before dawn, the fast itself, and iftar at sunset. They also touch on taraweeh, the night prayer that many people pray in congregation during Ramadan, and on Laylat al-Qadr, the night sought in the last ten nights of the month.

If any of these words are new to you, the explanations after each question will introduce them gently. You do not need to know anything before you start: the quiz is written for beginners as much as for people who have fasted for years.

One question asks about zakat al-fitr, the charity given before the Eid prayer so that everyone can join the celebration. It is a good example of how the month links worship and generosity: the fast teaches restraint, and the charity turns that lesson into something practical for people in need.

Making your own Ramadan quiz

Once you have played this quiz a few times, try writing your own questions. Pick a topic from the month, such as the prophets, the history of the first Ramadan, or the foods different countries eat at iftar, and write four options for each question, with only one right answer.

Writing questions is a wonderful way to learn, because you have to check every fact before you can ask about it. Children love setting quizzes for their parents, and the family iftar table is a perfect place to try them out. Keep the questions friendly, keep the answers short, and always explain the answer afterwards, just as this page does.

After the quiz: keep learning
