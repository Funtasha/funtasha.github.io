Historical Photograph Archive Guide

Historical photographs are windows into the past: they show streets, faces, work and daily life as they really looked. This guide explains where to find them, how to read what they show, and how to keep old prints safe for years to come.

This page is a guide only. It describes real public archives in general terms and invents no holdings. Old photographs are fragile: handle them with clean, dry hands, and ask a professional conservator before treating any valuable or damaged print.

What this is and who needs it

A historical photograph is any old photograph that tells us something about the past. It might be a formal portrait from a studio, a street scene taken by a passer-by, a family snapshot, or a news photograph from a great event. What they share is evidence: a photograph shows what a place looked like, what people wore, how they worked and how they lived, in a way that written records often cannot.

This guide is for anyone who wants to find historical photographs, understand them, or look after old prints of their own. Family historians use them to put faces to names. Local history groups use them to show how a town has changed. Teachers use them to bring the past to life for students. And many people simply have a box of old family photographs and want to know what they show and how to keep them safe.

Photographs live in two kinds of places: archives and private hands. Public archives — national libraries, national archives, museums and local record offices — hold large collections, and many of them have put parts of their collections online. The Library of Congress and the National Archives in the United States, and the British Library and the National Archives in the United Kingdom, are among the best-known public archives with large photograph collections. Private hands hold the rest: family albums, attic boxes and the collections of local societies, often uncatalogued and unknown to anyone outside the family.

Both matter. The public archives hold the famous images, but the private collections hold the everyday ones: the street your grandparents lived on, the shop your great-uncle ran, the school your mother attended. For most family and local history, the photographs that matter most are the ones closest to home.

How it works, step by step

Finding a historical photograph starts with a question: what are you looking for, and where might it be? A photograph of a particular place is most likely to be in the archive of that place — a local library, a town museum, or a national archive with a local collection. A photograph of a famous event is more likely to be in a national collection. Start with the archive closest to the subject, and work outward from there.

Most large public archives have an online catalog you can search by place, person, date or subject. Searching takes practice: try different words for the same thing, try the old name of a place as well as the new one, and try broad terms before narrow ones. If the online catalog does not have what you want, the archive may still hold it — not everything has been catalogued online, and older catalogs are sometimes on paper in the reading room.

  1. Write down what you are looking for: the place, the people, the event and the rough date.
  2. Search the online catalog of the archive closest to the subject, trying different words.
  3. If the online search fails, contact the archive and ask — archivists know their collections well.
  4. When you find a photograph, note its reference number so you can find it again.
  5. Read the photograph: look at the clothes, the vehicles, the signs and the buildings for clues to its date.
  6. Check the back of a print for notes, stamps or a photographer's name — the back often tells more than the front.
  7. If the photograph is yours, store the original safely and work from a scan, not the print.

Reading a photograph is a skill that improves with practice. Start with the obvious: what is in the picture, and what does it tell you? Clothes, vehicles, street signs and shop names all carry dates. A streetcar, a horse-drawn cart and a motor car in the same picture tell you about a time of change. Then look for what is missing: a building that is not there yet, a road that has not been built. Absence can date a photograph as surely as presence.

Dating an old print also uses the photograph itself. The size and shape of the print, the thickness of the card it is mounted on, and the style of the photographer's studio stamp all changed over time. A portrait on thick card with the photographer's name printed below it is often a cabinet card, popular from the 1860s to the early 1900s. A small, glossy snapshot is usually much later. These clues give a range, not an exact year — but a range is often enough to place a photograph in a family's story.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Looking at historical photographs in public archives is often free. Many archives let anyone visit the reading room and view photographs without charge, and many have put large numbers of photographs online for free viewing. There may be a charge for copies, for high-resolution scans, or for permission to publish a photograph — the rules vary by archive and by the photograph's rights, so ask before you assume. Always check the terms before using an archive photograph in a book, a website or a display.

Preserving your own old photographs costs little in money and a little in time. The essentials are simple: clean, dry hands; a cool, dry, dark place to store them; and sleeves or boxes that do not damage the prints. Acid-free sleeves and boxes are sold for this purpose, and they are worth the small cost for photographs that matter to you. What costs more is scanning: a good scanner or a scanning service, and the time to scan and label each photograph. But a scan is the single best protection a photograph can have, because it survives even if the original is lost.

Before you start, gather what you already know. For family photographs, ask older relatives what they remember about the people and places in the pictures — and do it soon, because memories fade and the people who can identify a face will not always be there. Write the names on the back of a copy, never on the original, and keep the notes with the photographs. A photograph with no names is a mystery; the same photograph with names is family history.

For archive research, gather your question before you visit. Know the place, the rough date and the names involved, and bring a notebook. Archive visits go better with a clear question than with a vague hope, and the archivist can help you far more when you can say exactly what you are looking for.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake with old photographs is storing them badly. Attics and basements are the worst places: attics get hot, basements get damp, and both damage photographs over time. Photographs stored in a hot attic fade and stick together; photographs stored in a damp basement grow mould. The best place is a cool, dry room inside the house, away from sunlight and away from radiators and pipes.

The second mistake is handling prints carelessly. Fingers leave oils that damage the surface over time, and a print held by its face can be scratched or bent. Handle prints by their edges, with clean, dry hands, and lay them flat rather than stacking them face to face. Never use tape, glue or sticky notes on a photograph — the adhesive damages the surface and cannot be removed safely.

The third mistake is assuming a photograph shows what it seems to show. Old photographs were sometimes staged, retouched or mislabelled, and family stories about a photograph are not always right. Check a photograph against other evidence: the date the photographer was working, the clothes people wore, the buildings in the background. A photograph is evidence, but like all evidence it needs to be read carefully and checked against other sources.

  • Never store old photographs in a hot attic or a damp basement.
  • Never write on the front of a photograph, and never use a ballpoint pen on the back.
  • Never use tape, glue or sticky notes on a print.
  • Never laminate an old photograph — lamination is permanent and damages the print.
  • Never scan a fragile print on a flatbed scanner without a protective sleeve, and never force a curled print flat.
  • Never throw away a damaged photograph without asking a conservator — much can be saved.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist for your own old photographs, and again whenever you find photographs in an archive. It covers finding, reading, storing and sharing, in the order you will usually do them.

  • Write down what you are looking for: place, people, event and rough date.
  • Search the online catalog of the archive closest to the subject.
  • If the search fails, contact the archive and ask — archivists know their collections.
  • Note the reference number of every photograph you find, so you can find it again.
  • Read the photograph for clues: clothes, vehicles, signs, buildings and what is missing.
  • Check the back of a print for notes, stamps or a photographer's name.
  • Ask older relatives to identify people and places, and write the names on a copy, not the original.
  • Store originals in a cool, dry, dark place, in acid-free sleeves or boxes, away from heat and damp.
  • Scan the photographs you care about most, and keep the scans backed up in two places.
  • Check the rights before publishing or sharing an archive photograph.
  • Ask a professional conservator about any valuable or damaged print before treating it yourself.

If you only do one thing on this list, scan your most important photographs. A scan is a backup that survives fire, flood and fading, and it lets you share the photograph with family without handling the original. Scanning takes time, but it is the single step that protects a photograph's future.

Tips from real practice

Work from scans, not originals. Once a photograph is scanned, put the original safely away and use the scan for viewing, sharing and research. Every time a print is handled, it is at a little risk; a scan can be handled as often as you like. This is the single habit that keeps old photographs safe for the longest time.

Label everything, and label it now. A scan file named with the date, the place and the people in it will be findable in twenty years. A scan file named with a number only will be a mystery. The same goes for the originals: a note on the sleeve, not on the photograph, saying who and where and when, turns a box of pictures into an archive.

Share what you find. A photograph in a drawer helps nobody. Share scans with family, offer copies to a local archive or history group, and let other researchers know what you have. Photographs gain value when they are seen and used, and the person who identifies a face in your photograph may be someone you have never met.

Finally, enjoy the search. Looking for historical photographs is slow work, full of dead ends and small victories. The photograph you find after a long search — the street as your grandparents knew it, the face you have only heard described — is worth every hour. Archives are patient places, and the past rewards those who look for it carefully.

Questions people ask

Where can I find historical photographs?

In public archives such as national libraries, national archives, museums and local record offices, many of which have put parts of their collections online. The Library of Congress and the National Archives in the United States, and the British Library and the National Archives in the United Kingdom, are well-known starting points. Family photographs are often in private hands — albums, attic boxes and local history groups.

How can I tell how old a photo is?

Look for clues in the picture: the clothes, the vehicles, the street signs and the buildings. Then look at the photograph itself: its size, the card it is mounted on, and the photographer's studio stamp. A portrait on thick card with the photographer's name below is often a cabinet card, popular from the 1860s to the early 1900s. These clues give a range, not an exact year.

How should I store old printed photos?

In a cool, dry, dark place inside the house, away from sunlight, radiators and damp. Use acid-free sleeves or boxes, handle prints by their edges with clean, dry hands, and never store them in a hot attic or a damp basement. Scan the photographs you care about most and keep the scans backed up.

Can I share an old family photo online?

Your own family photographs are yours to share, but think about the people in them and about privacy, especially for living relatives. Photographs from an archive may have rights attached, so check the archive's terms before publishing or sharing them. When in doubt, ask the archive.

What is a photo archive?

A photo archive is a collection of photographs kept, catalogued and preserved so that people can find and use them. Archives may be public, like a national library, or private, like a family collection or a local history group. A good archive keeps a record of what each photograph shows, where it came from, and how to find it again.

How do I scan an old photo safely?

Use a flatbed scanner with a protective sleeve if the print is fragile, and never force a curled print flat. Scan at a good resolution so the scan is useful, and save a copy in two places. If a print is very fragile, torn or stuck to glass, ask a professional conservator before scanning it yourself.
