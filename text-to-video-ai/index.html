Text to Video AI

By the Funtasha team · Published 20 November 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026

Text-to-video AI turns a written description into a short moving clip, and the words you type decide almost everything about the result. This page gives you a working prompt builder, the formula behind a good prompt, and eight examples you can copy and try.

This page builds prompts — it does not make videos. Paste the prompt into a video tool such as Flow, Kling, PixVerse, or Runway, and the tool makes the clip. Never paste a prompt into a site you do not trust, and never pay for a prompt that is free here.

What text-to-video can and cannot do

Text-to-video AI does one thing: it reads a written description and makes a short moving clip from it. You type "a street food vendor flipping noodles in a busy night market", and a few moments later you have a few seconds of moving pictures. It is the fastest way to get footage you do not own a camera for, and it is the reason searches for AI video generation are up 344% year on year as of November 2026.

What it can do well is single shots: one subject, one action, one camera move, a few seconds long. A lighthouse in a storm. A product turning on a table. A cat chasing a butterfly. These are the clips that come out clean, because the tool only has to hold one idea steady for a few seconds. Free tools make clips of 5 to 15 seconds, and Google Flow makes 8-second clips — that short length is not a flaw, it is the shape of the medium.

What it cannot do is a story. Ask for a character who walks into a room, picks up a letter, reads it, and cries, and the tool will stumble — faces warp, hands melt, the letter becomes a book and then a bird. Long scenes, dialogue, and precise hand movements are beyond what today's free tools do well. The fix is to think in shots, not scenes: one clip per idea, and stitch the clips together afterwards in CapCut, which is free.

It also cannot read your mind. A prompt that says "a nice video" gives you whatever the tool thinks nice means. A prompt that names the subject, the action, the camera, the light, and the style gives you something close to what you imagined. The builder at the top of this page exists for exactly that reason: it turns your choices into a prompt with all five parts in it.

The prompt formula: subject, action, camera, light, style

Every strong text-to-video prompt follows the same shape, and once you know the shape, you can build a good prompt for any clip. The shape is five parts, in one or two sentences.

  • Subject. Who or what is in the clip, and what is it doing? A street food vendor flipping noodles. A lighthouse in a storm. A red bicycle leaning against a wall. Be specific: a sleeping cat is a different clip from a jumping cat.
  • Action. What moves? The vendor flips the noodles. The waves crash. The bicycle stays still while the camera moves. One clear action per clip — two actions fight each other, and the clip falls apart.
  • Camera. How does the camera move? Static, pan, dolly zoom, orbit, or crane. Naming the move is the fastest way to make a clip feel like film instead of a slideshow.
  • Light. What time is it, and where does the light come from? Warm sunset light, soft morning light, neon night light. Light sets the mood faster than any other word.
  • Style. How should it look? Cinematic, cartoon, realistic, or a clean product-video look. One style word changes everything, so pick it on purpose.

Put them together and you have a prompt: a subject, doing an action, in a place, with a camera move, in a light, in a style. The builder above does exactly that — fill in the subject and the setting, pick a camera move, a style, and a mood, and it assembles the sentence for you.

Camera moves, in plain words

The camera move is the part beginners skip, and it is the part that makes a clip feel professional. Five moves cover almost everything a free tool does well.

Static means the camera does not move at all. The subject moves, the world moves, the camera stays put. Static is the safest move for a first clip, and the best choice when the subject itself is doing something interesting — a vendor flipping noodles, a blacksmith at work, a child blowing out candles.

Pan means the camera sweeps slowly from one side to the other. A pan suits landscapes, streets, and rooms: it shows the place, not just the subject. Keep it slow — a fast pan turns into a blur on a free tool.

Dolly zoom means the camera moves slowly toward the subject, or the lens zooms in. It is the move that says "look closer". It suits product shots, portraits, and any moment where the detail matters more than the scene.

Orbit means the camera circles around the subject. It is the most dramatic of the five, and the hardest for a free tool to hold steady — use it for short clips, with one subject, in an open space. When it works, it looks like a film shot.

Crane means the camera rises above the scene, like a bird taking off. It suits openings and endings: a market waking up, a beach at dawn, a town at night. Start low, end high, and let the clip breathe.

Run one, then change a single word — swap the mood, or the camera move — and run it again. Watch how the clip changes. That small experiment teaches more than any list of rules, because you see the cause and the effect side by side.

When the clip comes out wrong

Every prompt writer has watched a clip come out wrong. The face melts halfway through. The hands grow extra fingers. The subject warps into something else, or the background ripples like water. It happens to everyone, and it is fixable — but only if you change one thing at a time.

Warping usually means the prompt asked for too much motion. A subject that runs, jumps, and turns in five seconds is three actions, and the tool cannot hold them all. Cut it to one action, and the warping usually stops. Melting faces usually mean the face is too small in the frame, or the camera moves too fast — slow the camera down, or bring the subject closer.

The biggest mistake is rewriting the whole prompt after a bad result. If you change five words at once and the next clip is better, you have no idea which word fixed it. Change one word, run it again, and look. That is how you learn what each word does, and it is much faster than guessing in the dark.

And if a prompt keeps failing in one tool, try the same words in another. Flow, Kling, PixVerse, and Runway read the same prompt differently, and a clip that fails in one often works in another. Kling is strongest for realistic people, PixVerse for effects — match the tool to the clip, not just the prompt to the tool.

Where to paste your prompt

The builder on this page writes the prompt. The video is made by the tool you paste it into, and four free tools take plain text prompts well.

Google Flow is the best free start: 50 free credits a day, five 8-second clips, and no visible watermark on most free clips. Paste your prompt, pick 9:16 for a vertical clip or 16:9 for a wide one, and generate. Kling is the pick when the clip needs a realistic person in it — free daily credits, with a watermark on free exports, and paid plans from about $7–10/mo. PixVerse is the effects pick: 60 starter credits plus 30 a day, free clips up to 540p, watermarked. Runway offers a one-time trial of 125 credits, watermarked, with paid plans from $12–15/mo — save it for a day when you have a clear plan, because the trial does not refill.

Whichever tool you choose, the routine is the same: paste the prompt, check the length and the shape, generate once, and watch the whole clip before you generate again. A prompt that looks right on paper can still surprise you on screen, and the surprise is usually worth one more try with a single word changed.

From one clip to a finished video

One clip is a shot. A video is shots in a row, and the step from one to the other is where most free videos are actually made. No free tool makes a long video in one click — the honest workflow is to plan the video as a row of short clips, generate one clip per idea, and stitch them together in CapCut, which is free and built for exactly this.

Start with the plan, not the prompt. A 30-second video is three or four clips: an opening shot, two middle shots, and a closing shot. Write one prompt per shot, using the builder above, and keep the style and the mood the same across all of them — that consistency is what makes separate clips feel like one video.

Then assemble. In CapCut, put the clips in order, trim the rough edges, and add captions — captions matter, because most people watch short video with the sound off. Cut to 9:16 for TikTok, Reels, and Shorts. Add music if the tool offers it, or keep the video silent and let the captions carry it. The finished video will not look like it came from a studio, but it will look like it came from someone who planned it — and that is what viewers notice.

Questions people ask

Does this page make the video for me?

No. This page builds a prompt — the words you paste into a video tool. The video itself is made by the tool you choose, such as Flow, Kling, PixVerse, or Runway. Think of this page as the scriptwriter, not the camera: it writes the words, and the tool turns them into moving pictures.

How long should a text-to-video prompt be?

One or two sentences is enough — long enough to name the subject, the action, the camera move, the light, and the style, but short enough that the main idea stays clear. Very long prompts confuse the tool, because it has to weigh every word at once. Start with the five parts in the formula, and add detail only where it matters.

Why do faces melt in AI video?

Faces are the hardest thing for a video model to hold steady, because a face moves in dozens of small ways at once. Melting usually means the face is too small in the frame, the camera moves too fast, or the prompt asks for too much action. Slow the camera down, bring the subject closer, and ask for one action per clip.

Which free tool is best for text-to-video?

Google Flow is the best free start: 50 free credits a day, five 8-second clips, and no visible watermark on most free clips. Kling is the better pick when the clip needs a realistic person in it, and PixVerse is the better pick when the clip needs an effect. All three take the same plain-text prompts, so one prompt can be tried in each.

How long is a free AI video clip?

Most free generators make clips of 5 to 15 seconds. Google Flow makes 8-second clips, and Google Vids makes clips of 3 to 10 seconds. A longer video is made by planning it as a row of short clips, generating one clip per scene, and stitching them together in CapCut with narration or captions over the top.

Can I use the same prompt in every tool?

Yes, the same words work in every tool, because all four read plain descriptions the same way. The clips will still come out different, because each tool has its own style — so a prompt that shines in Flow may need a small tweak in Kling. Try the same prompt in two tools before deciding it is a bad prompt.
