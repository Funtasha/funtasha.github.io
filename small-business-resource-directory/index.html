How to use this directory

This directory gathers resources a small business owner actually needs, grouped by what they help with: money, rules, customers, tools and learning. The three cards above link to official government business sites in the United States, Australia and the United Kingdom, and the sections below explain what each kind of resource is for and how to use it.

Start with the section that matches your current problem. If money is the problem, start with finance basics. If you are unsure what the rules require, start with legal information. If you need customers, start with marketing. You do not need to read the whole directory, and you do not need every resource on your first day.

Finance basics: money in, money out

Every small business needs the same few money habits: a separate account for the business, a record of money in and money out, a way to send invoices, and a plan for taxes. These habits matter more than any single tool, because they keep you honest about whether the business is earning or losing money.

The official business sites in the cards above all carry free finance guides written for small owners, covering cash flow, pricing, bookkeeping and tax basics. Your country's tax office also publishes free guides: the IRS in the United States, HMRC in the United Kingdom, and the ATO in Australia all explain what small businesses must record and when they must file.

When the business grows past simple records, a bookkeeper or accountant earns their fee quickly. They catch mistakes, remind you of deadlines, and answer questions a guide cannot. Ask other small owners in your area for a recommendation before you hire one, and agree the fee in writing before work starts.

Legal information: the rules of the road

Legal information for small businesses covers the same ground everywhere: choosing a business structure, registering a name, understanding licences, writing contracts, and keeping records. The rules differ by country and often by state or region, so always check the official source for your own area rather than copying what a business in another country does.

The government business sites linked above explain the common structures, such as sole trader, partnership and company, and what each one means for tax and liability. They also explain licences: which kinds of business need one, where to apply, and what happens if you trade without one. Read the official pages for your country before you spend money on anything else.

For contracts and legal documents, free templates are a starting point, not a finished answer. A template written for another country may use the wrong law, and a template written for a large company may not fit a one-person business. Use templates to learn what a document should cover, then get local advice for the documents that matter most, such as partnership agreements and employment contracts.

Marketing: finding your first customers

Marketing for a small business starts with one question: where do your customers already look? A local service is found through local search and word of mouth. A handmade product is found through marketplaces and social media. A business service is found through referrals and a clear website. Spend your effort where your customers already are, not where marketing articles tell you to be.

Free marketing comes first: a clear website with your services and prices, a business listing on the map service your customers use, and asking happy customers to tell their friends. Paid advertising comes later, once you know which message brings customers, because paying to show a message that does not work only spends money faster.

The government business sites carry free marketing guides, including how to write a simple marketing plan and how to research competitors. Your local chamber of commerce often runs free or low-cost workshops on marketing for small businesses, and they are a good place to meet other owners facing the same problems.

Tools and software for small teams

A small business needs only a handful of tools: a way to send invoices, a way to keep track of customers, a way to store files, and a way to plan work. Free versions of all four exist, and a new business should start free and pay only when the free version limits real work.

Choose tools that work on a phone, because much of small business life happens away from a desk. Choose tools that let you take your data with you, because switching tools later is painful if your customer list is locked inside one of them. And choose fewer tools rather than more: three tools you use every day beat ten tools you forget about.

Before paying for any tool, try it for two weeks on real work. A tool that looks good in a demonstration may not fit the way you actually work. If the trial ends and you have not used it, do not pay for it. The best tool is the one you open without thinking about it.

Learning and mentoring

Running a small business is a skill, and skills improve with teaching and practice. Free courses for small owners cover bookkeeping, marketing, pricing and hiring, and many are written by the same government agencies that publish the official business guides. A short course taken before a decision is cheaper than a mistake made without one.

A mentor is worth more than a course when the question is specific to your business. SCORE, a United States mentoring network, pairs new owners with experienced mentors for free one-to-one sessions, and its site is score.org. Many chambers of commerce run similar mentoring schemes, and local business networks often match new owners with people who have run a business for years.

Ask for help early, not when a problem is already serious. Mentors and advisers would rather answer a small question in ten minutes than untangle a big problem in ten hours. The owners who ask early are the ones who last.

Help close to home

Some of the best help for a small business is local. Your chamber of commerce knows the businesses in your area and the rules that apply to them. Your local council or municipality runs business support services, including licence advice and sometimes small grants. Your public library often has a business desk with market research, company records and free meeting rooms.

Local help has one advantage no website can match: the people there know your town. They know which streets get foot traffic, which suppliers are reliable, and which local rules catch new businesses out. A single conversation with a local adviser can save weeks of guessing.

To find local help, search for your town or city name together with business support, chamber of commerce, or small business advice. If your area has a business improvement district or a traders association, join it: the members are your neighbours, and they have already solved many of the problems you are about to meet.

Free help versus paid help

Free help is real and plentiful: government guides, library resources, mentoring networks and community workshops. It covers the basics well, and a new business should use all of it before paying for advice. Free help has one limit: it is general, and your business is specific.

Paid help earns its fee when the question is specific and the stakes are high. An accountant before a tax deadline, a lawyer before signing a lease, a designer before launching a brand. Pay for advice when a wrong answer would cost more than the fee, and agree the fee in writing before the work starts.

Be careful of paid help that promises fast results. Nobody can promise your business will grow, your taxes will vanish, or your marketing will work. Honest advisers explain what they will do, what it will cost, and what is outside their control. Anyone who promises a guaranteed outcome is selling, not advising.

How to judge a resource before you trust it

Not every resource that calls itself a guide is worth trusting. Check three things before you follow advice. First, who wrote it: a government agency, a known organisation, or a stranger selling a course. Second, when it was written: tax rules and platform features change, and old advice can be wrong. Third, whether it matches the official source for your country.

Be careful with advice that asks for money before it explains anything, advice that quotes no source, and advice that promises a specific result. Good resources name their sources, admit what they do not cover, and point you to the official pages where you can check for yourself.

When two resources disagree, trust the official one. Government business sites and tax offices publish the rules as they stand, and they update them when the rules change. A blog post or a video may be easier to read, but the official page is the one that counts.

A simple starting checklist

  1. Open a separate bank account for the business, and keep business money apart from personal money.
  2. Write down what you sell, who buys it, and what it costs you to provide it.
  3. Check the official business site for your country for the licences and registrations you need.
  4. Set up a simple way to send invoices and record money in and money out.
  5. Make a one-page plan for finding customers, starting with the free methods.
  6. Find one local source of help, such as a chamber of commerce or a library business desk.
  7. Book a short session with a mentor or adviser before your first big decision.

Questions people ask

Where should a new small business owner start?

With the official business site for your country, linked in the cards above. It explains the structure, licences and tax basics for your area, and it is free. After that, find one local source of help, such as a chamber of commerce.

Are government business resources really free?

Yes. The government business sites in the United States, Australia and the United Kingdom publish free guides on starting, running and growing a business. They also list free local services, such as mentoring and workshops.

Do I need an accountant for a small business?

Not on day one, but most owners benefit from one as the business grows. An accountant catches mistakes, reminds you of deadlines and answers questions a guide cannot. Ask other local owners for a recommendation.

What is SCORE?

SCORE is a United States mentoring network that pairs new business owners with experienced mentors for free one-to-one sessions. Its website is score.org. Similar mentoring schemes exist through chambers of commerce in many countries.

How do I know if business advice is trustworthy?

Check who wrote it, when it was written, and whether it matches the official source for your country. Be careful with advice that asks for money up front, quotes no source, or promises a guaranteed result.

What is the cheapest way to market a small business?

Start free: a clear website, a business listing on the map service your customers use, and asking happy customers to spread the word. Pay for advertising only after you know which message brings customers.
