Assistive Technology Comparison Guide

Assistive technology is any tool that helps a disabled person do something they could not do otherwise, from reading a screen to controlling a computer by voice. This guide compares the main kinds, explains who each one helps, and shows how to choose. Nothing here is for sale, and no price is quoted as fact.

What this is and who needs it

Assistive technology is any device, piece of software or tool that helps a disabled person carry out a task. It ranges from simple low-tech aids, like a magnifying glass or a large-print keyboard, to complex software that turns speech into text or follows the user's eyes. This guide compares the main kinds, explains what each one does and who it helps, and shows how to choose. It is for disabled people and their families, for teachers, employers and carers, and for anyone choosing a tool for someone else.

The most important idea in this guide is that the person comes before the product. Two people with the same condition may need completely different tools, because their tasks, their homes, their work and their habits differ. A screen reader that suits a programmer may frustrate a person who only wants to read the news. A voice control system that works in a quiet office may fail in a noisy workshop. Start with the person and the task, and the right tool usually becomes clear.

Assistive technology is not only for people with a diagnosed disability. Many tools help older people, people recovering from injury, and people working in difficult conditions. The captions on a video, the voice control on a phone and the large text setting on a tablet are all assistive technology, and millions of people use them without ever calling them that. Good tools help everyone; the label matters less than the fit.

How it works, step by step

Choosing assistive technology starts with a clear list of tasks. Write down what the person needs to do: read a screen, write a message, make a call, move around the house, join a meeting, or study for an exam. Be specific, because a vague goal like use the computer leads to a vague choice. A specific goal like read and reply to messages on a phone points to a short list of tools that can be compared properly.

Next, look at what is already available. Most phones, tablets and computers now include screen readers, magnifiers, voice control, large text and high-contrast modes for free. Try those first, because they cost nothing and they are already on the device the person knows. If the built-in tools are not enough, look at the specialist tools for the task: a dedicated screen reader, a braille display, a switch system, or an eye-tracking device. Read reviews from people who use the tool every day, and prefer honest accounts over sales material.

Then try the shortlisted tools in a real setting, not just in a shop. A tool that feels fine for ten minutes in a demonstration room may tire the user after an hour at home. Ask for a home trial where one is offered, and give the person time to learn the tool properly before judging it. Finally, get a professional assessment if the choice is important or expensive: an occupational therapist or an assistive technology specialist can rule out tools that look good but do not fit, and can suggest options you had not considered.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Costs vary enormously. Many built-in tools on phones and computers cost nothing, and some excellent specialist software is free. At the other end, dedicated hardware such as a braille display or an eye-tracking device can cost a great deal. This guide quotes no prices, because they change and they differ by country. Before you pay for anything, check whether a charity, an employer, a school, a health service or a government scheme in your country can help with the cost. Many people pay for tools that would have been provided free if they had asked.

Time is a cost too. Learning a screen reader or a voice control system takes weeks of practice, not an afternoon, and the first days can be frustrating. Budget time for learning, and expect the person to be slower at first than they were with the old way of doing things. That is normal, and it passes. Training from someone who knows the tool well speeds the process greatly, so ask the supplier or a local charity about lessons.

Before you start, gather the facts: the person's tasks, their device, their home and work setting, and any other tools they already use. Note what has been tried before and why it did or did not work, because that history saves time. If the person has a professional involved, such as a therapist or a teacher, bring them into the conversation early. The best choices are made by a small team around the person, not by one person shopping alone.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is buying the most expensive tool in the hope that price means quality. It does not. The best tool is the one that fits the person and the task, and it is often a free built-in feature rather than a costly specialist device. Start cheap, start simple, and spend more only when the simple option has genuinely failed. The second mistake is choosing a tool without trying it. A tool that looks impressive in a video may be awkward in real use, and a tool that looks plain may fit perfectly. Always try before you buy.

The third mistake is ignoring the setting. A voice control system needs a quiet room, an eye tracker needs steady light, and a screen magnifier needs a large screen. A tool chosen without thinking about where it will be used will disappoint, however good it is on paper. The fourth mistake is forgetting the person who will use it. A tool chosen by someone else, without the user's involvement, is often abandoned, however well-meant the choice was. The user must be part of the decision from the start.

The fifth mistake is skipping training. A powerful tool without training is like a piano without lessons: the user will play one tune and blame the instrument. Budget for lessons, practice time and follow-up support. The sixth mistake is buying once and never reviewing. Needs change, software updates, and new tools appear. Review the choice every year or so, and be ready to change when a better fit comes along.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this list when choosing any assistive tool. It keeps the decision grounded in the person, not the catalogue.

  • Write down the tasks the person needs to do, in plain words.
  • Try the free built-in tools on the person's own device first.
  • Shortlist two or three tools built for the task, and read reviews from daily users.
  • Try each shortlisted tool in a real setting for several days, not just in a shop.
  • Check the setting: light, noise, space, power and internet where the tool will be used.
  • Ask a professional, such as an occupational therapist, for an assessment if the choice matters.
  • Check whether a charity, employer, school or government scheme can help with the cost.
  • Budget time and money for training, practice and follow-up support.
  • Make sure the person who will use the tool is part of the decision.
  • Review the choice after a few months, and again each year.

If a tool fails the trial, that is useful information, not a failure. Cross it off the list and try the next one. The goal is a tool the person actually uses, not a tool that gathers dust on a shelf.

Tips from real practice

Start with the device the person already knows. A new tool on a familiar phone is easier to learn than a familiar tool on a new device, because the person only has to learn one new thing at a time. If a new device is unavoidable, allow extra learning time and expect extra frustration at first. Patience in the first weeks pays off for years.

Join a community of users. Online groups, local charities and user forums are full of people who have solved the exact problem you face, and their advice is usually more honest than a sales page. They will tell you which tools last, which break, which suppliers answer the phone, and which features matter in daily use. A ten-minute conversation with an experienced user can save weeks of research.

Finally, respect the user's choice, even when you disagree. A person may prefer a slower tool they enjoy to a faster tool they hate, or a simple tool they control to a clever tool that confuses them. The tool serves the person, not the other way round. If the user loves a tool and it does the job, that is the right tool, whatever the catalogue says. Independence and dignity matter more than speed, and a tool the person chooses is a tool the person will use.

Questions people ask

What is assistive technology?

Assistive technology is any device, software or tool that helps a disabled person carry out a task, from screen readers and hearing aids to adapted keyboards and voice control. It ranges from simple low-tech aids, like a magnifying glass, to complex software that turns speech into text. The best tool is the one that fits the person and the task, not the most expensive one.

What tools help blind computer users?

Screen readers such as NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver and TalkBack turn the screen into speech or braille. Braille displays show the same information under the fingertips, and screen magnifiers help people with some remaining vision. Most computers and phones now include these tools for free, so start with what your device already offers.

What helps people who cannot use a mouse?

Voice control software lets a person operate a computer by speaking commands, and eye-tracking devices follow the user's gaze. Switches, large-key keyboards, head pointers and mouth sticks give other ways to point and click. Keyboard shortcuts and a well-organised screen also help, because many tasks can be done without a mouse at all.

How do I choose the right assistive tool?

Start with the task, not the catalogue: write down what the person needs to do, then look for tools built for that task. Try the tool in a real setting for a few days, not just in a shop, and ask the person using it what feels easy and what feels tiring. A professional assessment can save money by ruling out tools that look good but do not fit.

Where can I try assistive technology?

Many disability charities, libraries and independent living centres run demonstration rooms where you can try tools for free. Some suppliers offer home trials, and online communities share honest reviews from people who use the tools every day. Always try before you buy, because a tool that looks impressive in a video may not suit the person who will use it.

Is assistive technology expensive?

It varies enormously: many built-in tools on phones and computers cost nothing, while specialist hardware can cost a great deal. This guide quotes no prices, because they change and they differ by country. Check whether a charity, an employer, a school or a government scheme in your country can help with the cost before you pay for anything yourself.
