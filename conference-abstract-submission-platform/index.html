    How abstract review works

    A conference abstract is a short summary of the work you want to present, usually a few hundred words, and it is the document reviewers use to decide whether your work belongs in the programme. Most conferences receive far more abstracts than they have places, so reviewers read quickly and decide fast. Your abstract is competing for attention with dozens or hundreds of others, and clarity is what makes it stand out.

    Reviewers typically read for four things: is the topic relevant to the conference, is the question or problem clear, is the method sound, and is there a result or a finding worth hearing about? They read in the order you write, so the first two sentences carry more weight than the last two. An abstract that buries its point in the final sentence is an abstract the reviewer may never reach.

    Review is usually anonymous in one direction or both, and reviewers follow a scoring sheet rather than their mood — but a scoring sheet still rewards clear writing, because a reviewer can only score what they understand. Write for a tired reviewer reading their fiftieth abstract of the day: short sentences, plain words, one idea per sentence, and the point up front. That is not dumbing down; it is respect for the reader's time.

    The structure that gets accepted

    Most accepted abstracts follow the same shape, and it is worth learning because reviewers expect it. The shape has four parts: background, method, results, and conclusion — sometimes called the four moves. Each part gets a sentence or two, and together they tell the whole story of the work in miniature.

    1. Background: what is the problem, and why does it matter? One or two sentences, no more.
    2. Method: what did you do? Name the approach, the data, or the design in plain words.
    3. Results: what did you find? Give the main finding, not every finding, and be specific.
    4. Conclusion: what does it mean? Say what the finding changes, or what should happen next.

    Not every abstract needs all four parts in equal measure — a work-in-progress abstract may have more background and less result, and a methods abstract may lead with the approach — but the shape is a useful check. If your draft has no result at all, ask yourself what the audience will learn from your talk. If it has no background, ask yourself why anyone should care. The builder's sentence count helps here: a draft with fewer than three sentences almost certainly misses a part of the shape.

    The title deserves the same care as the abstract. A good title names the topic and hints at the finding, in words a non-specialist can follow. Avoid titles that promise more than the work delivers, titles that are clever but unclear, and titles so long they need a second breath. The builder checks that a title is present, but only you can judge whether it is a good one.

    Writing clearly: words that work

    Clear abstract writing is mostly about short sentences and honest words. Aim for sentences of about twenty words or fewer, one idea per sentence, and verbs that say what happened rather than what was done to what. Compare the two: 'An investigation was conducted into the effects of the intervention' and 'We tested whether the intervention worked.' The second is shorter, clearer and easier to remember — and it uses fewer of your precious words.

    Cut the words that add nothing: 'very', 'really', 'in order to', 'it is important to note that', 'a number of'. Each one costs words and adds no meaning. Cut jargon that your audience does not share, and define any term you must use on first use. If a sentence needs a second reading, rewrite it — the reviewer will not give it one.

    Be specific where it counts. 'Many participants improved' says almost nothing; the number, the measure and the direction say everything. You do not need to pack the abstract with numbers, but the main finding deserves one. And be honest about limits: an abstract that claims more than the work supports will be found out in review, or worse, in the question session after your talk.

    Common mistakes that get abstracts rejected

    Reviewers reject abstracts for a short list of reasons, and most of them are avoidable. The commonest is promising results you do not have: an abstract that says 'results will be discussed' tells the reviewer there is nothing to judge yet, and reviewers judge what is in front of them. If the work is genuinely unfinished, say what you have so far and what the talk will cover — honestly, not hopefully.

    • Promising results you do not have — reviewers score the work in front of them, not the work you hope to finish.
    • Ignoring the word limit — a 500-word draft for a 250-word limit shows you did not read the instructions.
    • No clear question — an abstract that describes a topic without a question gives the reviewer nothing to evaluate.
    • Too much background, no result — the reader reaches the end knowing the field but not your finding.
    • Jargon without explanation — reviewers outside your narrow area cannot score what they cannot understand.
    • Copying the paper — an abstract is a summary written for the conference, not the first page of the paper pasted in.
    • Typos and sloppy formatting — small errors signal carelessness, and reviewers notice.

    The word limit deserves a special mention, because it is the easiest mistake to avoid and one of the commonest. Conferences set limits for a reason — the programme book has finite space, and reviewers read many abstracts. A draft over the limit may be cut off mid-sentence by the submission system, or rejected without reading. The builder shows your word count against the limit as you type, so there is no excuse for missing it.

    What happens after you submit

    After you submit through the conference's own system, your abstract goes to reviewers, who score it and send their scores to the programme committee. The committee decides which abstracts become talks, which become posters, and which are not accepted. That decision usually takes weeks, and the wait is normal — do not assume silence means rejection.

    If your abstract is accepted as a talk, you will usually receive the reviewers' comments along with the decision. Read them carefully, even the critical ones, because they tell you what the audience will ask about. If it is accepted as a poster, treat it as a real opportunity: posters reach people who skip talks, and the conversations at a poster are often the best part of a conference.

    If your abstract is rejected, read the comments, wait a day, then decide what to do. Sometimes the work needs more time and the conference was the wrong fit; sometimes a rewrite with a clearer question and a stronger result will succeed at the next event. Rejection of an abstract is not rejection of the work — reviewers judge a short summary under time pressure, and good work is rejected for bad reasons more often than anyone admits.

    A final check before you send it

    Before you paste your abstract into the conference's submission form, run through this final check. Read the abstract aloud — if a sentence is hard to say, it is hard to read. Check the word count against the limit one last time, and check the title. Read the conference's instructions again: have you followed the format, the sections, and any rules about author names or references?

    Ask someone who does not know your work to read the abstract and tell you, in their own words, what the work is about and what it found. If they cannot, the abstract is not clear enough yet, and another round of editing will help more than another round of worrying. A fresh reader catches what the writer cannot see, because the writer already knows what the abstract means.

    Then save your draft, copy it, and submit it through the conference's own system. Keep a copy of exactly what you submitted, with the date, so you can match it to the reviewers' comments later. And remember what the builder cannot check: that the work is honest, the claims are supported, and the abstract says what you actually did. That part is yours alone.

    Questions people ask

    What is a conference abstract?

    A conference abstract is a short summary of the work you want to present, usually a few hundred words, that reviewers use to decide whether your work belongs in the programme. It states the problem, the method, the main finding and what it means, in miniature. Think of it as the whole story of your work told in a few paragraphs, written for a reviewer who has many abstracts to read and little time.

    How long should it be?

    As long as the conference says — and no longer. Most conferences set a word limit, commonly between 150 and 500 words, and the limit is part of the instructions, not a suggestion. A draft over the limit may be cut off by the submission system or rejected without reading. Use the builder on this page to watch your word count against the limit as you type.

    How are abstracts reviewed?

    Reviewers read each abstract and score it against a set of criteria, usually covering relevance, clarity of the question, soundness of the method, and the value of the result. The programme committee then uses the scores to decide which abstracts become talks, which become posters, and which are not accepted. Review is often anonymous, and reviewers read many abstracts, so clarity and a point made early matter a great deal.

    What do reviewers look for?

    Reviewers look for a clear question, a sound method, a real result, and a reason the work matters to the conference audience. They look for all four in the order you write, so the first two sentences carry more weight than the last two. They also notice honesty: claims the work supports, limits stated plainly, and writing that respects the reader's time.

    How do I submit one?

    You submit through the conference's own submission system — usually an online form on the conference website, opened for a set submission period. This page does not submit anything: it helps you draft and check your abstract, and you paste the finished draft into the conference's form yourself. Read the conference's instructions carefully before you submit, including the format, the deadline and any rules about author names.

    What if my abstract is rejected?

    Read the reviewers' comments, wait a day, then decide what to do. Sometimes the work needs more time, sometimes the conference was the wrong fit, and sometimes a rewrite with a clearer question and a stronger result will succeed at the next event. Rejection of an abstract is not rejection of the work — reviewers judge a short summary under time pressure, and good work is rejected for bad reasons more often than anyone admits.
