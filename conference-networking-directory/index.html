How to use this guide

This guide explains how conference networking actually works: who to look for, how to start a conversation with a stranger, what to say after hello, and how to follow up so a chat becomes a contact. It is written for anyone who attends conferences — first-timers who have never networked, and experienced attendees who collect business cards and never use them. It carries no list of attendees, because a conference is made of people you meet, not people you look up.

Work through the guide in order if networking feels new: who to look for first, then how to start, then how to follow up. If you already meet people easily but never follow up, jump to the follow-up section, which is where most networking fails. The common mistakes at the end are worth reading whatever your level, because they are the habits that waste the most time.

One idea runs through the whole guide: networking is not collecting people, it is starting conversations that help both sides. The best networkers at a conference are not the loudest or the best connected — they are the people who listen well, ask good questions, and follow up afterwards. If you can do those three things, you can network.

Who to look for

Before the conference, decide who you want to meet and why. A vague goal — 'meet people' — produces vague results, while a specific goal produces real conversations. Think in three groups: people who do what you do, people who do what you want to learn, and people who decide things you care about. A short list of ten people, with a reason for each one, is worth more than a hall full of strangers.

Look for people in the programme: speakers on topics you care about, panel members, workshop leaders, and exhibitors whose work touches yours. Look at the attendee list if the conference publishes one, and note the names that matter to you. And look beyond the famous names — the most useful conversation at a conference is often with the person sitting next to you, who faces the same problems you do.

During the conference, use the natural meeting points: the coffee queue, the lunch table, the workshop you attend, and the end of a talk you both enjoyed. These are the places where starting a conversation is normal, because everyone there has something in common already. The best opening is the simplest: you are both here, you both chose the same session, and that is enough to start talking.

Do not only talk to people you already know. Conferences are one of the few places where approaching a stranger is expected and welcome, and the people you already know will still be there tomorrow. Set yourself a small target — three new people a day, say — and treat it as part of the conference, not an extra.

Starting a conversation

Starting a conversation with a stranger feels harder than it is, because the first sentence does all the work and the rest follows. The best first sentences are simple, honest and about something you share: the talk you just heard, the session you are both waiting for, or the reason you both came. 'What did you think of that talk?' works better than any clever line, because it asks for an opinion, and people like giving theirs.

Introduce yourself briefly and early: your name, what you do, and why you are here — in one sentence, not a speech. Then ask about the other person, and listen to the answer. People remember the conversations where they were listened to, and they forget the ones where they were talked at. Your goal in the first minute is not to impress; it is to find out whether you have something worth talking about.

Body language matters more than words at the start. Stand where people can join you, keep your hands free of a phone, and look approachable — a smile and an open stance invite conversation, while a turned back and a phone screen close it off. If you see two people deep in conversation, wait for a pause rather than interrupting; if you see someone standing alone, they are usually glad to be approached.

If a conversation does not take off, that is fine — not every stranger becomes a contact, and a polite exit is part of networking too. 'It was good to meet you — I am going to catch the next session' is enough. Move on without guilt: the next conversation may be the one that matters.

What to say after hello

After the introductions, the conversation needs a question that goes somewhere. The best questions are open ones — they cannot be answered with a single word — and they are about the other person's work or experience, not about yours. 'What are you working on at the moment?' and 'What brought you to this conference?' both work, because they invite a real answer.

Listen for the detail you can build on. If they mention a project, a problem or a place, ask about it: what is hard about it, what they have tried, what they would do differently. People open up when they feel heard, and a conversation built on their answers feels natural in a way a list of prepared questions never does. Take notes afterwards if the conversation matters — a name, a topic, a promise — because you will meet many people and memory fades fast.

Share something of your own, but briefly. A conversation is a trade, not an interview: after you have asked two or three questions, offer something back — what you do, what you are working on, a problem you share. The balance matters: too little sharing and you seem secretive; too much and you seem to be pitching. Aim for roughly half and half, and let the other person set the depth.

End the conversation well. When it has run its course, say what you enjoyed about it, suggest a next step if there is one — an email, a coffee, an introduction — and exchange contact details. A good ending turns a chat into a contact; a conversation that just trails off is forgotten by the next session.

The follow-up that turns a chat into a contact

The follow-up is where networking succeeds or fails, and it is where most people stop. A conversation without a follow-up is a pleasant memory that fades within a week. A conversation with a follow-up is a contact — someone who knows your name, remembers meeting you, and will answer your next message.

Follow up within a day or two, while the meeting is still fresh in both minds. Send a short message: where you met, what you talked about, and one thing that follows from it — an article you mentioned, an introduction you offered, or a question you want to ask. Keep it short and specific. A message that says 'great to meet you' and nothing else is easy to ignore; a message that continues the conversation is easy to answer.

If you promised something during the conversation — an introduction, a link, a document — send it with the follow-up, or as soon as you can afterwards. Promises kept are the foundation of trust, and a kept promise in the first message sets the tone for everything that follows. If you promised nothing, the follow-up itself is enough: it says you meant what you said when you said it was good to meet them.

After the first follow-up, stay in touch lightly. A message every few months — an article they might like, a question, a congratulation — keeps the contact alive without becoming a burden. Networking is not a single event; it is a habit of staying in touch with people whose work you respect, over years, not days.

Approaching speakers and busy people

Approaching a speaker after their talk is normal and welcome — speakers expect it, and most of them enjoy it. The best time is just after the session, when the speaker is still near the stage, or at the coffee break that follows. Start with something specific about the talk: a point that struck you, a question it raised, or how it connects to your own work. Speakers remember the people who engaged with their ideas, not the people who only said the talk was good.

Keep it brief and respect their time. A speaker at a busy conference may have a queue of people waiting, a next session to reach, or a flight to catch. Say what you want to say in a minute or two, ask your question, and offer a next step — an email, a coffee later — rather than holding them in the corridor. If they are clearly in a hurry, say so and let them go; a gracious exit leaves a better impression than a long monologue.

The same approach works for anyone busy or senior: be specific, be brief, and make it easy for them to respond. A message that says 'I enjoyed your talk on this topic and I have one question about it' is far easier to answer than a message that says 'I would love to pick your brain sometime'. Busy people say yes to small, clear requests and no to vague ones.

If you are shy, use the structure of the conference to help you. Ask a question in a session, join a workshop, volunteer to help — all of these put you next to people with a reason to talk. And remember that most people at a conference feel the same awkwardness you do; the person you approach is usually relieved, not annoyed, that someone spoke first.

Common mistakes

The mistakes that waste conference networking are few, and they are all habits rather than accidents. Learn them once and your conferences will be more useful for years.

  • Collecting 50 business cards and emailing none — a stack of cards is not a network; the follow-up is.
  • Talking only to people you already know — the friends you arrived with will still be there tomorrow; the strangers will not.
  • Pitching instead of conversing — a monologue about your product is a reason to walk away, not to stay.
  • No goal for the conference — wandering without a list of people to meet or questions to ask.
  • Forgetting names and details — meeting someone interesting and remembering nothing about them by evening.
  • Monopolising a speaker — a ten-minute corridor conversation that blocks everyone else waiting.
  • Never following up — the conversation that ends at the conference ends everywhere.

The thread running through all of these is simple: networking is a conversation followed by a message, and both parts matter. Meet people with curiosity, listen more than you talk, and follow up within a day or two. Do that at every conference, and the network builds itself — slowly, honestly, and in a way that lasts.
