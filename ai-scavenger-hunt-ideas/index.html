How to Plan a Scavenger Hunt with AI

By the Funtasha team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

A scavenger hunt is one of the easiest parties to run, and AI can write the clues for you in minutes. This guide gives you clue-writing prompt patterns, indoor and outdoor hunt plans, age-group adjustments, and twelve ready clues you can use straight away.

A scavenger hunt turns an ordinary house, garden, or park into an adventure. Players follow clues from one hiding place to the next, and the excitement builds with every find. The hard part is usually writing the clues, and that is exactly where AI helps.

With a good prompt, an AI chatbot can write rhyming clues, riddles, and picture clues matched to your home, your garden, or your party theme. You check the clues, hide the items, and the hunt runs itself. This guide shows you how, from the first clue to the final prize.

Keep hunts safe: choose hiding places that do not need climbing, reaching into hot or sharp places, or going near roads. For outdoor hunts, set clear boundaries, keep young children with an adult, and agree on a meeting point before you start.

How a scavenger hunt works

A scavenger hunt has three parts: a list of things to find, clues that point to each one, and a finish line. Players can hunt alone or in teams, and they can collect real objects, take photos of what they find, or solve a clue to reach the next hiding place. The photo version works well when the items are too big to carry or belong to someone else.

Most hunts have between eight and fifteen stops. Fewer than eight feels short, and more than fifteen can drag, especially with young children. Plan for about three to five minutes per stop, so a twelve-clue hunt takes roughly an hour, which is a good length for a party activity.

Decide the finish before you start. A small prize, a treat, or a certificate for every player keeps the ending happy, even for the team that comes last. If you want everyone to feel like a winner, give each team a different coloured ribbon or a silly title, such as fastest finders or best teamwork.

Writing clues with AI

The best clue prompts tell the AI four things: the hiding place, the age of the players, the style of the clue, and the theme. For example: write a rhyming clue for a hiding place under the sofa, for children aged six to eight, in a pirate theme. The more specific you are, the better the clue.

Ask for a mix of clue styles to keep the hunt interesting. Rhyming clues are fun to read aloud, riddles make players think, picture clues suit young children who cannot read yet, and word puzzles suit older players. You can ask for all four styles in one prompt and pick the best of each.

Always test every clue yourself before the hunt. Read it aloud and check that it points to the right place and only that place. If a clue could mean two different spots, ask the AI to make it more specific, or add a detail of your own, such as a colour or a nearby object.

Twelve ready clues

These twelve clues are written fresh for this guide and work in most homes and gardens. Each one points to a common hiding place, and you can change the wording to fit your own rooms. Hide a small item, a treat, or the next clue at each spot.

  1. Under the sofa. I am soft and low and hard to see, the place where cushions hide from me. Look beneath where you sit and rest, and find the clue I keep the best.
  2. Inside the fridge. I keep things cold both day and night, my light comes on and shines so bright. Open my door, but not too slow, a chilly surprise is waiting below.
  3. Behind a curtain. I hang and sway and block the sun, I hide the window just for fun. Peek behind me, soft and wide, a secret waits on the other side.
  4. Under the bed. I am dark and dusty, quiet and low, the place where lost socks like to go. Bend down and look beneath the bed, a hidden treasure lies ahead.
  5. Inside a shoe. I walk with you both far and near, I carry your foot from year to year. Check the shoe beside the door, a little prize is hidden for sure.
  6. Behind the garden gate. I open and close with a creak and a groan, I guard the path to the garden zone. Push me gently, look around, a clue is waiting to be found.
  7. Under a flower pot. I hold the flowers tall and bright, I sit in the garden, morning light. Lift me gently, peek below, a tiny secret waits to show.
  8. Inside a book. I hold the stories, page by page, I wait for readers of every age. Open the book with the red cover, a hidden note waits to discover.
  9. Behind the bathroom mirror. I show your face both morning and night, I shine and sparkle, clean and bright. Look behind me, do not fear, a clue is hiding very near.
  10. Inside the toy box. I hold the toys when play is done, I keep them safe for everyone. Dig through the blocks and bears and cars, a prize is hiding with the stars.
  11. Under the kitchen table. I hold the plates and cups and food, I gather the family, it is all good. Crawl beneath me, look up high, a secret clue is waiting nigh.
  12. Inside the mailbox. I hold the letters, big and small, I wait by the gate for one and all. Open my door with care and cheer, the final treasure is waiting here.

An indoor hunt plan

For an indoor hunt, pick eight to twelve hiding places spread across the rooms you want players to visit. Write one clue per hiding place, and decide whether players collect items or simply find the clue cards. Clue cards are tidier, because nothing needs to be carried back.

Start the hunt at a table with the first clue and a small bag or basket for each team. Give teams a time limit, such as forty minutes, and a rule that everything found must be shown at the finish table. A simple points system works well: one point per find, plus a bonus point for the team that finishes first.

For a rainy day, an indoor hunt can follow a theme, such as pirates, detectives, or space explorers. A theme makes the clues easier to write and the hunt more exciting, because every clue can carry a little story. Ask the AI to write the clues in the voice of a pirate captain or a space commander for extra fun.

An outdoor hunt plan

An outdoor hunt works best in a garden, a park, or a safe courtyard with clear boundaries. Walk the area first and note the hiding places: under a bench, behind a tree, beside a gate, inside a flower pot. Avoid anywhere near a road, water, or steep ground, and keep the area small enough that you can see or hear the players.

Photo hunts suit outdoor spaces well. Instead of collecting objects, players photograph each find: a red flower, a round stone, a bird on a fence, a cloud shaped like an animal. Photo hunts need no hiding at all, and they work for large groups because everyone can search at once.

For a garden party, combine both styles. Hide a few clue cards around the garden, and add a photo list for the spaces in between. Teams race to find the cards and photograph the list, and the first team back with everything wins, or every team wins a small prize for finishing.

Adjusting the hunt for different ages

Young children, aged three to five, need picture clues and short hunts. Draw or print a picture of each hiding place, keep the list to six or eight items, and let an adult walk with each child. Praise every find, and keep the prizes small and immediate, such as a sticker for each item found.

Children aged six to nine can read simple rhyming clues and manage a hunt of ten to twelve stops. Give them a clipboard with the clue list, let them work in pairs, and add one or two easy riddles to stretch their thinking. A map of the hunt area, even a rough hand-drawn one, makes them feel like real explorers.

Older children and adults enjoy harder clues: word puzzles, coded messages, and multi-step riddles. You can ask the AI to write clues that need a little research, such as a clue that points to a book on a shelf, or a clue hidden in a rhyme that only makes sense when read backwards.

A printable checklist structure

A good checklist fits on one page and has four parts: a title with the hunt theme, a numbered list of items or clues with a small box to tick, a space for the team name, and a finish line at the bottom. Keep the font large enough to read while walking, and leave space for notes or drawings.

For a photo hunt, the checklist is the list of things to photograph. For a clue hunt, the checklist can be the list of clues themselves, or a simple score sheet where teams tick each clue they solve. Either way, print one checklist per team, and give each team a pencil and a bag.

You can ask AI to lay out the checklist for you. Describe the items, the theme, and the age group, and ask for a simple one-page list with checkboxes. Then print it, add a few decorations by hand, and your hunt is ready to go.

Hosting tips for a smooth hunt

Start with a short briefing: explain the rules, the boundaries, and the time limit, and show everyone the finish point. Read the first clue aloud together so that everyone understands how the clues work, then let the teams go.

Keep an eye on the time. If a team is stuck on one clue for more than five minutes, offer a gentle hint, such as which room to search or whether the hiding place is high or low. Hints keep the hunt moving and stop frustration from spoiling the fun.

End with a small celebration. Gather everyone at the finish, count the finds, hand out prizes or certificates, and let each team tell the story of their hardest clue. The stories afterwards are often the best part of the whole hunt.

Questions people ask

How many clues should a scavenger hunt have?

Eight to fifteen clues is a good range. Fewer than eight feels short, and more than fifteen can drag, especially with young children. Twelve clues make a hunt of about an hour.

Can AI write clues for my own home?

Yes. Tell the AI your hiding places, the players' ages, and the theme, and it will write rhyming clues or riddles to match. Always test each clue yourself before the hunt.

What is the difference between a clue hunt and a photo hunt?

In a clue hunt, players solve clues to find hidden items or cards. In a photo hunt, players photograph things on a list instead of collecting them, which suits outdoor spaces and large groups.

How do I make a hunt fair for mixed ages?

Mix easy and hard clues, let younger children hunt with an adult, and use picture clues for children who cannot read yet. Teams with mixed ages work well because everyone can contribute.

What should the prize be?

A small treat, a sticker, or a certificate is plenty. The fun is in the hunt itself, so keep prizes simple and give every player something for finishing.
